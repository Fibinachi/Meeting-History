# Meet Sun Tzu — Standards Alignment (verified 2026-10-15)

Encounter: the student presses Master Sun of Wu — a voice bounded by thirteen short chapters of maxims and nothing else — on what the chapters teach (deception, speed, terrain, foreknowledge), the concubine-drilling story from the Shiji (four hundred years later, flagged as tradition), what war costs (answered in figures and maxims, never in grief), and what the chapters never mention. It closes with his scripted question: *thirteen chapters on winning, not one word on what it costs. Judge him.*

**Grade band fit for every framework below: 9–12** (high school ELA literacy / World History skills). Note: as with Socrates and Ashoka, the states' *content* anchors for classical China sit in lower grades (see caveats); the 9–12 weight comes from skills standards — primary-source reading, source criticism, argumentation — which are band-matched. No state in this set names Sun Tzu; that is stated honestly below rather than worked around.

## 1. C3 Framework (National Council for the Social Studies, 2013)

Grade band: **9–12**. (Indicator texts verified against independent curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim; the official NCSS PDF was not directly retrievable in this session — see verification note at end.)

### Dimension 2 — History

- **D2.His.6.9-12** — Analyze the ways in which the perspectives of those writing history shaped the history that they produced. (Two filters: Sima Qian wrote the Sun Wu biography ~400 years after the traditional lifetime — Han-dynasty perspective shaping the man; Lionel Giles's 1910 English is the diction the encounter speaks — a translator's judgment shaping every word the student hears. The debrief names the double filter.)
- **D2.His.8.9-12** — Analyze how current interpretations of the past are limited by the extent to which available historical sources represent perspectives of people at the time. (The honest caveat is the encounter's thesis: thirteen short chapters of maxims, no memoir, no letters, no life — and the historicity debate, which belongs to the debrief, never to the figure.)
- **D2.His.11.9-12** — Critique the usefulness of historical sources for a specific historical inquiry based on their maker, date, place of origin, intended audience, and purpose. (The Shiji drill story: maker — Sima Qian, c. 94 BC; purpose — exemplary anecdote in a dynastic history; the figure treats it as tradition attached to him, flagged as such. Giles's 1910 translation: first annotated English version, public domain — the text the maxims are quoted from.)
- **D2.His.16.9-12** — Integrate evidence from multiple relevant historical sources and interpretations into a reasoned argument about the past. (The closing judgment integrates the chapters' maxims, the Shiji tradition, and the debrief's reception history — Seven Military Classics in 1080, the business-book afterlife he could never know.)

### Dimension 4 — Communicating Conclusions and Taking Informed Action

- **D4.1.9-12** — Construct arguments using precise and knowledgeable claims, with evidence from multiple sources, while acknowledging counterclaims and evidentiary weaknesses. (The judgment must acknowledge the thinness — "a reader with only these chapters would go silent there too" — and what the silence costs.)
- **D4.4.9-12** — Critique the use of claims and evidence in arguments for credibility. (Evaluating the Shiji drill — legend, exemplary anecdote, or history, "no one can say which" — as evidence for the judgment, not as his words.)

## 2. Common Core ELA — Literacy in History/Social Studies, Speaking/Listening, Writing

Grade band: **9–12**. (Verified against the official Common Core State Standards Initiative site, thecorestandards.org.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 9–10 and 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.1 / RH.11-12.1** — 9–10: "Cite specific textual evidence to support analysis of primary and secondary sources, attending to such features as the date and origin of the information." / 11–12: "Cite specific textual evidence to support analysis of primary and secondary sources, connecting insights gained from specific details to an understanding of the text as a whole." (The thirteen chapters as primary source — with date/origin discipline: a received text edited over centuries, Cao Cao's 3rd-century commentary, the traditional 6th-century-BC dating vs. the Warring States-compilation theory.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.2 / RH.11-12.2** — 9–10: "Determine the central ideas or information of a primary or secondary source; provide an accurate summary of how key events or ideas develop over the course of the text." / 11–12: "Determine the central ideas or information of a primary or secondary source; provide an accurate summary that makes clear the relationships among the key details and ideas." (The maxims — deception, speed, terrain, foreknowledge, "supreme excellence consists in breaking the enemy's resistance without fighting" — are the central ideas the student must state; the encounter's Goal is that they feel the method and the silence underneath it.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.4** — Determine the meaning of words and phrases as they are used in a text, including analyzing how an author uses and refines the meaning of a key term over the course of a text (e.g., how Madison defines *faction* in *Federalist* No. 10). (Giles's English as the text: *the Moral Law*, *foreknowledge*, *the host*, *spies* — key terms whose period meanings the student must recover rather than modernize; the encounter forbids him modern words like "strategy" and "casualties" by name.)

### Speaking & Listening, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.1** — Initiate and participate effectively in a range of collaborative discussions (one-on-one, in groups, and teacher-led) with diverse partners on grades 11–12 topics, texts, and issues, building on others' ideas and expressing their own clearly and persuasively. (The encounter is a sustained one-on-one examination — "question him the way an examiner presses a witness.")
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.3** — Evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric, assessing the stance, premises, links among ideas, word choice, points of emphasis, and tone used. (The debrief teaches students to notice the shortness — "the shortest answers in the library because the corpus is the shortest in the library" — and what the maxims never say: the dead, the cost, the grief.)

### Writing, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.W.11-12.1** — Write arguments to support claims in an analysis of substantive topics or texts, using valid reasoning and relevant and sufficient evidence. (The closing judgment — thirteen chapters on winning, not one word on what it costs — is designed as a post-encounter argument prompt.)

## 3. South Carolina — Social Studies College- and Career-Ready Standards (2019)

Grade band: **Grade 6** for the classical-civilizations anchors — the content caveat stated honestly below.

- **6.1.P** (Grade 6) — Analyze the shift from early to classical civilizations and the enduring contributions of classical civilizations. (State guidance places classical China — the Qin and Han material adjacent to the text's world — under this indicator; the *Art of War* is among the enduring contributions. - **6.1.E** (Grade 6) — Analyze multiple perspectives on the political, intellectual, and social achievements of classical societies through a variety of primary and secondary sources. (The encounter is a primary source — the thirteen chapters — read against a secondary one — the Shiji tradition; keeping the two apart is the encounter's central discipline.)

## Verification caveats

1. **C3 Framework**: indicator texts verified against multiple independent curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim (they agree with each other and with the wave-1 verification of 2026-10-15); the official NCSS PDF itself was not directly retrievable this session. Official publisher: NCSS (2013), socialstudies.org/c3.
2. **Grade-band split**: the 9–12 alignments for Sun Tzu are skills standards (RH, SL, W, D2.His/D4). Every state places classical-China content below the 9–12 band (SC Grade 6, FL Grade 6, CA Grade 6) except NY Grades 9–10 (9.3b — the one on-band content exception) and Texas 9–12 (WH.1.B — on-band, but the indicator text was verified via compilation, not the TEA-hosted PDF).
3. **No state names Sun Tzu**: stated plainly, not worked around. FL SS.6.W.4.9 and CA 6.6.x are cited as the nearest genuine fits with the no-name-match caveat attached. Do not present them as Sun Tzu anchors.
4. **Thin-corpus honesty carries into the standards**: RH.11-12.1's date-and-origin clause is load-bearing here — the received text, the Cao Cao editing, the dating debate — and the alignment treats the Shiji material as secondary tradition throughout, never as the figure's voice.
