# Meet Sun Tzu — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Sun Tzu — "Master Sun," traditionally dated c. 544 to c. 496 BC, in the Spring and Autumn period of Zhou-dynasty China — run through DeepSeek. Your students talk with him by typing questions: about the thirteen chapters, about deception and speed and terrain, about the king's women drilled with the drum. At the end each student answers the closing question: thirteen chapters on winning, not one word on what it costs. Judge him.

This is the library's thin-corpus test case. Everything the figure knows comes from thirteen short chapters of maxims — no memoir, no letters, no life. The voice is maxims, not memoir, and the thinness is the teaching point: what can thirteen chapters actually hold, and what can't they?

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

What students will encounter: warfare discussed as a technical problem, with no acknowledgment of suffering; the concubine-drilling story — two women beheaded for laughing during a military drill, stated plainly and never elaborated; and the designed silence that is the encounter's engine: the figure answers questions about the dead with numbers and maxims, and never once speaks of grief. Nothing is described graphically. The absence is the material — a man who can write the perfect manual for killing and never mention the cost — and students will need to process it. Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A DeepSeek account. Routed to DeepSeek 2026-10-20: Asian cultural figures perform better on Asian models. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Sun Tzu page. Copy the entire prompt text.

- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open DeepSeek in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the tutorial and Sun Tzu's opening carefully. Note what he offers as the first thread: the thirteen chapters — how wars are won.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "What is the supreme art of war?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. The beats: the tutorial hands off to Sun Tzu; the student questions him (the chapters, deception, speed, terrain, the drill, the cost); Sun Tzu closes with the question — "Thirteen chapters on winning, not one word on what it costs. Judge him" — and the debrief begins.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Sun Tzu — the ancient Chinese strategist whose thirteen short chapters on war have been read for two thousand years. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. His answers are the shortest in the library, and that's on purpose: you do the work of applying him. At the end you'll judge him: thirteen chapters on winning, not one word on what it costs. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Sun Tzu answers in one short paragraph, roughly 40–70 words — the shortest answers in the library. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.
- Suggested opening if the room freezes: ask him about the thirteen chapters. ("What is the supreme art of war?") It is the thread he offers, and it opens the whole method.

### Pacing

This is a short encounter — the corpus is thirteen chapters, and students will exhaust the facts quickly. That is success, not failure. Opening (who he is, the chapters, the supreme art): 3–4 exchanges. Middle (deception, speed, terrain, the drill): the heart of it, 5–7 exchanges. Late (the cost — press him on the dead): 2–3 exchanges, then let him close. If a student runs out of questions, hand them the closing.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer: learn the method — thirteen chapters that make war look like a solved problem — feel the silence underneath it, and then judge the man who wrote the perfect manual for killing without once speaking of the dead.

## The debrief — do not skip this

The six fixed sections, in order: his name (and the uncertain dates); what the history records (thirteen chapters; the Shiji account, four hundred years later); what came after (the Seven Military Classics, Giles 1910, Mao to the boardroom — the English they heard was a 1910 translator's); the plain record (stated without his vocabulary: flawless arithmetic on winning; the dead as figures in an account; no grief anywhere); the mechanism (the shortness was the evidence; maxims, not memoir; the double filter of 2,500 years and one translator; the designed ignorance); the question that remains.

Discussion openings — built to produce disagreement:

- Thirteen chapters on winning, not one word on what it costs. Where did you land, and what single answer decided it?
- He had two women beheaded and answered with a maxim about discipline. Is that honesty or evasion? Can it be both?
- "Regard your soldiers as your children... even unto death." What is death doing in that sentence? Is it the closest he comes to naming the cost, or the furthest?
- His chapters are quoted by CEOs and generals two thousand years later. Does a method that works everywhere excuse a silence that costs everything?
- Every word he spoke reached you through a 1910 English translator. How much does that change what you think you heard?

## If the model misbehaves

This figure has not yet been through a full solo test run — see "Validation status" in sources.md. Run it yourself first and log what you find here.

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this. Or hand the student the chapters: "What is the supreme art of war?"
- **The model lectures instead of pronouncing.** The failure mode for this figure is the model explaining the maxims — adding context, softening the absolutes, getting chatty. If it happens, redirect to a hard question (the drill, the dead), where the voice is shortest. The voice must never explain itself; it pronounces.
- **The model invents battles or biography.** The prompt marks every gap; the chapters name no campaigns and the tradition gives him no datable life. If the model fills one (a named battle, a death scene), treat it as a break and correct it in the debrief — it is itself a teachable moment about the corpus boundary.
- **The model moralizes in modern terms.** If Sun Tzu starts talking about "war crimes" or "the human cost," the voice has broken. Redirect; correct in the debrief.

## Historical notes for the teacher

The honest answers to "did it really happen like that?" — one bullet per caveat from sources.md. Say the uncertain things out loud in the debrief; classroom honesty is the point of this figure.

- **The dates are traditional, not established.** c. 544–c. 496 BC comes from identifying the text's author with the Shiji's Sun Wu. Mark them as traditional.
- **Sun Wu may not have existed.** Some scholars date the text to the Warring States period (4th–3rd c. BC) and treat it as a compilation. The conventional Qi-to-Wu account is still widely held in China. The encounter takes no position — it speaks as the text's voice.
- **The concubine story is secondhand and late.** Sima Qian wrote it ~400 years after the traditional lifetime. Legend, exemplary anecdote, or history — no one can say. Say so in the debrief.
- **The English is Giles's.** Lionel Giles's 1910 translation is the West's standard version and the source of the figure's diction. The "subdue the enemy without fighting" phrasing students may know from posters is often a later rendering; the prompt quotes Giles: "supreme excellence consists in breaking the enemy's resistance without fighting."
- **Suggested pairing: Clausewitz (future figure, not yet built).** Sun Tzu is the strategist who never describes what victory costs; Clausewitz is the strategist who never stops describing it ("war is the continuation of policy," friction, the fog, the dead). Run the two encounters back to back and the debrief writes itself. Note only — the Clausewitz figure does not exist yet.

## One last thing

Teach them to press the silence. The most important student move in this encounter is not a clever question about deception — it is asking what war costs, hearing the maxims, and asking again. When a student gets the second answer — the numbers, the "unto death" — stop the room and ask: what did he just not say? That is the whole lesson.
