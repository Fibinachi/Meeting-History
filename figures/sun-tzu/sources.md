## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — say which.

- **The setting** (Wu, the court of King Helü, c. 512 BC) — pure dramatic device. The date is traditional, not documented; no source places him at a datable court scene.
- **The figure speaks of the concubine drill as something that happened** — the drill comes from the Shiji, written ~400 years after his traditional lifetime. He answers questions about it because the tradition attaches it to him; the Shiji account is never presented as his words.
- **The figure claims Sun Wu's identity** — the encounter speaks as the text's voice and does not adjudicate the historicity debate (see below). The debate belongs to the debrief and teacher notes, never to the figure.
- **The English voice** — Sun Tzu never spoke English. The figure's diction is Lionel Giles's 1910 translation; the debrief names the double filter (2,500 years + one translator).

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Written before the figure ships, even the ones decided not to matter.

- **The dates c. 544–c. 496 BC are traditional and uncertain.** They come from the traditional identification of the text's author with the Shiji's Sun Wu. Mark them as traditional; do not assert them as established.
- **Sun Wu's historicity is debated.** Some scholars argue the text is a Warring States-period compilation (4th–3rd c. BC) rather than the work of a single 6th-century-BC general; the earliest portions may postdate the traditional dating by a century or more. The conventional view — Sun Wu of Qi, fleeing to Wu, serving King Helü — is still widely held in China. The encounter takes no position; it speaks as the text's voice.
- **The text as we have it was edited over centuries.** The warlord Cao Cao (early 3rd c. AD) authored the earliest known commentary and edited the text, removing passages; the extent of his changes is unclear. What we call "the thirteen chapters" is a received text, not an autograph.
- **The concubine story is secondhand and late.** Sima Qian wrote it ~400 years after the traditional lifetime. It may be legend, exemplary anecdote, or history; no one can say which. The prompt treats it as tradition attached to the figure, flagged as such.
- **King Helü of Wu reigned 514–495 BC (traditional).** The reign dates are the conventional chronology.
- **His death is unrecorded.** The traditional c. 496 BC death date coincides with Helü's death; no account of Sun Wu's death survives. The figure does not speak of it.
- **"The Art of War" as the famous title is largely the West's framing.** Giles's 1910 translation made the text the English-speaking world's standard; the modern business-book afterlife is a 20th-century phenomenon the figure cannot know.
