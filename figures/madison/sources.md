## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — say which.

- **The June-1836 Montpelier conversation itself** — no such encounter is documented. His age (85), ailing health, and work on his papers in 1836 are documented; the conversation is a pure dramatic device.
- **His awareness of nearness to death** — documented that he was failing; he does NOT know he will die on June 28, 1836. That knowledge is withheld by date discipline.
- **The "guiding hand" framing of the Virginia Plan** — matches his own account (Randolph read the resolutions; the plan was the Virginia delegation's). The encounter deliberately has him decline authorship, as he did in life.
- **No broken silences.** Unlike Jefferson's encounter, Madison's has no Hemings-equivalent silence to break by design. His record on his slaves is the record: he freed none, and says so plainly.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Written before the figure ships, even the ones that don't matter.

- **The Notes are not a raw transcript.** Mary Bilder's *Madison's Hand* (2015) showed Madison revised them heavily between 1787 and his death — cross-outs, interpolated sentences, passages smoothed for later political purposes. The classroom should treat them as his record, not a tape recording. The figure believes his record is the record; the debrief says what scholarship found.
- **The Virginia Plan's authorship.** Madison never claimed to be its author; the Founders Online editors state his guiding influence in the Virginia caucus is "beyond dispute." The encounter follows his own line: he will not take the title and will not surrender his share.
- **Federalist authorship disputes.** The consensus (Adair 1944; Mosteller & Wallace 1964) gives Madison 29 numbers: 10, 14, 18–20, 37–58, 62, 63. Nos. 18–20 were a collaboration with Hamilton; Hamilton's posthumous list claimed more. The encounter's three load-bearing numbers (10, 51, 54) are undisputed.
- **"Wrong to admit... property in men."** His recorded remark of 25 August 1787 came in debate on the tax/duty clause, not as a vote against the importation clause. Do not inflate it into opposition he did not act on.
- **The Walsh letter is a 32-years-later account.** His 1819 explanation of the 1808 "middle measure" is his memory of the framers' intent, written during the Missouri crisis — valuable, but not contemporaneous.
- **Slaveholding numbers are estimates.** "About a hundred" at death (Encyclopedia Virginia); the 1820 census counted 106 in his household; tax records vary. The load-bearing fact is the other number: none freed.
- **The Jennings memoir date.** "A Colored Man's Reminiscences of James Madison" is commonly dated 1865; some catalogs give 1863. Either way it is past his horizon and belongs to the debrief.
- **Federalist 10 never mentions slavery.** This is an observation from the text, verifiable by reading it — the debrief's mechanism turns on it.
- **He was the last surviving signer of the Constitution** at his death — documented.
- **Height and voice.** 5'4", about 100 pounds, a thin low voice that back benches strained to hear — commonly cited; used for the voice notes, not the argument.
- **"Notes on Nullification"** is dated December 1834 in the standard listings (Hunt ed.); "Advice to My Country" 1834. Both are past his convention work and carry his last position.
