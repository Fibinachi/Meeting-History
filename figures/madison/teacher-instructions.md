# Meet Madison — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with James Madison (1751–1836), run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student renders the closing judgment: he built the machine. Did it survive him? Judge the architect.

This encounter is the library's systems-thinker at full strength. Madison explains his own theory (Federalist 10, in his own words, at full length), tells the Philadelphia story from his own Notes, defends the slavery compromises in the words he actually used, states his own reasons for the Bill of Rights reversal, narrates the War of 1812 and the bank as the machine teaching its builder, and spends his last strength answering the nullifiers who quoted his own 1798 words back at him. He speaks from complete conviction: quiet, precise, and certain the structure can hold. The student has two hundred and fifty years of evidence.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

- Students will discuss slavery as practiced, protected, and defended by the Constitution's architect — plainly, in his own vocabulary ("slaves," "property," "servitude").
- Students will encounter Federalist 54's logic in his own words: human beings as persons and property at once, defended as "the mixed character" and "the compromising expedient." It is rendered as he wrote it, not cleaned up and not editorialized in his voice.
- Students will discuss his own slaveholding: about a hundred people held at Montpelier, none freed — stated plainly by him, faced without euphemism.
- The debrief states that in 1844 Montpelier was sold and the sheriff seized and sold the enslaved community Madison had asked Dolley not to sell; that Paul Jennings was sold by Dolley in 1846 and later freed; and that the slavery compromises were undone by the Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendments.
- The figure speaks in June 1836, weeks before his death; he knows he is failing and says so. No graphic content.

Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## The slavery-compromises honesty requirement

Read this carefully — it is the load-bearing design decision of the encounter.

Your students will press him on the three-fifths clause and the slave-trade clause, and he will defend them — in the words he actually used. Federalist 54 is quoted from the 1788 text: persons and property at once, "the compromising expedient." His August 25, 1787 remark from the Notes ("wrong to admit in the Constitution the idea that there could be property in men") is given exactly where it belongs — in debate on the tax clause, not as opposition he acted on. The prompt forbids inventing any opposition beyond what the record holds.

He will also say, plainly, that he freed none of the people he held. Do not let him be softened: if the model starts supplying modern excuses for him, that is a voice failure — stop and correct it. The debrief does the plain stating; his voice does the defending. That split is the pedagogy.

If a student never asks about slavery, the suggestion pools will get them there — check that they did before you close.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Madison page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: 25–30 minutes for the encounter, 20–25 minutes for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the opening carefully: he introduces himself at Montpelier in June 1836, explains the format in character ("you ask, I answer"), and hands the student the thread — Philadelphia.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "What is Federalist 10 about?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. Then try a hard one: "What did you mean when you said it was wrong to admit property in men?" Then the hardest: "You freed none of them. Why?" Notice the voice — the quiet never lifting, the answers returning to the structure — and the bracketed nudges that point at what he doesn't say. That held quiet is the pedagogy.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with James Madison — the man who designed the Constitution, and a man who owned about a hundred people while he did it. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. He is completely certain his machine works. Your job is to find the parts of the machine that carry the most weight. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### Suggested pre-reading

Federalist 10 — and only Federalist 10. It is short enough to assign whole, it is the encounter's theoretical core, and the debrief turns on what it contains and what it doesn't. The prompt's canonical answer quotes it at full strength, so students who read it first will catch him in his own words. His Notes of Debates are the classroom's Philadelphia: the full text is public (Internet Archive; Yale's Avalon Project carries the day-by-day debates), and Montpelier's own ConText edition is the most readable way in. Do not pre-read Federalist 54 for them — let the encounter deliver it.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Madison answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- When the conversation has run its course — ideally after the student has pressed him on the machine, the compromises, and what he kept — he asks the closing question himself and stops: "I built the machine. Did it survive me? Judge the architect."
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Pacing

- Opening and Philadelphia: 4–6 exchanges. The Notes are the on-ramp — don't let students camp on the drafting history.
- Federalist 10, the compromises, his own slaves: 6–8 exchanges. This is the core. The Fed 54 logic, the August 25 remark, and "I have freed none of them" all live here.
- The Bill of Rights, the war, the bank, nullification: 4–6 exchanges. These deepen the judgment — especially the bank, where the machine teaches its builder, and the nullification letters, where his own words are quoted back at him.
- The closing: he asks it when the conversation has run its course. If a student types something like "I'm done" or "I want to judge you now," he closes. He never delivers the verdict himself.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — the answer: "Understand what he built, what he conceded to build it, and what it cost the people the machine was asked to carry — then judge him: he built the machine. Did it survive him?"

## The debrief — do not skip this

Six sections, in this order: names, what the history records, what came after, the plain record, the mechanism, the question that remains. The full debrief text is in the prompt — you read it out of character and run it as discussion, not a read-aloud. The revision finding on the Notes lives in section 3; the plain record of the compromises and his slaveholding lives in section 4; Federalist 10's silence lives in section 5. Make sure all three land.

Discussion openings (built to produce disagreement):

- Federalist 10 says factions can't be removed, only controlled — and never mentions slavery. Was that a blind spot, or a design choice? What's the difference?
- He said it was wrong to admit in the Constitution the idea of property in men — and defended three-fifths in print. What does "wrong to admit" mean if you admit it anyway?
- He freed none of the hundred people he held, and left $2,000 to the Colonization Society. What does a bequest to a society do that a manumission wouldn't?
- The nullifiers quoted his own 1798 words back at him, and he spent his last years writing against them. Who owned those words — him or them?
- He lost the federal negative, signed the bank he'd called unconstitutional, and watched the machine become something he hadn't designed. Did it survive him — or survive by becoming something else?

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **The voice lectures.** Madison's answers should stay near 60–100 words. If the model starts monologuing on republican theory, remind it of the global rules or restart — the canonical answers are the baseline.
- **The voice claims modern knowledge.** If he discusses anything after June 1836 — his death date, the 1840 publication, Jennings's freedom, the Civil War, the amendments — that's a date-discipline failure. The prompt forbids it; in a supervised run, treat the slip as a teaching moment: "What just happened? He couldn't have known that."
- **The voice invents opposition on slavery.** If he claims to have fought the slave-trade clause or opposed three-fifths at Philadelphia beyond what the Notes record, that's a corpus failure — stop and correct it. The canonical answers are the boundary: the August 25 remark where it belongs, Federalist 54 as written, the Walsh letter as written.
- **The voice softens him.** If he offers anything resembling a modern apology, or claims to have freed people he did not free, that's a voice failure. The prompt forbids both: "I have freed none of them. Not one."
- **The voice claims vindication.** If he says the machine survived, or that history proved him right, that's a date-discipline failure — he cannot know. He believes it will hold; he does not know it did.

## Historical notes for the teacher

- His Notes of Debates were revised heavily in later life (cross-outs, interpolations, political smoothing — Mary Bilder, *Madison's Hand*, 2015). They are his record, not a transcript.
- He never claimed authorship of the Virginia Plan; the Founders Online editors state his guiding influence in the Virginia caucus is "beyond dispute." The encounter follows his own line.
- The consensus Federalist attribution (Adair 1944; Mosteller & Wallace 1964): Madison wrote 29 numbers — 10, 14, 18–20 (18–20 in collaboration with Hamilton), 37–58, 62, 63. The encounter's load-bearing numbers (10, 51, 54) are undisputed.
- The "wrong to admit... property in men" remark (25 August 1787) came in debate on the tax clause — do not let students inflate it into opposition he never acted on.
- His will freed none of his roughly 100 enslaved workers; it left them to Dolley with a request she not sell them without their consent. In 1844 Montpelier was sold and the sheriff seized and sold the enslaved community anyway.
- Paul Jennings was sold by Dolley in 1846, freed by Senator Daniel Webster, and published his memoir in 1865 — all past Madison's horizon.
- Madison became president of the American Colonization Society in 1833 and left it $2,000 plus his grist-mill proceeds.
- His first draft of the Virginia Resolutions contained the words "null and void"; the legislature struck them.
- Federalist 10 names property, debt, and land as the sources of faction. It never mentions slavery.

## One last thing

Don't let his quiet do the excusing. His certainty is the load-bearing structure of the encounter — when students say "he makes sense," ask them what the sense was asked to carry. And teach them to ask the second question. The first question gets his architecture — "the machine can hold." The second — "and what did you ask it to hold?" — is where the judgment lives.
