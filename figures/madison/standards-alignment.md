# Meet Madison — Standards Alignment (verified 2026-10-15)

Encounter: students press a simulated James Madison (1751–1836) at Montpelier in early June 1836 — the architect of the Constitution, who took the only complete daily record of the Convention, defended the result as Publius (Federalist 10, 51, 54, 39 and twenty-six other numbers), and owned about a hundred people while he did it. They question him on the machine (factions, ambition counteracting ambition), on the compromises he built in and defended in print (three-fifths, the slave-trade clause), on the people he held and freed none of, on the Bill of Rights he first doubted, on the bank his own construction revised, and on the nullifiers who quoted his Virginia Resolutions back at him. It closes with his question: *he built the machine — did it survive him? Judge the architect.*

**Grade band fit for every framework below: 9–12** (high school U.S. History / Government / ELA literacy). Note: most states' *content* anchors for the founding era sit in grades 7–8 (see caveats); the 9–12 weight comes from South Carolina's USHC placement, Texas's high-school Government course, and skills standards — argument, source critique, deliberation — which are band-matched.

## 1. C3 Framework (National Council for the Social Studies, 2013)

Grade band: **9–12**. (Indicator texts verified against curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim; the official NCSS PDF was not directly retrievable in this session — see verification note at end.)

### Dimension 2 — Civics

- **D2.Civ.4.9-12** — Explain how the U.S. Constitution establishes a system of government that has powers, responsibilities, and limits that have changed over time and that are still contested. (The encounter's civic core: the federal negative he lost at Philadelphia, the bank his precedent revised, the nullifiers he spent his last years answering — the Constitution's contested limits argued by its architect.)

### Dimension 2 — History

- **D2.His.16.9-12** — Integrate evidence from multiple relevant historical sources and interpretations into a reasoned argument about the past. (The Convention Notes as multiple-perspective sources: the daily record of what every delegate said, revised by the old man over decades — the corpus spine of the encounter, and the evidence the student must synthesize to judge the machine.)
- **D2.His.8.9-12** — Analyze how current interpretations of the past are limited by the extent to which available historical sources represent perspectives of people at the time. (The Notes were not a raw transcript — Madison revised them heavily in later life; Mary Bilder's *Madison's Hand* (2015) showed how thoroughly the old man edited the young man's record. The debrief carries this; the encounter does not let him doubt it.)
- **D2.His.11.9-12** — Critique the usefulness of historical sources for a specific historical inquiry based on their maker, date, place of origin, intended audience, and purpose. (The Notes as a self-interested record; Federalist 54 as advocacy written to win ratification; the 1819 Walsh letter as retrospective self-explanation of the 1808 "middle measure.")

### Dimension 4 — Communicating Conclusions and Taking Informed Action

- **D4.1.9-12** — Construct arguments using precise and knowledgeable claims, with evidence from multiple sources, while acknowledging counterclaims and evidentiary weaknesses. (The closing judgment: did the machine survive him — with the three-fifths clause, the Notes' revisions, and the un-freed hundred as the counterclaims.)
- **D4.4.9-12** — Critique the use of claims and evidence in arguments for credibility. (Federalist 54's claim that the Constitution "decides with great propriety" — the student tests the builder's own defense against the debrief's plain record.)

*Verified against: uk.everfi.com C3 curriculum guide; go.boarddocs.com AP Government curriculum document; cdn.fairfieldschools.org district C3 documents. Official document: College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework for Social Studies State Standards, NCSS, 2013 (socialstudies.org/c3).*

## 2. Common Core ELA — Literacy in History/Social Studies & Speaking/Listening

Grade band: **9–12**. (Verified against the official Common Core State Standards Initiative site, thecorestandards.org.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 9–10

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.6** — Compare the point of view of two or more authors for how they treat the same or similar topics, including which details they include and emphasize in their respective accounts. (Federalist 10's theory of faction against the Anti-Federalists' consolidation fears — the same Constitution, two irreconcilable readings.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.2** — Determine the central ideas or information of a primary or secondary source; provide an accurate summary of how key events or ideas develop over the course of the text. (Federalist 10 and 51 as the canonical close-reading texts — "extend the sphere," "ambition must be made to counteract ambition.")

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.6** — Evaluate authors' differing points of view on the same historical event or issue by assessing the authors' claims, reasoning, and evidence. (The ratification debate: Publius's defense against the Anti-Federalist attack — and Madison's own 1787 self against his 1830 self on the Virginia Resolutions and nullification.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.8** — Evaluate an author's premises, claims, and evidence by corroborating or challenging them with other information. (Federalist 54's "persons and property" defense against the debrief's plain record: the clause gave slave states extra representation for people they held as property; he said it was wrong to admit property in men, and admitted it anyway.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.1** — Cite specific textual evidence to support analysis of primary and secondary sources, connecting insights gained from specific details to an understanding of the text as a whole. (The Notes' daily entries — the August 25 "wrong to admit… the idea that there could be property in men" — against the Constitution that did admit it.)

### Reading: Informational Text, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RI.11-12.9** — Analyze 17th-, 18th-, and 19th-century foundational U.S. documents of historical and literary significance (including The Declaration of Independence, the Preamble to the Constitution, the Bill of Rights, and Lincoln's Second Inaugural Address) for their themes, purposes, and rhetorical features. (The Federalist Papers are the canonical 18th-century foundational documents in this slot — his numbers 10, 51, 54, 39 are the encounter's texts.)

### Speaking & Listening, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.1** — Initiate and participate effectively in a range of collaborative discussions (one-on-one, in groups, and teacher-led) with diverse partners on grades 11–12 topics, texts, and issues, building on others' ideas and expressing their own clearly and persuasively. (The encounter *is* a sustained one-on-one examination of a witness.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.3** — Evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric, assessing the stance, premises, links among ideas, word choice, points of emphasis, and tone used. (The encounter's whole method: the architectural sentences, the stillness of the builder, the contradictions nested so precisely they don't touch the walls — Federalist 10 names property, debt, and land, and never once names slavery.)

### Writing, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.W.11-12.1** — Write arguments to support claims in an analysis of substantive topics or texts, using valid reasoning and relevant and sufficient evidence. (The closing question — did the machine survive him? — is designed as a post-encounter argument prompt, per the teacher instructions.)

## 3. South Carolina — Social Studies College- and Career-Ready Standards (2019), US History and the Constitution

Grade band: **9–12** (high school US History). South Carolina places the founding era at 9–12, which makes it the cleanest on-band state fit.

- **Standard 1** — Demonstrate an understanding of the influence of the Atlantic World on the regional and national development of republicanism and federalism from 1607–1815.
- **USHC.1.CE** — Assess the major developments of the American Revolution through significant turning points in the debates over independence and self-government from 1763 to 1791. (State guidance explicitly names the Federalist Papers among the early founding documents in scope — Madison's Publius numbers are the encounter's central texts; the three-fifths and slave-trade compromises are the Convention's resolutions on slavery.)
- **USHC.1.P** — Summarize the changing relationship between individuals and the government during the period 1607 to 1800. (State guidance names the Ratification Debates, the "Necessary and Proper" Clause, the Bill of Rights, and the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions among its content — Madison is on both sides of this indicator: the architect who lost the federal negative, the Bill of Rights' author, the Resolutions' drafter, the nullifiers' unwilling authority.)
- **USHC.1.CC** — Analyze the processes of continuity and change in the debates over the proper role of the central government and neutrality in foreign affairs from 1789 to 1815. (The bank fight of 1791 and the 1815 veto — "precedent had revised my construction" — as the continuity-and-change case.)
- **USHC.1.E** — Utilize primary and secondary sources to investigate the impact of Atlantic influence on the development of republicanism and federalism. (The Notes, the Federalist numbers, the "Vices" memo, and the Walsh letter as the source set.)

*Verified against: the official SC Department of Education US History and the Constitution Alignment Guide (August 2024), an official interpretation of the 2019 standards (ed.sc.gov).*

## 4. Other states (quick hits)

### New York (K–12 Social Studies Framework, Grade 7)

- **7.4.C.II** — Students will examine how key issues were resolved during the Constitutional Convention, including: state representation in Congress (Great Compromise), the balance of power between the federal and state governments (federalism), the prevention of parts of government becoming too powerful (three branches), and the counting of the enslaved African American community for purposes of congressional representation and taxation (the Three-Fifths Compromise). (The Convention's compromises — including the one he defended in Federalist 54 — named explicitly.)
- **7.4.B.I** — Students will investigate the successes and failures of the Articles of Confederation, determine why many felt a new plan of government was needed, and explain how the United States Constitution attempted to address the weaknesses of the Articles. (His "Vices of the Political System" memo is the scholarly answer to this indicator.)
- **7.5.D.I** — Students will examine events of the early nation including Hamilton's economic plan, the Louisiana Purchase, the Supreme Court decision in Marbury v. Madison, and the War of 1812 in terms of testing the strength of the Constitution. (Marbury bears his name; the War of 1812 was his presidency — the machine under strain.)

### California (History–Social Science Content Standards, Grade 8)

- **8.2.3** — Evaluate the major debates of the Constitutional Convention, including their resolutions on slavery and the rights of individuals. (The three-fifths compromise, the slave-trade clause, and his August 25 objection — the Convention debates named explicitly.)

## Verification caveats

1. **C3 Framework**: indicator texts were verified against three independent curriculum documents that quote the Framework verbatim (they agree with each other); the official NCSS PDF itself was not directly retrievable this session. Treat the official publisher as NCSS (2013), socialstudies.org/c3.
2. **Grade-band split.** Only South Carolina (USHC) and Texas's high-school Government course (§113.44) place founding-era content at 9–12. New York, Florida, and California teach the founding era in grades 7–8; Texas's high-school U.S. History course (§113.41) begins at 1877. The encounter targets 9–12, so those anchors are real but one band below target.
3. **Texas §113.41**: the task brief suggested §113.41 might hold founding-era anchors; it does not — it is "United States History Studies Since 1877." The on-band Texas anchor is §113.44 (US Government), verified above.
4. **No standard asks the encounter's question.** Every code above covers the Convention, the Federalist Papers, or source evidence — none asks students to judge whether the machine survived its architect. The judgment is the library's design, not a standards artifact; the standards supply the evidence base.
5. All verifications in this document were performed on 2026-10-15.
