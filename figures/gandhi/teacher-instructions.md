# Meet Gandhi — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Mohandas K. Gandhi (1869–1948), run through DeepSeek. Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student renders the closing judgment, stated plainly: **he freed a nation without firing a shot — and his early pages carry the racism of his time. Judge him whole.**

Gandhi speaks from Birla House, Delhi, in January 1948, his fast for communal peace just ended, looking back over his life. He is the non-violence seat on the library's shelf — and this encounter refuses to let him be only that. Its judgment engine is satyagraha's power and its limits, plus the early South African writings that complicate the saint.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

- Gandhi's **early South African writings carried the racism of the colony**: he argued Indians should not be classed with Black Africans, used the period's slur for them, and described them in degrading terms. The encounter paraphrases around the slur — it is never reproduced — but the substance is stated plainly and not explained away. **This material is standard curriculum and carries an OPEN tier: the controversy does not require the guided tier, but the teacher must not skip it.** Plan to run the debrief.
- **Communal violence and partition** are discussed: riots, massacres, the blood of 1946–48. Facts stated plainly, never described in graphic detail.
- **Fasting/starvation** is central to the late voice (Calcutta, September 1947; Delhi, January 1948). Students with eating-disorder histories may need a heads-up.
- The **assassination** is held entirely in the debrief — the figure knows nothing of it. The debrief names the killer, the date, and the hanging.

Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A DeepSeek account. Routed to DeepSeek 2026-10-20: Asian cultural figures perform better on Asian models. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Gandhi page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: ~25–30 for the encounter, ~20–25 for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open DeepSeek in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read Gandhi's opening carefully. Note the threads he offers: satyagraha (and what it is not), the salt, the fasts, the early years in South Africa.
4. Now type a question yourself — e.g., "What did you write about the Africans in South Africa?" Watch what happens: Gandhi answers in one short paragraph — plainly, without softening — and then, outside the voice, in brackets and italics, the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. The encounter's beats: (a) the tutorial hands off to Gandhi; (b) the student questions him through early (the experiments, South Africa, satyagraha), middle (Dandi, the fasts, the early writings), and late (what satyagraha could not stop, the judgment) phases, following the bracketed suggestions; (c) Gandhi closes with the scripted closing question; (d) the debrief runs in the teacher's modern voice.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Mohandas Gandhi — the man who led India's freedom struggle without firing a shot, speaking from Delhi in January 1948, just after his last fast. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. He will tell you what satyagraha is, and he will not hide his early writings from you. At the end you'll be asked to judge him whole. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Gandhi answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- He will not volunteer the judgment; the student must press him toward it. That is the assignment working as designed.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Pacing

Opening: 2–3 exchanges. Don't let students camp on biography — Porbandar and the law degree are background, not the encounter. Early (the experiments, satyagraha, Hind Swaraj): 6–8 exchanges. Middle (Dandi, the fasts, the early writings — this is where the encounter lives): 8–12 exchanges. Late (the limits, the judgment): 4–6 exchanges, then the closing. If a student stalls, Gandhi hands them a question himself — point at that, not at the suggestions.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer: find out what satyagraha was, what it cost, what it could and could not do — and then judge the man whole: the nation freed without firing a shot, and the early pages that carry the racism of his time.

## The debrief — do not skip this

The six fixed sections, in order: (1) his name; (2) what the history records; (3) what came after — the assassination and the trial, the RSS ban, King and Mandela, the Ghana statue controversy; (4) the plain record — what he did and what he wrote, without euphemism and without his vocabulary; (5) the mechanism — the experimenter voice, and the designed refusal to either deny or apologize; (6) the question that remains.

Run it as a discussion, not a read-aloud. Discussion openings, built to produce disagreement:

- He never took the early words back, though his later life opposed discrimination of all kinds. Does the later life redeem the early pages — or do the early pages indict the saint?
- Satyagraha made the Empire unworkable but could not stop partition's blood. Is that a limit of the method, or of the moment?
- He fasted against his own people in Calcutta and Delhi — against rioters, not rulers. Is that leadership or coercion? Would you call it nonviolent?
- Guha says he "outgrew his racism quite decisively"; Mandela said to judge him in the context of his time. Is either of them letting him off — or are they doing the judging the encounter asks for?

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this. Gandhi also has scripted stall lines and will hand the student a question.
- **The early writings get softened.** Some models will sand the early-writings answer into "he reflected the views of his time" or invent a retraction or apology. That is a voice failure: the prompt forbids it explicitly. Re-paste the early-writings canonical answer and the MODEL INSTRUCTION and continue.
- **The voice drifts modern.** If Gandhi starts lecturing about racism as a modern concept, uses words like "systemic," "ally," or "trauma," or claims vindication by history (Mandela, King, statues), the model has broken voice. Re-paste the Voice notes and Corpus vocabulary sections and continue.
- **Saint-making.** If the model turns him into a serene sage dispensing wisdom, it has broken voice: he is an obstinate experimenter, not a guru. Redirect with the guardrail line.
- **Invented foreboding.** He must never hint at January 30. His documented words on the attempts ("I aim to live to a hundred and twenty-five") are the whole of it.

## Historical notes for the teacher

- He never publicly retracted the early words. The record shows a decisive shift in substance (the Zulu episode reframed in the *Autobiography*; the 1942 Roosevelt letter naming Africa's exploitation), not a retraction. Guha and Desai & Vahed disagree on how to read the arc; the encounter presents the record and hands the judgment to the student.
- The 1908 "commingle" line is cited via secondary sources — treat it as secondary-sourced, not a verified primary quotation.
- "My life is my message" is documented: written in Bengali (*Amar Jeevan-i Amar Vani*) as his farewell message at Calcutta, September 1947.
- Keep the famous misattributions out: "Be the change," "an eye for an eye," and "first they ignore you" have no source in the corpus.
- The attempts he knew: 1934 Poona bomb, 1944 Panchgani (Godse with a dagger, per secondary accounts), 1946 train sabotage. He knew nothing of January 20 or January 30, 1948.
- Godse was hanged at Ambala on November 15, 1949; Gandhi's sons Manilal and Ramdas had pleaded for commutation. The RSS was briefly banned; the ban was lifted in 1949.
- The Gandhi Heritage Portal (gandhiheritageportal.org) hosts the full *Collected Works* (100 volumes) and the complete runs of his journals — the strongest single link for students who want to read further.

## One last thing

Teach them to ask the second question about the early writings. The first answer states the words; the second asks what became of the man who wrote them. The encounter is built so that the first answer is never the whole story — the student who stops at "he admitted it" has not yet done the judging.
