# Meet James Wilson — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with James Wilson (1742–1798), run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student renders the closing judgment: **He grounded the republic in the people and died running from his debts. Judge him.**

Wilson is the library's encounter with the founding's most democratic mind in its least disciplined life. He lectures even in conversation — the professor among the founders — and he answers questions about his debts with accounts and schemes, never with an accounting of conscience. That silence is the design.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

Students will meet a dying man: the encounter discusses debt, debtors' imprisonment, and death. Wilson discusses his slaveholding plainly and without euphemism ("slaves," in his own vocabulary). Nothing is graphic and nothing is violent. Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the James Wilson page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: 25 minutes for the encounter, 20 minutes for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the opening carefully — he names both threads himself: the doctrine and the ruin. Note which one he gives you first.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "What happened to your money?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. Work through the beats: the doctrine (sovereignty), the Convention, the Lectures, Chisholm v. Georgia, then the debts. End by telling him you are ready for the closing. Note the two bracketed "did you notice" nudges and where they fire.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with James Wilson — a man who signed both the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution, and who died hiding from his creditors. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. At the end, you'll be asked to judge him. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, they answer." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Wilson answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief. (He is a lecturer by design; the brevity is the leash.)
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- To end, the student tells him they are ready for the closing. He asks the closing question and stops.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Pacing

- Opening: 2–3 exchanges. He states who he is and hands them the two threads. Don't let students camp here.
- The doctrine and the Convention: 4–6 exchanges. This is where the voice is strongest — let them hear it.
- Chisholm v. Georgia: 2–3 exchanges. The sovereignty theory applied.
- The ruin: 4–6 exchanges. This is the encounter's center of gravity. Students who only ask about the doctrine will leave with half a Wilson.
- The closing: 1 exchange. He asks; they answer.

## The goal, stated plainly

The student holds the collision whole: the most democratic theory of sovereignty any founder wrote, in the mouth of a man who could not live inside his own doctrine of obligation. The only answer the encounter accepts is theirs.

## The debrief — do not skip this

The six fixed sections, in order, are in the prompt: names, what the history records, what came after, the plain record, the mechanism, the question that remains. Run it as a discussion, not a read-aloud.

Discussion openings — built to produce disagreement:

- Wilson taught that law binds because people consent to it. He fled his creditors. Does the second discredit the first — or is that a category mistake?
- He ruled that Georgia was no sovereign, because sovereignty belongs to the people. But the sheriff of Philadelphia could jail him. Is that a contradiction in his doctrine, or two different things?
- He was one of six men who signed both founding documents. Does that fact cut for or against your judgment?
- He kept a household slave while writing that all men are by nature equal and free. Does that belong in the same judgment as the debts, or a different one?
- What would it have taken for him to answer the money question the way he answered the sovereignty question?

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **Wilson warms up or gets contrite about the ruin.** This is the known failure mode — the model wants the professor to be sorry. He is not; he is practical about the money and silent about the meaning. If he starts performing contrition or self-pity, restart the exchange: re-ask the question.
- **Wilson lectures past the word limit.** Reinforce the brevity in a plain instruction outside the voice ("Answer in one short paragraph"), then continue.
- Not yet tested on a live model. Note any quirks you find here after your solo run.

## Historical notes for the teacher

The honest answers to "did it really happen like that?"

- **Both documents:** Exactly six men signed the Declaration and the Constitution: George Clymer, Benjamin Franklin, Robert Morris, George Read, Roger Sherman, and James Wilson. The debrief uses this — Wilson is one of six.
- **The Lectures:** Delivered 1790–91 at the College of Philadelphia, published 1804 by his son Bird. Public domain; selections are classroom-usable — the sovereignty passages and the "law of nature" opening are the entry points. Students who read him first will hear the voice's register when it arrives.
- **The ruin:** Say it plainly. He speculated in western lands on credit; the Panic of 1796–97 broke him; he was jailed for debt in Philadelphia and in Burlington, New Jersey (1797); he fled south; he died in Edenton, North Carolina, on August 21, 1798, at fifty-five, while still a justice of the Supreme Court. Reports of a third detention in North Carolina are thin — treat as uncertain.
- **The Convention speeches** come to us through Madison's Notes — reported, not transcribed. Substance is faithful; wording is not.
- **The slavery detail** (household slave, freed 1794; quiet at the Convention; the three-fifths proposal) comes from modern secondary histories. Wilson left no systematic treatment of slavery in his own writings.
- **He died a disgraced man and was recovered later.** His remains were returned to Philadelphia in 1906 (Christ Church); twentieth-century scholars recovered him as the founder's theorist of popular sovereignty. He knew none of it — that ignorance is the design's payoff in section 3 of the debrief.

## One last thing

Teach them to press him when he turns a question about his debts into a lecture about his principles. The second question — asked after the deflection — is the encounter. If a student accepts the first answer and moves on, they will leave with the doctrine and miss the man. The bracketed nudge *[Did you notice where he went when the money came up?]* exists for exactly that moment. Point at it.
