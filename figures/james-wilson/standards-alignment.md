# Meet James Wilson — Standards Alignment (verified 2026-10-15)

Encounter: the student presses James Wilson (1742–1798), the Scottish-born Philadelphia lawyer who signed both the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution, taught popular sovereignty in the first systematic lectures on American law, and ruled in *Chisholm v. Georgia* (1793) that a state is no sovereign — then speculated in western lands, was jailed for debt, fled south to Edenton, North Carolina, and died a sitting Supreme Court justice hiding from his creditors. It closes with the judgment: *he grounded the republic in the people and died running from his debts. Judge him.*

**Grade band fit for every framework below: 9–12** (high school Government / U.S. History / ELA literacy). Founding-era content sits in grades 7–8 in most states (see caveats); South Carolina places it at 9–12.

---

## 1. C3 Framework (National Council for the Social Studies, 2013)

Grade band: **9–12**. (Indicator texts verified against multiple independent curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim; the official NCSS PDF was not directly retrievable in this session — see verification note at end.)

### Dimension 2 — Civics

- **D2.Civ.4.9-12** — Explain how the U.S. Constitution establishes a system of government that has powers, responsibilities, and limits that have changed over time and that are still contested. (The Lectures taught that all power is originally in the people — "the original sovereign" — and that government is the people's contrivance; at the Convention he pressed for direct election of representatives and the chief magistrate, and sat on the Committee of Detail that gave the Constitution its first shape.)
- **D2.Civ.1.9-12** — Distinguish the powers and responsibilities of local, state, tribal, national, and international civic and political institutions. (*Chisholm*: Georgia pleaded she was a sovereign and could not be sued; Wilson answered that to the Constitution "the term sovereign is totally unknown" — that "there are citizens, but no subjects," and Georgia was no sovereign. The people are.)
- **D2.Civ.3.9-12** — Analyze the impact of constitutions, laws, treaties, and international agreements on the maintenance of national and international order. (The Eleventh Amendment, ratified in 1795 within his lifetime, overturned *Chisholm* — the amendment process answering a Supreme Court opinion; he discusses the reversal in voice as fact while insisting the doctrine stands.)

### Dimension 2 — History

- **D2.His.16.9-12** — Integrate evidence from multiple relevant historical sources and interpretations into a reasoned argument about the past. (The Lectures, the *Chisholm* seriatim, his Convention speeches as reported in Madison's Notes, his 1797–98 letters to his son, and the debt-and-jailing record — the sources the closing judgment must weigh against each other.)

### Dimension 4 — Communicating Conclusions and Taking Informed Action

- **D4.1.9-12** — Construct arguments using precise and knowledgeable claims, with evidence from multiple sources, while acknowledging counterclaims and evidentiary weaknesses. (The closing judgment — whether the theory stands when the man falls — is this standard as a student task.)
- **D4.4.9-12** — Critique the use of claims and evidence in arguments for credibility. (The load-bearing deflection: he answers money questions with accounts and schemes, never conscience; students learn to weigh the credibility of his self-account against the plain record of the flights to Philadelphia, Burlington, and Edenton.)

Official document: College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework for Social Studies State Standards, NCSS, 2013 (socialstudies.org/c3); official NCSS PDF not directly retrieved this session.*

## 2. Common Core ELA

### Reading: Informational Text / History-Social Studies, 11–12 and 9–10

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RI.11-12.9** — Analyze 17th-, 18th-, and 19th-century foundational U.S. documents of historical and literary significance for themes, purposes, and rhetorical features. (His *Chisholm* seriatim as public legal text; the Lectures as the first systematic American law; the Constitution whose first shape came from his Committee of Detail. His rhetorical features are the encounter's signature: rhetorical questions he answers himself, enumerations — first, secondly, thirdly — the "I conceive" certainty.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.8** — Evaluate an author's premises, claims, and evidence by corroborating or challenging them with other information. (His premise that "the term sovereign is totally unknown" tested against the Eleventh Amendment's reversal; his claim that he intended to pay tested against the jailing record and the flight south.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.6** — Compare the point of view of two or more authors for how they treat the same or similar topics, including which details they include and emphasize in their respective accounts. (Wilson's "Georgia is no sovereign" against the state-sovereignty position the Eleventh Amendment adopted — two accounts of the same question: who is sovereign?)

### Speaking & Listening, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.3** — Evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric, assessing the stance, premises, links among ideas, word choice, points of emphasis, and tone used. (The encounter's method: the professorial register that lectures even in conversation, that answers its own questions — students must evaluate whether the certainty-at-rest rhetoric earns its confidence.)

### Writing, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.W.11-12.1** — Write arguments to support claims in an analysis of substantive topics or texts, using valid reasoning and relevant and sufficient evidence. (The closing judgment is designed as a post-encounter argument prompt, per the teacher instructions.)

## 3. South Carolina — Social Studies College- and Career-Ready Standards (2019)

Course: **United States History and the Constitution** (9–12) and **United States Government** (9–12).

- **USHC.1.CE** — Assess the major developments of the American Revolution through significant turning points in the debates over independence and self-government from 1763–1791. (The official development notes explicitly name the Constitution among the founding documents in scope — his Convention argument for direct election and popular sovereignty, and his seat on the Committee of Detail.)
- **USHC.1.P** — Summarize the changing relationship between individuals and the government during the period 1607–1800. (The Lectures' theory of the individual citizen under a Constitution that has "citizens, but no subjects" — *Chisholm*, 1793, inside the band.)
- **USHC.1.CC** — Analyze the processes of continuity and change in the debates over the proper role of the central government and neutrality in foreign affairs from 1789–1815. (Federal courts versus the states: *Chisholm* (1793) and the Eleventh Amendment (1795) as the debate over central power in action.)
- **USG.1.ER** — Analyze the philosophical influences on core political principles in the American constitutional democracy. (The law of nature, popular sovereignty as the philosophical ground of the republic.)
- **USG.1.IN** — Interpret founding documents and principles that led to the creation of the American constitutional democracy. (The Lectures as founding legal doctrine; the *Chisholm* opinion as founding judicial interpretation.)

## Verification caveats

1. **C3 Framework**: indicator texts were verified against multiple independent curriculum documents that quote the Framework verbatim (they agree with each other); the official NCSS PDF itself was not directly retrievable this session. Treat the official publisher as NCSS (2013), socialstudies.org/c3.
2. **Grade-band split.** Only South Carolina places the founding era in 9–12 courses (USHC / US Government). New York, Texas, Florida, and California all teach the Revolution and founding documents in grades 7–8. The honest high-school anchors outside SC are: Texas §113.41(b)(1) (Celebrate Freedom Week) and Florida's SS.912.A.1 inquiry benchmarks (the method, not the content).
3. **USG.1.IN** was verified against a secondary compilation (SC State House Tour standards doc) rather than ed.sc.gov directly; recheck before citing in print.
4. **No standard asks the encounter's question.** Every code above covers popular sovereignty, the Convention, the judiciary, or the sources — none asks whether the doctrine stands when the man falls. The judgment is the library's design, not a standards artifact; the standards supply the evidence base.
5. **Convention words are Madison's reporting** (per the figure's own sources.md); the RH.9-10.6 comparison should be framed as reported speech, not transcript.
