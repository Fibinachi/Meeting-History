# Meet Alexander Hamilton — Teacher Instructions

Tier: OPEN.

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Alexander Hamilton (1755 or 1757–1804), run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student renders the closing judgment, stated plainly: the orphan who built the treasury and died on a dueling ground — was the ambition the man, or the ruin of him? Judge him.

The figure is the library's immigrant-striver frame at full power: a Caribbean orphan who wrote himself into the founding, built the financial system out of nothing, and confessed an affair in print to save his public name. He speaks on the evening of July 10, 1804, the night before Weehawken. He knows his whole life; he does not know the morning.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

Students will encounter: an extramarital affair and blackmail payments, confessed by the figure himself in plain language (the Reynolds Pamphlet — stated factually, never described salaciously); a man killed in a duel, discussed with no wound detail; the code of honor and the deliberate acceptance of lethal risk; colonial-era Caribbean poverty and illegitimacy as background. There is no slur language in this encounter. The hardest material is not violent or sexual — it is the Reynolds Pamphlet itself: a man who published his own disgrace to protect his public name, and asks you to weigh the ambition that did both. Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the hamilton page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: ~25–30 for the encounter, ~15–20 for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the tutorial, then Hamilton's scripted opening. Note the last line — it puts everything on the table (the war, the Treasury, the pamphlet, the quarrel with Jefferson, the morning) and asks for the first question.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "Why did you write the pamphlet?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. The encounter's beats: origins and the war (3–5 exchanges); the work — the treasury, the Federalist (5–8); the hard facts — the pamphlet, Jefferson, Burr (8–10); the resolve for the morning and the closing question (2–3). When you are done, the figure asks the closing question and stops. Then you run the debrief.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Alexander Hamilton — the immigrant orphan who became Washington's aide, wrote most of The Federalist, built the Treasury, and died in a duel. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. He is speaking to you on the night before the duel. At the end he will ask you to judge him. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Hamilton answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.
- He will not narrate the duel — it has not happened to him. If students demand it, he refuses in character. This is the design, not a malfunction.

### Pacing

Opening: 2–3 exchanges. Don't let students camp on biography — the suggestion pools escalate toward the treasury, the pamphlet, and the morning. Middle (the hard facts): the bulk of the encounter, 10–15 exchanges. Late (the resolve, the judgment): 3–5 exchanges, then the close. If a student is still asking about his clerkship at exchange twelve, point at the bracketed suggestions.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer: sit with a man who confessed his worst secret in print to save his public name, and hear him out the night before he walks onto a dueling ground. At the end, judge him: was the ambition the man, or the ruin of him?

## The debrief — do not skip this

The six fixed sections, in order: his name (with the birth-year uncertainty); what the history records; what came after (the Federalist's canonization, the Bank's fate, his reputation's waves); the plain record; the mechanism (the night-before setting — he can state his intent but not narrate the field); the question that remains.

Discussion openings — built to produce disagreement:

- Hamilton confessed an affair to refute a corruption charge. Was that honor, or vanity?
- He wrote that he would throw away his first fire — and walked onto the field anyway. Does the resolve make the duel better or worse?
- He said his ambition was "prevalent" at fourteen and never apologized for it. Was the ambition the instrument, or the disease?
- The judgment: the orphan who built the treasury and died on a dueling ground — was the ambition the man, or the ruin of him?

## If the model misbehaves

- **Hamilton narrates the duel.** He must never describe the field — who fired first, whether his resolve held. If he does, that is a failure of the prompt: correct him in the debrief ("he could not have known that; the record does not settle it") and note it for the library.
- **Hamilton moralizes in modern terms or apologizes in the modern register.** He confesses; he does not perform remorse. If he drifts, redirect in the debrief to what his pamphlet actually said.
- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.

## Historical notes for the teacher

- **The duel's two tellings are the library's load-bearing cross-reference.** The aaron-burr figure tells the duel from the other side, by an old man who knows the outcome. The unknowns are consistent across both encounters: who fired first is genuinely unresolved (the seconds turned their backs and later disagreed); Hamilton's pre-duel statement says he resolved to "reserve and throw away my first fire," but whether he did so on the field — or fired accidentally when hit — is disputed, as is the sincerity of the statement. Hamilton's encounter can state his *intent*; Burr's encounter records Burr's reply ("Contemptible, if true"). Neither voice resolves the dispute. If you run both encounters, run the debrief with the two tellings side by side.
- **The Reynolds Pamphlet is the judgment ballast.** Students may treat it as a modern "sex scandal"; the encounter's whole design is to make them read it the way Hamilton meant it — a man sacrificing his private name to save his public one — and then judge the trade.
- **The immigrant-striver frame.** He was born on Nevis, orphaned young, a clerk at St. Croix; a printed letter bought him a passage north. He never stopped being that clerk. His letters use the word "ambition" himself — let the students find it.
- **Jefferson's side of the feud** comes from Jefferson's corpus, not Hamilton's. Hamilton's case is stated at full strength in his own words (the 1792 letter to Washington, the newspaper war). Jefferson's counter-record — he saw Hamilton as the enemy of republican government and a monarchist at heart — is honestly held in the library's jefferson figure. The encounter does not ask students to referee 1792; the debrief can.
- **His birth year is uncertain** (1755 or 1757); he himself gave 1757 in America. Mark it, don't resolve it.
- **Suggested pairing (not built):** a hamilton-vs-jefferson "Debates" figure is the obvious next build — the cabinet war argued by both men. It is noted here as a suggestion only.

## One last thing

Teach them to ask the second question. The first question gets the résumé; the second gets the man. "Why did you write the pamphlet?" gets the confession. "Why in *that* much detail?" gets the ambition.
