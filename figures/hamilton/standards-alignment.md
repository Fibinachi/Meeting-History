# Meet Alexander Hamilton — Standards Alignment (verified 2026-10-15)

Encounter: students question a simulated Alexander Hamilton (1755 or 1757–1804) on the evening of July 10, 1804, the night before Weehawken — the immigrant orphan who became Washington's aide, wrote fifty-one of The Federalist Papers, and built the American financial system from nothing. They press him on the Treasury (funded debt, assumption, the Bank, manufactures), on the cabinet war with Jefferson, on the Reynolds Pamphlet (the affair confessed in print to answer a corruption charge), on the challenge from Burr, and on the thrown-away fire he has resolved to hold — which he states but will not narrate, because the field has not happened to him. It closes with his question: *the orphan who built the treasury and died on a dueling ground — was the ambition the man, or the ruin of him? Judge him.*

**Grade band fit for every framework below: 9–12** (high school U.S. History / Government / ELA literacy). Note: most states' *content* anchors for the founding era sit in grades 7–8 (see caveats); the 9–12 weight comes from South Carolina's USHC placement, Texas's high-school Government course (which names the Federalist Papers explicitly), and skills standards — argument, source critique — which are band-matched.

## 1. C3 Framework (National Council for the Social Studies, 2013)

Grade band: **9–12**. (Indicator texts verified against curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim; the official NCSS PDF was not directly retrievable in this session — see verification note at end.)

### Dimension 2 — Civics

- **D2.Civ.6.9-12** — Critique relationships among governments, civil societies, and economic markets. (The financial system itself: the funded debt that "tied every man's fortune to the government's survival," the assumption of state debts, the Bank, the duties sheltering infant industry — his design for binding markets to government.)
- **D2.Civ.10.9-12** — Analyze the impact and the appropriate roles of personal interests and perspectives on the application of civic virtues, democratic principles, constitutional rights, and human rights. (The encounter's load-bearing move: his own named motive — "I will not apologize for my ambition" — and the honor code that takes him to Weehawken, weighed against the public measures he defends with argument.)

### Dimension 2 — History

- **D2.His.11.9-12** — Critique the usefulness of historical sources for a specific historical inquiry based on their maker, date, place of origin, intended audience, and purpose. (The pre-duel statement as self-authored evidence — written the night before, sealed with his will; the Reynolds Pamphlet as a strategic confession, published to answer a public charge; the two seconds, who turned their backs and later disagreed, as the only witnesses.)
- **D2.His.16.9-12** — Integrate evidence from multiple relevant historical sources and interpretations into a reasoned argument about the past. (The treasury reports, the fifty-one Federalist numbers, the Pamphlet, the Burr correspondence, the pre-duel statement — the student synthesizes them into the closing judgment.)

### Dimension 4 — Communicating Conclusions and Taking Informed Action

- **D4.1.9-12** — Construct arguments using precise and knowledgeable claims, with evidence from multiple sources, while acknowledging counterclaims and evidentiary weaknesses. (The closing judgment — was the ambition the man or the ruin of him — with the unresolved field as the acknowledged evidentiary weakness: "The only witnesses were the two seconds, who turned their backs and later disagreed.")
- **D4.4.9-12** — Critique the use of claims and evidence in arguments for credibility. (His distinction between the real crime and the refused apology: "I confessed the private shame to answer the public charge" — the student tests what he treats as the crime against what he refuses to apologize for.)

## 2. Common Core ELA — Literacy in History/Social Studies & Speaking/Listening

Grade band: **9–12**. (Verified against the official Common Core State Standards Initiative site, thecorestandards.org.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 9–10

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.6** — Compare the point of view of two or more authors for how they treat the same or similar topics, including which details they include and emphasize in their respective accounts. (His 1792 newspaper essays as T.L., An American, Amicus, Catullus, Metellus against Freneau's *National Gazette* — the cabinet war fought in print, two partisan presses on the same measures.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.8** — Assess the extent to which the reasoning and evidence in a text support the author's claims. (The treasury reports' claims — the debt "if not excessive, would be a national blessing" — against the design he states: tying every man's fortune to the government's survival.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.6** — Evaluate authors' differing points of view on the same historical event or issue by assessing the authors' claims, reasoning, and evidence. (The ratification debate: his fifty-one Federalist numbers against the Anti-Federalist attack — the standard's canonical use case.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.8** — Evaluate an author's premises, claims, and evidence by corroborating or challenging them with other information. (The Reynolds Pamphlet's central argument — that the private shame answers the public charge of speculation — tested against the payments and correspondence he himself published.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.3** — Evaluate various explanations for actions or events and determine which explanation best accords with textual evidence, acknowledging where the text leaves matters uncertain. (The field: who fired first, whether he threw away his shot as he resolved to — the debrief marks it "genuinely unresolved," and the encounter's design is built around the marked unknown.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.1** — Cite specific textual evidence to support analysis of primary and secondary sources, connecting insights gained from specific details to an understanding of the text as a whole. (The pre-duel statement's exact resolve — "I will reserve and throw away my first fire" — and the 1769 Stevens letter's "my Ambition is prevalent.")

### Reading: Informational Text, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RI.11-12.9** — Analyze 17th-, 18th-, and 19th-century foundational U.S. documents of historical and literary significance (including The Declaration of Independence, the Preamble to the Constitution, the Bill of Rights, and Lincoln's Second Inaugural Address) for their themes, purposes, and rhetorical features. (His fifty-one Federalist numbers are the 18th-century foundational documents in this slot — "eighty-five numbers in less than a year; fifty-one are mine.")

### Speaking & Listening, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.1** — Initiate and participate effectively in a range of collaborative discussions (one-on-one, in groups, and teacher-led) with diverse partners on grades 11–12 topics, texts, and issues, building on others' ideas and expressing their own clearly and persuasively. (The encounter *is* a sustained one-on-one examination of a witness — "You ask. I answer.")
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.3** — Evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric, assessing the stance, premises, links among ideas, word choice, points of emphasis, and tone used. (The encounter's whole method: the heat on his honor versus the argument on his measures — attacks on his character draw fire, attacks on his policies draw the Federalist cadence.)

### Writing, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.W.11-12.1** — Write arguments to support claims in an analysis of substantive topics or texts, using valid reasoning and relevant and sufficient evidence. (The closing question — was the ambition the man, or the ruin of him? — is designed as a post-encounter argument prompt, per the teacher instructions.)

## 3. South Carolina — Social Studies College- and Career-Ready Standards (2019), US History and the Constitution

Grade band: **9–12** (high school US History). South Carolina places the founding era at 9–12, which makes it the cleanest on-band state fit.

- **Standard 1** — Demonstrate an understanding of the influence of the Atlantic World on the regional and national development of republicanism and federalism from 1607–1815.
- **USHC.1.CE** — Assess the major developments of the American Revolution through significant turning points in the debates over independence and self-government from 1763 to 1791. (State guidance explicitly names the Federalist Papers among the early founding documents in scope — his fifty-one numbers, the defense of the Constitution he built the Treasury under.)
- **USHC.1.P** — Summarize the changing relationship between individuals and the government during the period 1607 to 1800. (State guidance names "Hamilton vs. Jefferson (Industry vs. Agriculture)" among its content — the cabinet war is in the standard; the assumption and Bank fights are its evidence.)
- **USHC.1.CC** — Analyze the processes of continuity and change in the debates over the proper role of the central government and neutrality in foreign affairs from 1789 to 1815. (The financial system as the opening position: funded debt, assumption, the Bank — and its dismantling and rebuilding across the century.)
- **USHC.1.E** — Utilize primary and secondary sources to investigate the impact of Atlantic influence on the development of republicanism and federalism. (The treasury reports, the Federalist numbers, and the Pamphlet as the source set.)

*Verified against: the official SC Department of Education US History and the Constitution Alignment Guide (August 2024), an official interpretation of the 2019 standards (ed.sc.gov).*

## Verification caveats

1. **C3 Framework**: indicator texts were verified against three independent curriculum documents that quote the Framework verbatim (they agree with each other); the official NCSS PDF itself was not directly retrievable this session. Treat the official publisher as NCSS (2013), socialstudies.org/c3.
2. **Grade-band split.** Only South Carolina (USHC) and Texas's high-school Government course (§113.44) place founding-era content at 9–12. New York, Florida, and California teach the founding era in grades 7–8; Texas's high-school U.S. History course (§113.41) begins at 1877. The encounter targets 9–12, so those anchors are real but one band below target.
3. **Texas §113.41**: the task brief suggested §113.41 might hold founding-era anchors; it does not — it is "United States History Studies Since 1877." The on-band Texas anchor is §113.44 (US Government), verified above.
4. **No standard asks the encounter's question.** Every code above covers the financial system, the Federalist Papers, the ratification debate, or source evidence — none asks students to judge whether the ambition was the man or the ruin of him. The judgment is the library's design, not a standards artifact; the standards supply the evidence base.
5. All verifications in this document were performed on 2026-10-15.
