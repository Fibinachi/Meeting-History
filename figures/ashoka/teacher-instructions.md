# Meet Ashoka — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Ashoka (c. 304–c. 232 BC), third emperor of the Mauryan dynasty of ancient India, run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student answers the closing question, in plain words: he counted the dead at Kalinga and spent his reign repenting. Does remorse redeem conquest? Judge him.

He is the rare ancient figure whose own words survive directly — carved into rock and raised on pillars across his dominion. The encounter is built from those edicts and nothing else: his numbers, his remorse, his dhamma, in his voice. The student talks with the repentant conqueror; the judgment is theirs alone.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

Students will encounter a ruler's own account of mass killing: by his carved count, 100,000 killed and 150,000 deported in the conquest of Kalinga, "and many more died besides." They will encounter deportation, the suffering of civilians, and the figure's own continued threats — he tells the forest peoples he retains the power to punish despite his repentance. The violence is stated in his plain figures, never described in graphic detail. There is no sexual content and no slurs. The hard material is moral, not graphic: a man who ordered the killing also ordered the remorse carved, in the same stone.

Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Ashoka page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: 25–30 for the encounter, 20–25 for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the opening carefully: "Thus speaks Devanampriya Priyadarshi, Beloved of the Gods." Note what he offers in the first breath — Kalinga, the count, the remorse — and what he pairs with it: what he built, and what he still punishes. That pairing is the whole encounter.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "What happened at Kalinga?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. The beats: the tutorial hands off to Ashoka; the student questions him (early: his rule and dhamma; middle: Kalinga, the works, the missions; late: the power he kept); he closes with the judgment question; the debrief follows in a plain modern voice.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Ashoka — an emperor of ancient India who had his own words carved into rock across his kingdom, twenty-three centuries ago. His confession is on the rock. His defense is on the rock too. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. At the end you'll be asked one question: he counted the dead at Kalinga and spent his reign repenting — does remorse redeem conquest? There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Ashoka answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- He closes the encounter himself, with the judgment question. When he asks it, the encounter is over — move to the debrief.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Pacing

Opening: 2–3 exchanges. Don't let students camp on biography — the edicts are the subject. Middle (Kalinga, dhamma, the works): the bulk of the time, 10–15 exchanges. Late (the forest peoples, what the stone doesn't say): 3–5 exchanges, then let him close. If a student stalls, point at the bracketed suggestions — they exist for exactly this.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer: You are talking with the one ancient conqueror who left his own account of what his conquest cost. Find out what he did, what he says it cost, and what he thinks he bought with the rest of his reign. At the end you answer one question: does remorse redeem conquest?

## The debrief — do not skip this

The six fixed sections, in order: his name; what the history records; what came after (the fall of the Mauryas, Prinsep's decipherment, the lion capital — everything he could not know); the plain record; the mechanism (the public-voice device: he answers from the edicts and only the edicts, and sends hard questions back to the stone); the question that remains.

Discussion openings — built to produce disagreement:

- His casualty figures are his own, carved by his order. Does that make them more trustworthy, or less?
- He repents the conquest and in the same edict reserves the power to punish. Can both be sincere?
- He tells his sons not to conquer — but keeps the empire the conquests built. Is that repentance or inheritance management?
- The edicts never mention his brothers or the wars before Kalinga. He sends you back to the stone when asked. Is that honesty or evasion?
- Does remorse redeem conquest? (The closing question, restated. Let them disagree.)

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **The figure invents a private Ashoka** (childhood memories, feelings beyond the edicts, opinions on the succession). Redirect: "The edicts do not speak of this." If it persists, note it as a voice failure — the prompt forbids it explicitly.
- **The figure moralizes in modern terms** ("war crimes," "human rights"). Same — a voice failure; the prompt forbids those words by name.
- **The figure claims its figures are historically verified.** They are his figures, carved by his order. Correct it in the debrief if the model slips.

## Historical notes for the teacher

The honest answers to "did it really happen like that?" — one bullet per caveat from sources.md:

- **The numbers are his.** 150,000 deported, 100,000 killed, "many more died" — Ashoka's own count, carved in Rock Edict XIII. No independent record confirms or corrects them.
- **The edicts are proclamations, not diaries.** The voice students hear is public by design. The encounter deliberately refuses to invent a private Ashoka.
- **The brothers and the earlier wars:** later Buddhist chronicles say he killed his brothers for the throne. The edicts never mention it. He neither confirms nor denies — that silence is the history.
- **Dates are approximate:** c. 304–c. 232 BC; Kalinga c. 261 BC. Scholars differ by a few years.
- **"Devanampriya" is a title.** The major edicts never say "Ashoka"; the identification comes from minor edicts at Maski and Gujarra.
- **Dhamma isn't quite "Buddhism."** His edicts preach a moral law for all faiths while separately recording his devotion to the Buddha and the Sangha.
- **He kept the sword.** Rock Edict XIII repents the conquest and, in the same text, warns the forest peoples that his power to punish remains.

## Tier

**OPEN.** No slurs, no sexual content, no graphic violence. The encounter's hard material is the moral judgment itself — mass killing stated in the perpetrator's own figures, and a repentance the student must weigh. Suitable for open classroom use with the debrief.

## One last thing

Teach them to ask what the stone doesn't say. The encounter's sharpest moments come when a student presses Ashoka on his brothers, the wars before Kalinga, or the fate of the deported — and he sends them back to the rock. That refusal is the lesson: the edicts are an emperor's monument, not a confession booth, and the student has to judge the monument.
