# Meet Ashoka — Standards Alignment (verified 2026-10-15)

Encounter: the student questions Ashoka — Devanampriya Priyadarshi — late in his reign (c. 233 BC), speaking only from his edicts: the Kalinga conquest of his eighth regnal year (150,000 deported, 100,000 killed, "and many more died"), his remorse carved in Rock Edict XIII, his definition of dhamma, the welfare works, "conquest by dhamma" among the Greek kings, and the forest-peoples warning that his repentance did not disarm him. Where the edicts go silent — his brothers, the wars before Kalinga — he points to the stone. It closes with his scripted question: *does remorse redeem conquest? Judge him.*

**Grade band fit for every framework below: 9–12** (high school ELA literacy / World History skills). Note: as with Socrates, the states' *content* anchors for classical India sit in lower grades (see caveats); the 9–12 weight of this encounter comes from skills standards — primary-source analysis, source criticism, argumentation — which are band-matched.

## 1. C3 Framework (National Council for the Social Studies, 2013)

Grade band: **9–12**. (Indicator texts verified against independent curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim; the official NCSS PDF was not directly retrievable in this session — see verification note at end.)

### Dimension 2 — History

- **D2.His.6.9-12** — Analyze the ways in which the perspectives of those writing history shaped the history that they produced. (The edicts are imperial proclamations, not diary entries — the debrief says so outright: "You were never talking to a confession. You were talking to a monument." The remorse exists in the stone because an emperor put it there.)
- **D2.His.8.9-12** — Analyze how current interpretations of the past are limited by the extent to which available historical sources represent perspectives of people at the time. (The casualty figures are *his* figures, carved by his order — no independent record confirms or corrects them; the encounter treats them as his claim, never as verified fact.)
- **D2.His.10.9-12** — Detect possible limitations in various kinds of historical evidence and differing secondary interpretations. (The succession bloodshed of the later Buddhist chronicles vs. the edicts' silence — secondhand, centuries later, outside the corpus; the figure neither confirms nor denies.)
- **D2.His.11.9-12** — Critique the usefulness of historical sources for a specific historical inquiry based on their maker, date, place of origin, intended audience, and purpose. (The edicts as sources: maker — the emperor; audience — subjects, officers, posterity; purpose — to rule by dhamma and to instruct his sons. The load-bearing nudge — "where he sends you when the question gets hard — back to the stone" — is this critique in miniature.)
- **D2.His.16.9-12** — Integrate evidence from multiple relevant historical sources and interpretations into a reasoned argument about the past. (The closing judgment must integrate Rock Edict XIII's remorse with its forest-peoples warning — "the king who repents is still the king" — and the debrief's plain record.)

### Dimension 4 — Communicating Conclusions and Taking Informed Action

- **D4.1.9-12** — Construct arguments using precise and knowledgeable claims, with evidence from multiple sources, while acknowledging counterclaims and evidentiary weaknesses. (Does remorse redeem conquest? — the argument must acknowledge that the remorse is real in the stone and that the stone was also his instrument of rule.)
- **D4.4.9-12** — Critique the use of claims and evidence in arguments for credibility. (Weighing his carved numbers — his count, his remorse, his warning — as evidence for the judgment, against the chroniclers' later stories.)

*Verified against: crfcap.org Washington State Standards/C3 dashboard; cdn.fairfieldschools.org district C3 documents; c3teachers.org blueprint documents. Official document: College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework for Social Studies State Standards, NCSS, 2013 (socialstudies.org/c3).*

## 2. Common Core ELA — Literacy in History/Social Studies, Speaking/Listening, Writing

Grade band: **9–12**. (Verified against the official Common Core State Standards Initiative site, thecorestandards.org.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 9–10 and 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.1 / RH.11-12.1** — 9–10: "Cite specific textual evidence to support analysis of primary and secondary sources, attending to such features as the date and origin of the information." / 11–12: "Cite specific textual evidence to support analysis of primary and secondary sources, connecting insights gained from specific details to an understanding of the text as a whole." (The edicts are first-person primary sources — rock, pillar, and minor edicts, c. 259–241 BC — and date/origin discipline is the whole encounter: what the stone says vs. what it doesn't.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.2 / RH.11-12.2** — 9–10: "Determine the central ideas or information of a primary or secondary source; provide an accurate summary of how key events or ideas develop over the course of the text." / 11–12: "Determine the central ideas or information of a primary or secondary source; provide an accurate summary that makes clear the relationships among the key details and ideas." (Dhamma — "little evil, much good," Pillar Edict II's definition — is the central idea the student must state in plain terms; the debrief's Goal section demands it.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.6** — Evaluate authors' differing points of view on the same historical event or issue by assessing the authors' claims, reasoning, and evidence. (His edicts' account of the succession — silence — vs. the later Buddhist chronicles' account of fratricide; assessing claims, reasoning, and evidence across a two-millennium gap, with the debrief's decipherment history as context.)

### Speaking & Listening, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.1** — Initiate and participate effectively in a range of collaborative discussions (one-on-one, in groups, and teacher-led) with diverse partners on grades 11–12 topics, texts, and issues, building on others' ideas and expressing their own clearly and persuasively. (The encounter is a sustained one-on-one examination — "question him the way a jury questions a witness.")
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.3** — Evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric, assessing the stance, premises, links among ideas, word choice, points of emphasis, and tone used. (The debrief teaches students to notice the edict cadence — and the designed move of pointing to the stone when the question gets hard.)

### Writing, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.W.11-12.1** — Write arguments to support claims in an analysis of substantive topics or texts, using valid reasoning and relevant and sufficient evidence. (The closing judgment — does remorse redeem conquest — is designed as a post-encounter argument prompt.)

## Verification caveats

1. **C3 Framework**: indicator texts verified against multiple independent curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim (they agree with each other and with the wave-1 verification of 2026-10-15); the official NCSS PDF itself was not directly retrievable this session. Official publisher: NCSS (2013), socialstudies.org/c3.
2. **Grade-band split**: the 9–12 alignments for Ashoka are skills standards (RH, SL, W, D2.His/D4). Every state places classical-India content below the 9–12 band (SC Grade 6, FL Grade 6, CA Grade 6) except NY Grades 9–10 (9.3b/9.3c — the one on-band content exception) and Texas 9–12 (WH.1.B — on-band, but the indicator text was verified via compilation, not the TEA-hosted PDF).
3. **Florida and California**: the Asoka-naming anchors (FL SS.6.W.4.4, CA 6.5.6) are Grade 6. They are included because they name him — the honest content fit — with the band caveat stated, not hidden.
4. **No figure-specific persecution or "conquest" code is claimed**: the moral content of the encounter (remorse, retained power to punish) is carried by the C3 and writing standards, not by a content code.
