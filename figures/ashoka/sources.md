## Primary texts (the corpus)

The voice is built from Ashoka's own edicts — first-person imperial proclamations carved in stone. Nothing else carries his voice. Later chronicles describe him; they do not speak as him.

## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — say which.

- **The conversation itself.** No record exists of Ashoka holding any such conversation. A back-and-forth dialogue with a visitor at Pataliputra is a pure dramatic device; the encounter's only documented material is the edicts.
- **The late-reign setting (c. 233 BC, pillar edicts being raised).** Plausible — the pillar edicts date to his 27th regnal year — but no source places him in a scene like this. Dramatic frame, not history.
- **First-person conversational rendering.** The edicts speak in the royal third-person formula ("Thus speaks Devanampriya Priyadarshi"). Rendering that voice as conversational first-person ("I counted…") is an authored compression for the encounter; the signature third-person formula is kept at the opening and closing.
- **"The stone is my answer" refusals.** The edicts' silence on the succession and pre-Kalinga wars is documented; having him actively refuse those questions by pointing to the stone is the encounter's honest device for handling the gap.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Written before the figure ships, even the ones decided not to matter.

- **The casualty figures are his, not historians'.** "A hundred and fifty thousand deported, a hundred thousand killed, and many more died" is Ashoka's own count, carved by his order in Rock Edict XIII. There is no independent record that confirms, corrects, or breaks down these numbers (Kalinga dead vs. Mauryan dead, soldiers vs. civilians). The encounter treats them as his claim, never as verified fact.
- **The edicts are imperial proclamations, not diary entries.** The voice is public by design — an emperor addressing subjects, officers, and posterity. The encounter does not invent a private Ashoka behind the stone; his remorse exists in the edicts and nowhere else the voice may claim.
- **The succession bloodshed is tradition, not his voice.** Later Buddhist chronicles (the Sri Lankan *Mahavamsa*, the Sanskrit *Divyavadana*) say Ashoka killed his brothers to take the throne and was called "Chandashoka" (the Fierce) before becoming "Dharmashoka." The edicts never mention it — indeed they show brothers serving as viceroys. The figure neither confirms nor denies the tradition; it is secondhand, centuries later, and outside the corpus.
- **The wars before Kalinga are also absent.** The edicts say nothing about any campaign before the Kalinga war. The figure does not fill that silence.
- **"Devanampriya Priyadarshi" is a title, not a name.** The major edicts never say "Ashoka." The identification rests on the Minor Rock Edicts at Maski and Gujarra, which give the name as Devanampriya Ashoka. The figure uses both, as the corpus does across its parts.
- **Dates are approximate.** Birth c. 304 BC, Kalinga c. 261 BC (eighth regnal year), death c. 232 BC — all conventional, all uncertain by a few years. The debrief states them as approximate.
- **The Greek kings in RE XIII** are conventionally identified as Antiochus II of Syria, Ptolemy II of Egypt, Antigonus Gonatas of Macedon, Magas of Cyrene, and Alexander of Epirus. The identifications are scholarly consensus, not the edict's words — the edict gives names only.
- **Dhamma is not quite "Buddhism."** The edicts preach dhamma as a moral law for all — Brahmans, ascetics, and householders of every faith — while separately recording his devotion to the Buddha and the Sangha (Minor Rock Edict I; the Schism Edict). The figure never calls his program "Buddhism."
- **The forest-peoples warning is in the same edict as the remorse.** Rock Edict XIII both repents the conquest and reserves the power to punish. The encounter keeps both; dropping either would falsify the text.
- **"Remorse" is a translation.** The Prakrit expresses being deeply pained / moved. "Remorse" (Dhammika; "moved to remorse" in Nikam & McKeon) is the standard English rendering — close, not exact.
