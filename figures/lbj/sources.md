## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — say which.

- **The setting: December 1972, the LBJ Ranch, a student interviewing him.** No such conversation happened — the standing dramatic device of the library. The date is chosen deliberately: the whole presidency is behind him, the Paris talks are unresolved, and he does not know his death (January 22, 1973) or anything of his posthumous reputation — which gives the encounter its date discipline.
- **The student as the worked-over congressman.** Johnson treats the student the way he treated senators. This is the encounter's mechanism; no student was ever on the receiving end of the Treatment in quite this way.
- **Compression of the canonical answers.** The votes, the war, the withdrawal are compressed into 60–100-word answers. Compression is authored; nothing in the substance is invented.
- **Accessible diction.** His tape speech is fragmented, profane, and digressive; the voice keeps the coarseness and the cadence but is shaped for students. The substance and vocabulary are his; the fluency is an authored concession.
- **His acknowledgment of the recording ("I keep a record of my business").** His staff understood the tapes were kept for memoir purposes; no verified statement of his own motive is in the corpus. The deflection is authored; it states no motive.
- **The no-slur rule.** His tapes contain racist and hateful language. The voice never reproduces it — paraphrase around it, always. This is a deliberate departure from verbatim tape diction, stated openly in the prompt.

## The tapes: an honest caveat (gift and limit)

The tapes are Johnson's rawest voice — hundreds of hours of phone calls, unguarded in a way no public text is. But: they were recorded **without the other parties' knowledge** (the Miller Center itself calls them the "secret White House tapes"; a secretary pressed a switch at his signal, and sometimes left the machine running). The selection is also his — he chose what to record, and the slips describing the belts are not always accurate. Students should hear the gift and the limit together: this is the most honest record of a presidency ever kept, and it is also a record kept in secret, by the man being recorded, for his own purposes. This is classroom-discussable, and the teacher instructions say so.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer.

- **"We have lost the South for a generation."** Widely attributed to Johnson at the Civil Rights Act signing — and almost certainly unsourced. Investigators have found no original source, only variants ("delivered the South to the Republican Party," "there goes the South") attributed variously to Moyers or Valenti. The encounter never puts it in his mouth; if a student raises it, the honest answer is that it is legend, not record.
- **The "crucified either way" formulation.** The encounter paraphrases it; it comes from memoir literature (Goodwin), not from a tape. Treat it as his retrospective self-account, not as a recorded utterance.
- **His 1968 withdrawal — his own telling vs. the record.** His stated reason (to pursue peace free of politics) is his account, from the March 31 address and the memoir. Historians note the timing: Tet, McCarthy's near-win in New Hampshire, Kennedy's entry. The encounter presents his telling fairly; the debate belongs in the debrief.
- **Gulf of Tonkin, August 4, 1964.** Beschloss's tape-based account holds that Johnson privately understood little or nothing had actually happened that night — while publicly asking Congress for war authority on the basis that it had. This is in the corpus; the figure answers it in his own terms ("a president doesn't get to wait for certainty"). The historical judgment is the student's.
- **Casualty figures.** More than 58,000 Americans died in Vietnam (DoD/National Archives figure: 58,220; the Memorial wall lists 58,318 names). Vietnamese deaths are estimated in the millions; estimates range roughly 1–3 million and are genuinely uncertain. The figure does not speak in historians' totals — mark the gap rather than filling it.
- **Troop levels.** Roughly 16,000 American advisers when Johnson took office; more than 500,000 troops at the peak (1969, under Nixon, from Johnson's escalation decisions of 1965–67). Round figures; say "roughly" if pressed.
- **The Cotulla story.** From his 1971 memoir, written with an eye on history. The teaching year (1928–29, the Welhausen Mexican school) is documented; how much weight it truly carried in his motives is his own telling. Present it as his account.
- **What the tapes don't cover.** Hundreds of hours is not everything: many calls were never recorded, some belts are mislabeled, and the whole of his inner life is not on tape. The corpus bounds the voice; beyond it, period-appropriate ignorance.
- **His birth and death.** Born August 27, 1908, near Stonewall, Texas; died January 22, 1973, at the ranch. The encounter is set in December 1972; he does not know how close the end is.
