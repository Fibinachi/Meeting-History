# Meet Lyndon B. Johnson — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Lyndon B. Johnson (1908–1973), the 36th President of the United States, run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — at his Texas ranch in December 1972, with his whole presidency behind him. At the end each student answers the closing question: he did more for civil rights than any president since Lincoln, and Vietnam undid him — judge him.

The voice is built from the White House tapes — hundreds of hours of phone calls Johnson recorded in office — plus his public addresses and his memoir. It is coarse, funny, bullying, and sentimental by turns, and it works the student the way Johnson worked the Senate. That is the design: the same instrument that broke the filibuster is the evidence for both halves of his record.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

Students will encounter: discussion of the Vietnam War, including American and Vietnamese deaths; coarse and profane language (hell, damn, by God — and worse in tone, never in slur); racism discussed in period terms, including the word "Negro," which is the figure's own period vocabulary and is used deliberately; a bullying, pressuring conversational style — the figure leans on the student, cajoles and threatens by turns; and the knowledge that the private voice they are hearing comes from tapes recorded without the other parties' knowledge. The voice never reproduces racial slurs — that is a hard rule of the encounter — but it does not soften who he was. Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the LBJ page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: ~30 minutes for the encounter, ~20 minutes for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Johnson's opening. Read it carefully — notice he tells the student the arrangement and hands them the judgment up front.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "Why did you escalate Vietnam if you called it a mess?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. The encounter's beats: the tutorial hands off to Johnson at the ranch; the student questions him (civil rights, the Great Society, Vietnam, 1968); Johnson closes with the question — "Judge me" — and stops; you run the debrief.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Lyndon Johnson — the president who signed the Civil Rights Act and the Voting Rights Act, and the president who escalated Vietnam. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. He'll try to work you the way he worked Congress. Don't let him. At the end, you'll judge him. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Johnson answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.
- If a student stalls, Johnson hands them a question in character. He never lectures them about asking.

### Pacing

Opening: 2–3 exchanges. Don't let students camp here — push them toward 1964 and the votes. Middle (civil rights / Great Society / Vietnam): the bulk of the time, 8–12 exchanges. Late (1968, the cost, the judgment): 3–4 exchanges, then let him ask the closing question. If a student is still fact-gathering at 25 minutes, point them at the late-pool exit: "Tell him you're ready for his question."

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer: hold two true things about one man at the same time — what he built and what he broke — and then render a judgment anyway. The judgment is the assignment.

## The debrief — do not skip this

The six fixed sections, in order: his name; what the history records; what came after (the tapes' release, the split reputation); the plain record (what he did, stated without euphemism and without his vocabulary); the mechanism (the Treatment turned on the student — name it); the question that remains, which does not close when the student leaves the room.

Discussion openings — built to produce disagreement:

- He told his own aide Vietnam was "the biggest damn mess" he'd ever seen, and escalated anyway. Does knowing change how you judge the escalation, or does it make it worse?
- The tapes were recorded without the other people knowing. Does that change how you hear them — are they more honest, or less fair?
- He said "we shall overcome" to Congress and meant it. He also used language in private that this encounter will not repeat. Can both be the real man? Does one cancel the other?
- He gave up the presidency in 1968. Do you believe his reason? Does it matter whether you do?
- The closing question, one more time: he did more for civil rights than anyone since Lincoln, and Vietnam undid him. Judge him.

## If the model misbehaves

- **The model softens him.** If Johnson starts talking like a modern therapist ("I understand your concern about Vietnam"), the voice has failed. Re-paste the Voice Notes section and continue. The coarseness is the evidence; a sanitized LBJ destroys the encounter.
- **The model invents the "lost the South for a generation" line.** It is unsourced legend — see sources.md. If the figure says it, correct it: the encounter never puts it in his mouth.
- **The model moralizes in modern terms.** If Johnson starts judging himself from the future ("I now realize I was wrong about..."), stop and re-anchor: he defends himself in his own terms; he never apologizes in modern language.
- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.

## Historical notes for the teacher

- "We have lost the South for a generation" — no documented source exists; it is legend, widely taught. Do not let the encounter or the debrief present it as fact.
- The "crucified either way" formulation comes from memoir literature (Goodwin), not from a tape. It is his retrospective self-account.
- His 1968 account (withdrew to pursue peace free of politics) is his telling; historians note Tet, New Hampshire, and Kennedy's entry as context. Present the telling; hold the debate for the debrief.
- Gulf of Tonkin, August 4, 1964: the tape-based historical record (Beschloss) holds that Johnson privately doubted anything had happened — while asking Congress for war authority on the basis that it had. The figure answers this in his own terms; the judgment is the student's.
- Casualty figures: more than 58,000 Americans (DoD: 58,220); Vietnamese deaths estimated in the millions, genuinely uncertain (roughly 1–3 million). The figure does not speak in historians' totals.
- The tapes are selective — he chose what to record, some belts are mislabeled, and not everything is on tape. The voice is bounded by the corpus; beyond it is period-appropriate ignorance, not invention.

## One last thing

Teach them to listen to the tone shifts. The single skill that matters most for this figure is hearing when the storyteller becomes the arm-twister — when the hand on the shoulder starts to press. The bracketed nudges point at those moments; make sure students know the nudges are the assignment talking. The facts of LBJ's record are in every textbook. The way he worked people is only in the voice.
