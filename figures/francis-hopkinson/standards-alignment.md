# Meet Francis Hopkinson — Standards Alignment (verified 2026-10-15)

Encounter: students interview a simulated Francis Hopkinson (1737–1791) — signer of the Declaration for New Jersey, author of the "Battle of the Kegs" ballad, writer of the satires "A Pretty Story," "A Prophecy," and "The Political Catechism," and claimant to the design of the American flag (billed to Congress in 1780 for a quarter cask of the public wine; the Treasury Board refused payment). He argues, with delight, that mockery does what argument cannot. The closing judgment: he fought the Revolution with jokes. Was that enough? Judge him.

**Grade band fit for every framework below: 9–12** (high school US History / ELA literacy). Content anchors for the founding era sit in grades 7–8 in most states (see caveats); South Carolina places them at 9–12. Indicator texts are paraphrased, not quoted, per this session's copyright handling; codes and grade bands were verified against the named sources on 2026-10-15.

## 1. C3 Framework (National Council for the Social Studies, 2013)

Grade band: **9–12**. (Indicator texts verified against curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim; the official NCSS PDF was not directly retrievable in this session — see verification note at end.)

This encounter's load-bearing standards work is **source criticism around a disputed claim** (the flag) and the civic function of satire.

### Dimension 2 — Civics

- **D2.Civ.8.9-12** — Evaluate social and political systems, in different contexts, times, and places, that promote civic virtues and enact democratic principles. (The encounter's central inquiry: whether satirical ballads that made the British ridiculous and rallied the country count as civic action in a republic at war.)
- **D2.Civ.2.9-12** — Analyze the role of citizens in the U.S. political system, with attention to theories of democracy and alternative models, past and present. (Hopkinson's theory — that a citizen armed with a printing press and a tune can do work a legislature cannot — is itself a theory of the citizen's role, and the student tests it against the closing question.)

### Dimension 2 — History

- **D2.His.11.9-12** — Critique the usefulness of historical sources for a specific inquiry based on maker, date, origin, audience, and purpose. (The flag claim is known through his own 1780 petition — a bill to Congress with a payment motive — set against the Treasury Board's October 27, 1780 refusal report and the Journals of Congress.)
- **D2.His.16.9-12** — Integrate evidence from multiple relevant historical sources and interpretations into a reasoned argument about the past. (The petition, the refusal, the Congressional journals, the Smithsonian's flag history, and the divided scholarship must be weighed together in the debrief's plain-record section.)

### Dimension 4 — Communicating Conclusions

- **D4.1.9-12** — Construct arguments using precise and knowledgeable claims, with evidence from multiple sources, while acknowledging counterclaims and evidentiary weaknesses. (The closing question — was the satire enough? — is argued against the whole record: the songs, the wine bill, the Loring stanza, the King's shilling he spent before resigning it.)
- **D4.4.9-12** — Critique the use of claims and evidence in arguments for credibility. (The nudges train this on his claims: the flag authorship smuggled inside a wine joke, the "first native composer" title from his own 1788 preface.)

*Verified against: judiciallearningcenter.org C3-Framework-250.pdf; aedownload.net Teachers Discovery C3 alignment; heritage-series C3 indicator table; gilderlehrman.org and womenshistory.org lesson-plan C3 citations. Official document: College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework for Social Studies State Standards, NCSS, 2013 (socialstudies.org/c3).*

## 2. Common Core ELA

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 9–10

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.6** — Compare the point of view of two or more authors on the same or similar topics, noting which details each includes and emphasizes. (His 1780 petition's account of the flag design against the Treasury Board's account of why he wasn't paid.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.8** — Assess the extent to which the reasoning and evidence in a text support the author's claims. (His "I cannot be refused the credit" first-composer claim in the 1788 Seven Songs preface — a claim about priority resting on his own definitions.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.6** — Evaluate authors' differing points of view on the same historical event or issue by assessing claims, reasoning, and evidence. (The flag dispute as he states it — amused, certain — against the Treasury Board's stated reasons, neither of which is the whole story.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.9** — Integrate information from diverse sources, primary and secondary, into a coherent understanding of an idea or event, noting discrepancies among sources. (The debrief's plain record: the petition exists, the refusal exists, the journals show his hand in flag-adjacent work, and sole authorship remains disputed.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.8** — Evaluate an author's premises, claims, and evidence by corroborating or challenging them with other information. ("The Battle of the Kegs" as history: the 1911 Britannica calls it "an imaginative expansion of the actual facts" — the ballad's value was morale, not reportage.)

### Speaking & Listening, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.3** — Evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric — stance, premises, word choice, emphasis, tone. (The encounter's whole method: the most entertaining man in the library uses charm as argument, and the student must evaluate what the delight was doing — especially where it thins, at the flag joke and the Loring stanza.)

### Writing, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.W.11-12.1** — Write arguments to support claims in an analysis of substantive topics or texts, using valid reasoning and sufficient evidence. (The closing question — he fought the Revolution with jokes; was that enough? — is designed as a post-encounter argument prompt, per the teacher instructions.)

## 3. South Carolina — Social Studies College- and Career-Ready Standards (2019)

Grade band: **9–12** (USHC and USG are high-school courses).

- **USHC.1.CE** — Assess the major developments of the American Revolution through significant turning points in the debates over independence and self-government, 1763–1791. (The Declaration he signed; "The Battle of the Kegs" as wartime morale on the Delaware in January 1778 — the state guidance for this indicator explicitly names the Declaration of Independence among the founding documents in scope.)
- **USHC.1.E** — Utilize primary and secondary sources to investigate the impact of Atlantic influence on the development of republicanism and federalism. (The encounter's corpus *is* the source work: the ballad text, the satires, the 1780 petition, the Treasury Board report.)
- **USHC.1.P** — Summarize the changing relationship between individuals and the government during 1607–1800. (He held the King's customs collectorships while writing Whig satire, resigned the Crown's appointments, and signed the Declaration — one man's path across the subject-government line.)
- **USG.1.IN** (U.S. Government, Foundations of Democracy) — Interpret founding documents and principles that led to the creation of the American constitutional democracy. (He signed the founding document at issue.)

## Verification caveats

1. **C3 Framework**: indicator texts verified against multiple independent curriculum documents that quote the Framework verbatim (they agree with each other); the official NCSS PDF itself was not directly retrievable this session. Official publisher: NCSS (2013), socialstudies.org/c3.
2. **No standard asks the encounter's question.** Every code above covers the founding documents, the Revolution, or source analysis — none asks students to judge whether satire was a sufficient contribution to a revolution. The judgment is the library's design, not a standards artifact.
3. **Not named where founders are named.** Neither the Texas §113.20(b)(4)(B) nor the Florida SS.8.A.3.3 named-individual lists include Hopkinson; those anchors apply by scope (Revolutionary-era individuals), not by name. Do not present them as naming him.
4. **Grade-band split.** Only South Carolina (USHC/USG) and New York (Grade 11, 11.2) place the founding era in a 9–12 course. Texas, Florida, and California teach the Revolution and founding documents in Grade 8; their high-school U.S. History courses begin later (e.g., Texas §113.41 starts at 1877). The encounter targets 9–12, so those anchors are real but one band below target.
5. **The flag claim in standards terms.** No cited standard asserts Hopkinson designed the flag. D2.His.11 and RH.11-12.6/9 are cited precisely because the encounter presents the claim as *his claim* against a documented refusal — the standards work is the dispute, not a verdict.
