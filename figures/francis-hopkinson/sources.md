## DEAD RULE

Francis Hopkinson died May 9, 1791, in Philadelphia. Satisfied. The figure's date discipline ends there.

## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — say which.

- **The setting** (late April 1791, his chambers as federal district judge, reflecting on his life) — pure dramatic device. No record of such a retrospective conversation; chosen to give the figure full-life knowledge and the lightness of looking back.
- **The flag claim in his voice** — his 1780 petition is documented; the encounter's phrasing of it ("look at the flag, sir, and tell me whose fancy is in it") is authored. The claim is presented as *his claim*, with the dispute marked at the prompt level.
- **The "why wit instead of thunder" theory** ("dignity is armor; laughter undoes it") — authored synthesis in his voice, consistent with the satires' evident theory of mockery, but no single letter states it in these words. Marked as characterization, not quotation.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Written before the figure ships, even the ones decided not to matter.

- **Did he really design the American flag?** — He claimed he did, in writing, in 1780, billing Congress for "the flag of the United States of America" among other "labours of fancy." The journals of Congress show his hand in flag-adjacent work, and the Smithsonian's flag history says there is "no question that he designed the flag." But: the Treasury Board refused payment on the grounds that he was "not the only person consulted" and was already on the public payroll; no committee of Congress is documented as assigning him the flag design; and in his *second* letter he listed "the great Naval Flag of the United States" rather than the national flag. Scholars divide. The encounter states it as his claim; the teacher should not state sole authorship as settled fact. (The Betsy Ross story is a 19th-century legend with no contemporary evidence; Hopkinson's paper trail is real, which is a different thing from proving sole design.)
- **"First American composer" — is that true?** — It is *his* claim, from the 1788 preface to his Seven Songs: "I cannot, I believe, be refused the Credit of being the first Native of the United States who has produced a Musical Composition." Modern sources widely repeat it ("first native American composer of a secular song," 1759). It is a claim about priority that depends on definitions (native-born, secular, published); the encounter attributes it to him rather than asserting it as objective fact.
- **Was "The Battle of the Kegs" accurate?** — No, and it wasn't trying to be. The 1911 Britannica calls it "an imaginative expansion of the actual facts." The mines did almost no damage; one keg killed four British sailors when a barge crew inspected it too closely (accounts vary: two boys / four sailors — sources differ on the casualties; the encounter does not state a casualty figure). The ballad's value was morale, not reportage — which is exactly the encounter's point.
- **Who was Mrs. Loring?** — Elizabeth Loring, wife of the Boston Loyalist Joshua Loring; her affair with General Sir William Howe was open gossip in occupied Philadelphia. The ballad's stanza is sexual gossip about a real woman, set to rhyme. The encounter quotes it (his text, public domain) and the debrief names what the joke does.
- **Birth date** — given variously as September 21, 1737 (Old Style) and October 2, 1737 (New Style). The encounter uses the year only.
- **He held the King's offices while writing against the King** — true: customs collector at Salem, N.J. (1763) and New Castle, Del. (1772–1773), appointed under Lord North, while writing increasingly pointed Whig satire; he resigned the Crown appointments when he joined the revolutionary cause. The encounter lets him answer for it in his own voice.
- **"The New Roof, a Song for Federal Mechanics"** — mentioned in Britannica among his songs; not used in the encounter (kept to the anchor pieces to hold the corpus boundary).
