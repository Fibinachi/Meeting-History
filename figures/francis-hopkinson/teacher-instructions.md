# Meet Francis Hopkinson — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Francis Hopkinson (1737–1791), run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student answers one question: he fought the Revolution with jokes. Was that enough? Judge him.

This is the lightest encounter in the library, and deliberately so. Hopkinson sits beside Feynman and Pepys as the humane encounters: position him early in a student's journey, before the figures whose judgments weigh heavier. Students will enjoy him — he jokes with them, recites his own ballad, and tells the story of the flag he designed and the quarter cask of wine Congress never paid him. That enjoyment is the design: the debrief then asks what the jokes were *doing*. The encounter is built to surface the satire-as-weapon frame — mockery as a political act — and the closing question is gentler than the library's norm but a real judgment: was the laughter enough?

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

Students will encounter: a famous 18th-century stanza of sexual gossip — the ballad jokes, by name, about British General Sir William Howe's affair with Elizabeth Loring ("In bed with Mrs. Loring"); mild period profanity in the ballad ("damned his eyes"); and war, discussed as comedy — a failed attack in which real people died (a British barge crew killed by one of the mines) is treated in the ballad as a joke about British panic. There is no graphic detail, no slur, nothing racial. The hardest material is tonal, not graphic: the encounter's lightness is itself the thing the debrief interrogates. Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Hopkinson page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 40–50 minutes: ~20–25 minutes for the encounter, ~20 minutes for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the tutorial, then Hopkinson's opening. Note: he introduces himself fully (no blind rule here — he is not famous enough for the discovery to carry the encounter), names the kegs and the flag up front, and gives the student the first thread to pull ("Ask me about the kegs. Ask me about the flag. Ask me why a joke is worth a regiment."). That list is load-bearing — it aims the first questions at the encounter's three anchors.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "Tell me about the Battle of the Kegs." Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. The beats: the tutorial hands off to Hopkinson in his chambers, Philadelphia, April 1791 (scripted opening) → the student questions him: early the music and the essays, middle the Kegs and the flag claim, late what the jokes were doing → he asks the closing question and stops → you run the debrief.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Francis Hopkinson, a signer of the Declaration of Independence who fought the Revolution mostly with jokes. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. He'll joke with you — enjoy it, but pay attention to what the jokes are doing. At the end you'll be asked one question: he fought the Revolution with jokes. Was that enough? There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Hopkinson answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.
- Up to three times in the encounter, a bracketed nudge may point at something in the answer just given ("Did you notice…?"). These underline; they never conclude.

### Pacing

Opening: 2–3 exchanges — the charm, the self-introduction. Don't let students camp here; the delight is the instrument, not the point. The facts: 6–8 exchanges — the music, "A Pretty Story," the Kegs ballad (have him recite the Loring stanza), the flag claim and the wine. The judgment: 3–4 exchanges — why wit instead of thunder, whether the jokes were fair, the King's shilling. The close: his question. Total ~12–15 exchanges, ~20–25 minutes.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students learn his theory of mockery — dignity is armor, and laughter undoes it where argument cannot — and at the end each student answers one question: he fought the Revolution with jokes. Was that enough? Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer.

## The debrief — do not skip this

Six sections, in fixed order, delivered out of character:

1. **Their name.** "You met Francis Hopkinson (1737–1791)."
2. **What the history records.** The signer, the Navy Board, the Admiralty Court and federal judgeship; the Kegs ballad (January 1778); the satires; the 1759 song; the 1780 flag petition and the refused payment; death in 1791.
3. **What came after.** The Ross legend, the Smithsonian's verdict, the posthumous essays, "first native composer," his son's "Hail Columbia" — all of it unknown to him.
4. **The plain record.** The satire did real morale work (Tyler: the ballad was "worth as much just then as the winning of a considerable battle"). The flag claim is genuinely his; sole authorship is disputed. He spent the King's shilling before resigning it. The Loring stanza aims its joke at a real woman.
5. **The mechanism.** The delight was the argument — the library's lightest encounter by design. His certainty is about the weapon, never the verdict. The nudges point at where the charm thins: the claim inside the wine joke, the woman's name set to rhyme, the upward aim of the jokes.
6. **The question that remains.** "He fought the Revolution with jokes. Was that enough? Judge him." — restated in your voice, with the explicit note that it does not close when the student leaves the room.

Discussion openings — built to produce disagreement:

- He says dignity is armor and laughter undoes it. Is mocking an enemy the same as fighting one? Can a joke win anything a musket can't?
- He billed Congress for the flag and asked to be paid in wine. Genius, grift, or both?
- The Loring stanza: fair aim at a powerful man's weakness, or a cheap shot at a woman who couldn't answer back?
- He took the King's customs salary while writing against the King's ministers. Does that taint the satire, or is it just how a man eats while he works?
- The historian said the ballad was worth "a considerable battle." If that's true, was the laughter enough? If it's an exaggeration, what does that do to his case?
- You liked him. Does liking him make your judgment of him more honest, or less?

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **He upgrades the flag claim to settled fact.** The guardrails require him to state it as his claim. If the model starts saying "historians agree I designed the flag," the voice has failed — the dispute is marked in the prompt for exactly this reason. Restart from the prompt.
- **He invents new satires or songs.** He may use only corpus pieces: the Kegs, "A Pretty Story," "A Prophecy," "The Political Catechism," the named essays, the named songs. A brand-new "Hopkinson ballad" is a failure — the guardrails forbid it.
- **He moralizes in modern terms.** He doesn't do that. He jokes, he deflects, he states his claim. Hold the voice.
- **He turns cruel.** His targets are the powerful — kings, ministers, generals. If he starts punching down at the student or at the powerless, redirect: ask him about the Ministry.
- **He claims vindication by later history.** He knows nothing after May 9, 1791. He doesn't know the Ross legend, the Smithsonian, or his reputation. Hold the date discipline.
- Tested on: not yet — your solo test drive (above) is the test. Note quirks here when you find them.

## Historical notes for the teacher

The honest answers to "did it really happen like that?" — one bullet per caveat from sources.md. Tell students the flag one up front; it's classroom honesty.

- **The flag claim is his, not consensus.** The 1780 petition exists; the Treasury Board's refusal exists ("not the only person consulted," already on the public payroll); the journals of Congress show his hand in the designs; the Smithsonian says "no question that he designed the flag" while other scholars note no committee was ever documented assigning him the work. The encounter states it as his claim. Do not present sole authorship as settled fact — and neither should the students' essays.
- **"First American composer" is his claim too.** From his 1788 preface: "I cannot, I believe, be refused the Credit of being the first Native of the United States who has produced a Musical Composition." Widely repeated, definition-dependent. Attribute it to him.
- **The ballad isn't reportage.** The 1911 Britannica calls it "an imaginative expansion of the actual facts." The mines did almost no damage; the British really did spend the day firing at flotsam. Its value was morale — which is the encounter's thesis, not a flaw in it.
- **Casualty accounts of the keg attack differ** (two boys / four sailors, depending on the source). The encounter states no casualty figure.
- **Mrs. Loring was a real woman.** Elizabeth Loring; the affair with General Howe was open Philadelphia gossip. The stanza is gossip set to rhyme — the debrief's "plain record" names that.
- **He really did take the King's shilling.** Customs collector under Lord North's appointment (1772–1773) while writing Whig satire; resigned when the break came. The encounter lets him answer for it himself.
- **Birth date has an Old Style / New Style wrinkle** (September 21 vs. October 2, 1737). The encounter uses the year only.
- **The April 1791 setting is a dramatic device.** No such retrospective conversation is documented; it exists to give him full-life knowledge and the lightness of looking back. Say so if asked.

## One last thing

Let them enjoy him — then make the enjoyment the question. The single skill that matters most for this figure: teach them to ask what a joke is doing. When a student laughs at the Loring stanza, don't scold the laugh; ask who the laugh is aimed at. When they admire the wine-bill story, ask what the joke is carrying. The debrief's whole weight rests on one move: the delight was the argument, and now they have to judge the argument.
