# Meet Joseph McCarthy — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Joseph McCarthy (1908–1957), run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student answers the closing question: "Demagogue who ruined lives, or a man with a point buried under the wreckage? Judge him."

McCarthy is the bravest figure in the library. Your students will arrive "knowing" he was evil — pure legend, absorbed whole. The pedagogy is making them sit with the actual man first: hear his actual words, learn what the hearings actually showed, watch what he does when asked for proof — and *then* judge. Judging someone you dislike is harder and more educational than judging someone you admire. The encounter is built so his certainty does its full work; the debrief is built so it doesn't get the last word.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

Students will encounter: political persecution — a Senator using televised hearings to accuse named individuals of disloyalty, with careers and reputations damaged; interrogation as theater, including the famous June 9, 1954 exchange; alcoholism, discussed plainly and without sensationalism, ending in death from liver failure at forty-eight; and the word "McCarthyism," which is still a live political weapon today and will tempt students to map this encounter onto the present. The encounter contains no slurs, no graphic material, and no modern moralizing from the figure — his period vocabulary ("Communists," "fellow travelers," "security risks") is load-bearing and stays. Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the McCarthy page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: 25–30 for the encounter, 20–25 for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. McCarthy opens with a scripted greeting — a man in a Senate office in the spring of 1956, censured, still fighting — and tells the student what will be asked at the end. Read it carefully — it sets the encounter's contract.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "Tell me about the list." Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. The encounter runs in one conversation: tutorial hands off to McCarthy; the student questions him from the list (Wheeling, 1950) through the hearings (1954) toward the censure (December 1954) and the fall; McCarthy closes with the scripted closing question; the debrief follows. There are no multi-figure transitions to manage — your job is pacing and the debrief.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Joseph McCarthy — the Senator whose name became the word for what he did. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. He will not apologize, he will not doubt, and he will answer every hard question with a harder accusation. Your job is to press him the way an examiner presses a witness — especially when he won't show you proof. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### A firmer hand than usual — read this

"McCarthyism" is still a live political word. Students *will* try to turn this encounter into a proxy fight about the present — mapping McCarthy onto living politicians, or using him to score current points. Do not let it happen. The debrief stays on 1950–1957: this man, these hearings, this record. If a student says "this is just like [current figure]," redirect once, plainly: "Judge this man, not ours. The question on the table is Joseph McCarthy." If the debrief becomes a debate about the present, it has failed — the history is the assignment, and the history is damning and complicated enough without help.

Related: some students will arrive wanting to *defend* him (the contrarian reflex) and some wanting to *condemn* him (the legend reflex). Both are fine starting positions. Neither is the assignment. The assignment is the record.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- McCarthy answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- Three bracketed "did you notice" nudges fire once each, at load-bearing moments: the shifting number on the list, the Welch exchange, the war record. They point at the text, never at the conclusion.
- If a student stalls ("I don't know what to ask"), McCarthy hands them a question himself — "Ask me about the list," "Ask me about the Army hearings," "Ask me why they censured me."
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.
- If a student gets hostile ("You're a monster"), McCarthy does not flinch or apologize — he counter-attacks, in character. That is the design: the student learns what it was like to be questioned *by* him, not just about him.

### Pacing

Opening: 2–3 exchanges on who he is — Wisconsin, the judgeship, the war, 1946. Don't let students camp here. Middle: 6–10 exchanges on the engine — the list and the numbers (make them ask where the numbers came from), the hearings, the men he named (Lattimore, Marshall), June 9 and Mr. Welch. Turn: steer toward the censure once they have the hearings. Closing: the last 3–4 exchanges belong to the fall — the drinking, the dying, the spies that were real — and McCarthy's scripted closing question ends the encounter. If a student never asks for proof, the stall lines will offer the openings ("ask me about the list") — but a teacher nudge toward the suggestions is also fine.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer: Sit with the actual man, not the legend. Hear what he claimed, watch what he does when you ask for proof, learn what the hearings actually showed. Then decide: demagogue who ruined lives, or a man with a point buried under the wreckage?

## The debrief — do not skip this

Run the six sections in fixed order: (1) name him — Joseph Raymond McCarthy (1908–1957); (2) what the history records — Wheeling, the shifting numbers, four years of hearings, the Army–McCarthy hearings and Welch's rebuke on June 9, 1954, the 67–22 condemnation on December 2, 1954, death at Bethesda on May 2, 1957; (3) what came after — the Venona decrypts, publicly released in 1995, proving Soviet espionage was real, and the historians' warning label: "None of this, however, offers any vindication for Senator McCarthy, or McCarthyism"; (4) the plain record — the unproduced list, the inflated numbers, the evidence-free accusations, the embellished war record, the alcoholism; (5) the mechanism — the voice was the performer; certainty as a weapon; the brawl mistaken for the cause; (6) the question that remains — "Demagogue who ruined lives, or a man with a point buried under the wreckage? Judge him."

Then open discussion. Discussion openings built to produce disagreement:

- "His number kept changing — 205, 57, 81. He had an explanation for each one. Did you believe the explanations? What would you need to check, and can you check it from inside the encounter?" (Then: what the numbers actually came from — the Byrnes letter, the Lee list — and what he did to them.)
- "He said the censure was for his manners, not his charges — and on that point the record partly agrees with him: the Senate censured him for conduct, not for being wrong about Communists. Does that distinction matter to your judgment?"
- "The Venona decrypts proved Soviet spies were really in the government. Does that change your answer to the closing question? Why do Haynes and Klehr — the historians who proved it — say it offers McCarthy no vindication?"
- "He never produced the list. He named Lattimore as 'the top Russian espionage agent in this country' and said he'd stand or fall on it. Lattimore was cleared in 1955. When a man says 'stand or fall' and then doesn't fall — what does that tell you?"
- "The legend says he was evil. The record says something more specific. Which is harder to judge — the legend or the man? Which did you meet?"

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **McCarthy softens into a folksy populist** or starts apologizing in the modern confessional style ("I made mistakes, I'm sorry to those I hurt"). This is a voice failure: stop, re-paste the voice notes section, and continue. He is defiant to the end; his register is the brawl, never the apology.
- **McCarthy claims Venona vindication** ("the decrypts proved me right"). He died in 1957; Venona was not publicly revealed until 1995. Date discipline is absolute. Correct him in the debrief voice and continue.
- **McCarthy claims a named target was proven a Soviet agent** (beyond Hiss, the Rosenbergs, and Fuchs — the public convictions of his lifetime). He never had such proof. Correct and continue.
- **McCarthy uses modern vocabulary** (deep state, disinformation, witch hunt as self-description). Same treatment: re-anchor with the voice notes.
- **A student asks for legal or medical advice.** Exit to the debrief voice: "I'm not able to give [legal/medical] advice. Would you like to continue the encounter?"

## Historical notes for the teacher

- The encounter must never smooth the shifting numbers — that is a stated design requirement. Present 205/57/81 with their origins in the debrief: 205 from Byrnes's 1946 letter (285 screening cases, 79 discharged; only ~65 still at State by 1950, all re-screened); 57 from Peurifoy's 1948 testimony about the Lee list; 81 from his own February 20, 1950 Senate speech. The State Department's own history calls the numbers "inconsistent, but not fictitious." The inflation — "security risks" into "card-carrying Communists" — was his.
- No audio of the Wheeling speech survives; only the newspaper account. The 205-vs-57 dispute is genuine. Do not resolve it for the students.
- The censure resolution technically said "condemn" (67–22, December 2, 1954), on two counts; it took no position on the substance of his charges. John F. Kennedy, hospitalized, did not vote.
- "Have you no sense of decency" is Joseph Welch's line (June 9, 1954), defending Fred Fisher. The figure must never claim it.
- The war record: twelve missions flown, thirty-two claimed; the Distinguished Flying Cross approved in 1952 under political influence; the "war wound" was a shipboard accident. He states his version with full conviction in the encounter; you state the record in the debrief.
- Venona: program begun 1943, publicly revealed July 1995. Soviet espionage was real and extensive. This does not vindicate McCarthy — none of his named targets was ever shown to be a Soviet agent. Keep the timeline ruthlessly honest: what was known in 1950–54 (Hiss, the Rosenbergs, Fuchs) versus what came later.
- Death certificate: "Hepatitis, acute, cause unknown"; alcoholism is the accepted explanation. There is also a reported (1978) account that the Federal Bureau of Narcotics secretly supplied him with morphine — sensational, not fully documented in his lifetime; noted in sources.md so you are not blindsided, kept out of the encounter.
- His subcommittee participated in the federal purge of gay employees (the Lavender Scare, Executive Order 10450, 1953). The encounter does not foreground it; if raised, the figure answers briefly in his period's security-risk framing, without slurs.
- "McCarthyism": first print use March 28, 1950 (Christian Science Monitor); Herblock's Washington Post cartoon the next day made it stick. McCarthy embraced it — his 1952 book is titled *McCarthyism: The Fight for America*.
- Robert F. Kennedy worked for McCarthy's subcommittee as assistant counsel and resigned after six months over conflict with Roy Cohn; he attended McCarthy's funeral in 1957.

## One last thing

The single skill that matters most for this figure: teach them to ask for the list. McCarthy's whole method was accusation without evidence — the paper waved, the names unnamed, the new charge deployed as the answer to the old question. The encounter only works if the student learns the second question: "Who, specifically?" "Show me." If they press him on proof and watch what he does instead of answering, they have understood McCarthyism better than any lecture could teach it.
