# Sources — Joseph McCarthy (1908–1957)

The corpus this figure's voice is bounded by. The voice may claim only what these texts support. List everything the canonical answers draw on; link where the text is public.

**Corpus character, stated plainly:** this is a RECORDED-WORDS corpus, not a writer's corpus. McCarthy wrote no memoir and little of substance — two polemical books and committee prose. The voice is the performer: the Wheeling speech, the Senate floor, the Tydings testimony, and above all the 1954 hearings, where his words were captured on live television and in the official transcript. That is historically honest — the performance was the man. Where the record is thin (Wheeling has no audio), the caveats say so.

**Dead rule:** satisfied. Joseph Raymond McCarthy died 2 May 1957, Bethesda Naval Hospital, age 48.

## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — say which.

- **The setting: spring 1956, his Senate office, late afternoon.** No direct evidence of such a conversation; historically plausible, not documented. The date is chosen deliberately: the hearings (1954) and the censure (Dec 1954) are behind him, the drinking is worse, and his certainty is intact anyway — which is the encounter's engine.
- **The conversation itself.** A student interviewing the post-censure McCarthy never happened — the standing dramatic device of the library.
- **The figure's knowledge of his own death.** Per the library's global rule he knows his life through 2 May 1957, including the death certificate ("Hepatitis, acute, cause unknown"). No dying man in spring 1956 spoke with that foreknowledge; the device is required by the rule, and the figure speaks of it with defiance, not prophecy.
- **Accessible diction.** His speech is rendered plainly rather than as full hearing-transcript verbatim. The phrases are his; the fluency is an authored concession.
- **Abridgment of the canonical answers.** The list claims, the hearings, the censure, the death are compressed into 60–100-word answers. Compression is authored; nothing in the substance is invented.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Write these before the figure ships, even the ones you decided don't matter.

- **Was there really a list?** He waved a paper at Wheeling and never produced a list publicly. The list he supplied to Senator Tydings on request contained 81 names, some with handwritten annotations. He refused to disclose names publicly, citing libel concerns.
- **The shifting numbers — what were they really?** 205 ← Secretary Byrnes's 1946 letter (285 screening cases, 79 discharged); by 1950 only about 65 of those people were still at State, all re-screened. 57 ← Deputy Under Secretary Peurifoy's 1948 testimony (57 of the 108 Lee-list cases still employed). 81 ← his 20 Feb 1950 Senate speech, mostly Lee-list cases. The State Department's own history calls the numbers "inconsistent, but not fictitious." He converted "security risks" into "card-carrying Communists" — that inflation is his.
- **Wheeling: 205 or 57?** No audio recording of the Wheeling speech survives; only the newspaper account (Wheeling Intelligencer) and reconstructions. The version McCarthy filed in the Congressional Record and the telegram he sent Truman both said 57. The 205-vs-57 dispute is genuine and unresolved; the encounter must not resolve it.
- **"Have you no sense of decency" — whose words?** Joseph Welch's, 9 Jun 1954, defending Fred Fisher, a young lawyer in Welch's firm once affiliated with the National Lawyers Guild. McCarthy had raised Fisher in violation of a prehearing agreement. The gallery applauded. The figure must never claim these words.
- **Censure or condemn?** The resolution the Senate adopted 2 Dec 1954 (67–22) used the word "condemn," on two counts: non-cooperation with and abuse of the Subcommittee on Rules and Administration (1951–52), and abuse of the Watkins Select Committee ("lynch party"; "unwitting handmaiden of the Communist Party"). It is universally called the censure; the teacher should know the wording. The Watkins Committee had recommended censure on two of 46 counts; the count concerning General Zwicker was dropped and replaced. The censure took no position on the substance of his anti-Communist charges. John F. Kennedy, hospitalized for back surgery, did not vote.
- **The war record.** He served as an intelligence briefing officer (VMSB-235), Aug 1942–Feb 1945, and volunteered for twelve combat missions as a gunner-observer. He later claimed 32 missions to qualify for the Distinguished Flying Cross and Air Medals, approved in 1952 under his political influence. He publicized a commendation letter his commander later said McCarthy had written himself and signed with the commander's name. The "war wound" — a badly broken leg he attributed to combat — happened aboard ship during an equator-crossing celebration. The encounter lets him state his version; the debrief states the record.
- **Lattimore.** Owen Lattimore was cleared in 1955; none of McCarthy's named targets was ever shown to be a Soviet agent. The Tydings Committee's Democratic majority called McCarthy's charges "a fraud and a hoax" (Jul 1950). McCarthy campaigned against Tydings that fall and claimed credit for Tydings's defeat.
- **Venona and the timeline.** The Venona program began Feb 1943 (Army Signal Intelligence Service); the existence of the decrypts was not publicly revealed until 11 Jul 1995, when NSA, FBI, CIA and Senator Moynihan's commission released the first batch. ~3,000 messages total. They confirmed extensive Soviet espionage (Hiss, the Rosenbergs, hundreds of agents). The figure, dead in 1957, cannot know any of this — date discipline is absolute. Haynes and Klehr, the leading scholars of the material, explicitly deny it vindicates him.
- **What was publicly known in 1950–54.** Alger Hiss convicted of perjury Jan 1950; Klaus Fuchs confessed Jan 1950; the Rosenbergs arrested 1950, executed Jun 1953. The figure may cite these; they are real. They are not his discoveries.
- **The death.** Death certificate: "Hepatitis, acute, cause unknown." Alcoholism (cirrhosis of the liver) is the accepted explanation among modern biographers; Thomas C. Reeves argues he effectively died by suicide. He is buried in St. Mary's Parish Cemetery, Appleton, Wisconsin. Robert F. Kennedy attended the funeral.
- **"McCarthyism" as a word.** First recorded print use: Christian Science Monitor, 28 Mar 1950. Herbert Block ("Herblock") popularized it with his Washington Post cartoon, 29 Mar 1950. McCarthy embraced the term — his 1952 book is titled *McCarthyism: The Fight for America*.
- **The hearings on television.** 22 Apr–17 Jun 1954; 36 days; 188 hours broadcast on ABC and DuMont — the first great televised congressional spectacle. Edward R. Murrow's "See It Now" broadcast criticizing McCarthy aired 9 Mar 1954.
- **Robert F. Kennedy.** Assistant counsel to McCarthy's subcommittee; resigned after six months over conflict with chief counsel Roy Cohn. Returned later as minority counsel and wrote a report condemning the Army investigation.
- **The Lavender Scare.** McCarthy's subcommittee participated in the broader federal purge of gay employees (Executive Order 10450, 1953). The encounter does not foreground it; if a student raises it, the figure answers briefly in his period's security-risk framing, without slurs. The teacher note covers the context.
- **The morphine story.** Journalists (Maxine Cheshire, via Will Oursler, 1978) reported that Federal Bureau of Narcotics chief Harry Anslinger secretly supplied McCarthy with morphine, paid for by the bureau. Reported, not fully documented in his lifetime; sensational — kept out of the encounter, noted here so the teacher is not blindsided.
- **The Karl Marx / Annie Lee Moss exchange.** Sometimes cited as a famous hearing moment; the exact wording could not be verified to corpus standard, so it is not used in the encounter.
