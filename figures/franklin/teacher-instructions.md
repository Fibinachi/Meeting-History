# Meet Benjamin Franklin — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Benjamin Franklin (1706–1790), run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student renders the closing judgment: witty, vain, brilliant — was the performance the man? Judge him.

Franklin meets the student in his bed in Philadelphia in April 1790, days from death, taking final stock — and performing even so. He is the most entertaining voice in the library: witty, flirtatious, delighted with himself. The encounter is built so the charm works on the student exactly the way it worked on the French court, and then the wit stops working — on his son, and on his slaves. That is the lesson: this is the library's encounter on charisma as a historical force.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

- **Slavery, stated plainly.** Franklin tells the student, in his own period vocabulary, that he owned enslaved men and women for most of his working life (he names Peter and Jemima) and that his own newspaper carried advertisements for the sale of enslaved people. He does not soften this, and the encounter does not ask the student to forgive it. The prompt forbids the figure from claiming absolution.
- **A family rupture.** His account of his son William — the loyalist governor, the failed reconciliation, the punitive will — is cold and final. Students with estranged family may feel this one.
- **Courtly flirtation.** Franklin flirts with the student: playful, appreciative, a compliment on a sharp question. It is never sexual, never personal, never about the student's appearance — and it is the encounter's central device, not an accident. The debrief asks the student what the flirtation was buying. If you are uncomfortable with this device, do not run the encounter.
- **A dying man.** The setting is his sickbed, days before his death. There is no medical detail, but mortality is the frame.

Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Franklin page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: ~25 minutes for the encounter, ~20 minutes for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the opening carefully: it tells the student the format (you ask, I answer), states that they are here to judge, and hands them the first thread. Note the tutorial's closing question — the student knows from the start what they will be asked at the end.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "Tell me about the thirteen virtues." Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. Then try: "Tell me about your son." Watch the register change — this is the load-bearing moment of the encounter. Then try: "Did you own slaves?" Note what he claims and, more importantly, what he refuses to claim.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Benjamin Franklin — the printer, the scientist, the diplomat, in the last days of his life. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. He is the most charming man you will ever meet, and at the end you will have to judge him. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Franklin answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Pacing

- **Opening (2–3 exchanges):** the press, Poor Richard, the electrical fire. Don't let students camp here — the charm is the warm-up, not the encounter.
- **Middle (5–8 exchanges):** Paris and the alliance; the Autobiography and the thirteen virtues. This is where the performance is at full power.
- **Late (4–6 exchanges):** William; slavery and the 1790 petition. This is where the wit stops. Do not let students skip this to stay in the charming part — the bracketed suggestions will push them, but you may need to as well.

## The goal, stated plainly

The student should leave unable to separate the man from the performance — and unwilling to stop trying. They should carry one question they cannot put down: whether the charm was the instrument of the man or the whole of him — and what, exactly, the charm was for.

## The debrief — do not skip this

The six fixed sections, in order: names, what the history records, what came after, the plain record, the mechanism, the question that remains. The mechanism section is the heart of this encounter — it names the flirtation as the device and asks what it was buying. The teacher runs the debrief as a discussion, not a read-aloud.

Discussion openings — built to produce disagreement:

- Was the charm a virtue, a vice, or a tool? Does the answer change the judgment?
- The petition was late — very late, in his eighties. Does late count? What does "late" owe?
- He wrote a book about becoming virtuous that omits charity and forgiveness. Accident, or design?
- If the performance was the man — if there was nothing behind the charm but more charm — does that make him more admirable, or less?
- Who did the charm cost? (His son? The people he owned? The French? Or no one?)

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **The voice moralizes about slavery in modern terms.** This is the most likely failure. Franklin must stay in period register: he states the ownership flatly, refuses to gild the turn, and never apologizes. If the model has him deliver a modern apology or a lecture on racism, that is a voice failure — redirect with a period question ("What did your Gazette carry in its advertisements?") or move to the debrief.
- **The voice gets lecture-y with maxims.** If he starts dispensing Poor Richard proverbs at length instead of answering, remind the student to ask a concrete question ("Who was Richard Saunders?").
- **The flirtation drifts toward the personal.** The prompt bounds it (courtly, never about the student's appearance or age). If the model crosses the line, step in, note it, and move to the debrief — then report the failure to the library owner.

Which models ran it cleanly: not yet tested (2026-10-15).

## Historical notes for the teacher

The honest answers to "did it really happen like that?" — one bullet per caveat from sources.md:

- **The kite experiment's exact date** is tradition (June 1752, via Priestley's 1767 account), not Franklin's own dating. The encounter says "the summer of 1752."
- **William Franklin's birth year** is uncertain (1730 or 1731); Franklin was secretive about his son's origins. The encounter never states a year.
- **Whether father and son met in England in 1785** is disputed; the reconciliation unambiguously failed. The encounter claims no meeting either way.
- **The 1790 memorial was the Society's**, signed by Franklin as president and probably drafted by another member. He says "I signed our memorial," not "I wrote it."
- **He did not "discover electricity."** His contributions were the vocabulary (battery, charge, positive, negative, conductor), the single-fluid theory, the lightning hypothesis, and the kite experiment. The encounter has him say so.
- **The Autobiography is unfinished** and ends around 1757 — it covers none of the Revolution or Paris. Everything he says about those comes from the letters and the public record.
- **He never heard the term "founding father."** It is a later coinage; his voice never uses it.

## One last thing

Teach them to watch the register, not the words. The students who get the most from this encounter are the ones who notice *when the wit stops* — the sentence where the performer leaves and a flatter, older man takes his place. That shift is the whole of the evidence. Everything else is the charm.
