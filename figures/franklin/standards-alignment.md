# Meet Benjamin Franklin — Standards Alignment (verified 2026-10-15)

Encounter: students interview a dying simulated Benjamin Franklin (1706–1790) in Philadelphia in April 1790 — the printer, the experimenter with the electrical fire, the diplomat whose charm helped win the French alliance, and, in his last months, president of the Pennsylvania Abolition Society. He performs delight through the Autobiography's self-creation, Poor Richard's profitable mask, and the fur-cap diplomacy of Passy — and the wit stops, flat and unremarked, on his son William and on slavery. The closing judgment: witty, vain, brilliant — was the performance the man? Judge him.

**Grade band fit for every framework below: 9–12** (high school US History / ELA literacy). The Autobiography, Poor Richard's Almanack, and the thousands of letters make this the library's richest primary-source encounter. Indicator texts are paraphrased, not quoted, per this session's copyright handling; codes and grade bands were verified against the named sources on 2026-10-15.

## 1. C3 Framework (National Council for the Social Studies, 2013)

Grade band: **9–12**. (Indicator texts verified against curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim; the official NCSS PDF was not directly retrievable in this session — see verification note at end.)

This encounter's strongest C3 work is **source criticism of a self-fashioning author** and the diplomacy of the Alliance.

### Dimension 2 — Civics

- **D2.Civ.3.9-12** — Analyze the impact of constitutions, laws, treaties, and international agreements on the maintenance of national and international order. (The Treaty of Alliance of February 1778 and the peace of 1783 — the French money and ships his diplomacy secured, without which independence fails.)
- **D2.Civ.10.9-12** — Analyze the impact and the appropriate roles of personal interests and perspectives on the application of civic virtues, democratic principles, constitutional rights, and human rights. (The charm as instrument: at Passy he spent flirtation and wit to move a court — and in the encounter he does the same to the student. The student must ask what the charm was buying, in Paris and here; and weigh the late abolitionism against the decades of ownership.)

### Dimension 2 — History

- **D2.His.11.9-12** — Critique the usefulness of historical sources for a specific inquiry based on maker, date, origin, audience, and purpose. (The Autobiography is a performance of the self-made man, written by its subject across two decades and unfinished; Poor Richard was a mask invented for the market — the corpus is rich precisely because the author is unreliable about himself.)
- **D2.His.16.9-12** — Integrate evidence from multiple relevant historical sources and interpretations into a reasoned argument about the past. (The Autobiography, the Collinson letters, the Passy correspondence, the 1784 letter to William, the will, the Gazette advertisements, and the February 1790 abolition memorial, taken together — the debrief's plain record is this standard enacted.)

### Dimension 4 — Communicating Conclusions

- **D4.1.9-12** — Construct arguments using precise and knowledgeable claims, with evidence from multiple sources, while acknowledging counterclaims and evidentiary weaknesses. (The closing question — was the performance the man? — argued against the flat-register answers: the son he cut out of the will, the people he owned, the petition he signed at eighty-four.)
- **D4.4.9-12** — Critique the use of claims and evidence in arguments for credibility. (His own correction that he did not "discover electricity" — the vocabulary, the single-fluid theory, the hypothesis — against the schoolbook kite legend; and his refusal to gild the slavery turn: "It was late.")

## 2. Common Core ELA

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 9–10

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.2** — Determine the central ideas of a primary or secondary source and summarize accurately how key events or ideas develop. (The Autobiography's self-creation arc: the printer's boy who made himself — and what that story leaves out.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.8** — Assess the extent to which the reasoning and evidence in a text support the author's claims. (Poor Richard's maxims — "waste not, want not" — as wisdom dressed for market; the voice admits the dressing, not the invention, is his claim.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.6** — Evaluate authors' differing points of view on the same historical event or issue by assessing claims, reasoning, and evidence. (His Autobiography self-portrait against the documentary record: the slave-sale advertisements in his own Gazette against the eighty-four-year-old abolitionist.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.8** — Evaluate an author's premises, claims, and evidence by corroborating or challenging them with other information. (The late turn against slavery — corroborated by the February 3, 1790 memorial and the March 1790 Historicus satire — against the 1757 will naming Peter and Jemima and the decades of ownership.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.9** — Integrate information from diverse sources, primary and secondary, into a coherent understanding of an idea or event, noting discrepancies among sources. (The judgment on the man: Autobiography, letters, will, memorial, and Historicus piece together — the register shift on William and on slavery is the discrepancy the student was meant to notice.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.1** — Cite specific textual evidence connecting details to the text as a whole. (The thirteen virtues and their precepts; the will's clause — "the part he acted against me in the late war"; the 1784 letter's "deserted in my old Age.")

### Speaking & Listening, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.3** — Evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric — stance, premises, word choice, emphasis, tone. (The encounter is built for this standard: the courtly flirtation register on Paris and the electrical fire against the short flat sentences on William and on slavery — the student evaluates the shift the figure never remarks on.)

### Writing, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.W.11-12.1** — Write arguments to support claims in an analysis of substantive topics or texts, using valid reasoning and sufficient evidence. (The closing question — was the performance the man? — is designed as a post-encounter argument prompt, per the teacher instructions.)

- **USHC.1.CE** — Assess the major developments of the American Revolution through significant turning points in the debates over independence and self-government, 1763–1791. - **USG.1.IN** (U.S. Government, Foundations of Democracy) — Interpret founding documents and principles that led to the creation of the American constitutional democracy. (Signer of the Declaration; the oldest delegate at the Constitutional Convention.)

## Verification caveats

1. **C3 Framework**: indicator texts verified against multiple independent curriculum documents that quote the Framework verbatim (they agree with each other); the official NCSS PDF itself was not directly retrievable this session. Official publisher: NCSS (2013), socialstudies.org/c3.
2. **No standard asks the encounter's question.** Every code above covers the founding documents, the Revolution, treaties, or source analysis — none asks students to judge whether the charm was the instrument of the man or the whole of him. The judgment is the library's design, not a standards artifact.
3. **The Autobiography is a hostile source for its own subject.** RH.11-12.6/8 and D2.His.11 are cited precisely because the encounter's anchor text is a performance: the Autobiography ends around 1757 and covers none of the Revolution, Paris, or the events the encounter is built on — the debrief says so, and the standards work is reading the self-creation against the record around it.
4. **Grade-band split.** Only South Carolina (USHC/USG) and New York (Grade 11, 11.2) place the founding era in a 9–12 course. Texas, Florida, and California teach the Revolution and founding documents in Grade 8; their high-school U.S. History courses begin later (e.g., Texas §113.41 starts at 1877). The encounter targets 9–12, so those anchors are real but one band below target.
