## What is Franklin's own words vs. what is the record around him

- **Franklin's own words:** the *Autobiography*, the *Almanack* maxims, the letters in *The Papers of Benjamin Franklin* — including the 1784 letter to William, the Collinson letters on electricity, the Passy correspondence, and the Historicus satire. The voice's wit, cadence, aphoristic style, and flirtation come from these.
- **Around him, not his:** the Treaty of Alliance and Treaty of Paris (state papers, not his prose); the seconds' and Priestley's accounts of the kite experiment; Congress's reception of the 1790 memorial (the House debate, Jackson's speech); the abolition society memorial itself, which was likely drafted by another member and signed by Franklin as president. The prompt uses these for facts Franklin could state, but the voice never claims authorship it doesn't have — it says "I signed our memorial," not "I wrote it."
- **Notably thin in Franklin's own corpus:** he gives no single account of a conversion on slavery — the prompt scripts that absence as a refusal to invent one, not as a filled gap. He wrote almost nothing about William in the *Autobiography* — that silence is marked, not filled.

## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — say which.

- **The meeting itself (a single sitting, April 1790, in his bed in Philadelphia)** — pure dramatic device. Franklin was genuinely dying that month (died April 17, 1790); no such interview occurred.
- **Compression of a lifetime into an hour** — the encounter has him speak on events sixty years apart as present-tense memory. Dramatic compression.
- **The flirtation directed at the student** — the courtly, flirtatious register is modeled on his actual correspondence with women friends (e.g., the letters to Madame Brillon); directing it at the student is an authored extension. It is the encounter's central device, and the debrief names it as such.
- **The scripted canonical answers' phrasing** — the facts and boundaries are documented; the sentences themselves are authored. The "deserted in my old Age" passage and the thirteen virtues' precepts are his words, quoted closely.
- **The setting's dying-man framing** — he was in genuine physical decline (pleurisy, gout, the stone are the period terms); the encounter stages its final-reckoning tone on that documented decline.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Written before the figure ships, even the ones that don't matter.

- **When was the kite experiment?** The common date is June 1752, but that comes via Joseph Priestley's 1767 *History of Electricity*, not from Franklin's own dating. Franklin's October 1752 letter to Collinson describes the kite proposal; whether and exactly when he performed it rests on Priestley's account of what Franklin told him. The prompt says "the summer of 1752" and does not claim a day.
- **William Franklin's birth year is uncertain.** Sources give 1730 (Wikipedia: February 22, 1730) or 1731; Franklin himself was secretive about his son's origins. The prompt never states a year.
- **Did father and son meet in England in 1785?** Some accounts report a brief meeting when Franklin landed in England on his return from France; others hold they never met again after 1776. The reconciliation unambiguously failed — the letters show it and the will confirms it. The prompt claims no meeting either way; it says only "we never became father and son again."
- **Who wrote the 1790 memorial?** It was the Society's memorial, signed by Franklin as president; scholarship holds it was probably drafted by another member. The voice says "I signed our memorial" — it never claims sole authorship.
- **He did not "discover electricity."** Electrical phenomena were known before him; his contributions were the vocabulary (battery, charge, positive, negative, conductor), the single-fluid theory, the lightning hypothesis, and the kite experiment. The prompt has him correct the record himself.
- **The Poor Richard maxims were not all original.** Many were adapted from existing English and French proverbs. The voice calls them "mine, dressed for market" — the dressing, not the invention, is the claim.
- **He never heard the term "founding father."** It is a later coinage. The prompt forbids the phrase in his voice.
- **The Autobiography is unfinished and ends around 1757** — it covers none of the Revolution, Paris, or the events the encounter is built on. Everything he says about those comes from the letters and the public record, and the debrief says so.
