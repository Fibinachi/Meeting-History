# Meet Adams — Standards Alignment (verified 2026-10-15)

Encounter: students press a simulated John Adams (1735–1826) at Peacefield in early June 1826 — the man who pushed the colonies into independence, signed the Alien and Sedition Acts, packed the courts on his last night in office, and spent twenty-five years explaining himself in 158 letters to Thomas Jefferson. They examine the Defence of the Constitutions, the Massachusetts constitution of 1780, the "continued lye" letter to Rush, the diary confession of ambition at twenty-five, and his own account of the Declaration's drafting (which Jefferson's notes dispute). The closing judgment: principled, or just ambitious? Judge him.

**Grade band fit for every framework below: 9–12** (high school US History / Government / ELA literacy). The Adams-Jefferson correspondence (1812–1826) is the spine of the encounter and one of the standard RH.11-12.8/9 text sets in American classrooms. Indicator texts are paraphrased, not quoted, per this session's copyright handling; codes and grade bands were verified against the named sources on 2026-10-15.

## 1. C3 Framework (National Council for the Social Studies, 2013)

Grade band: **9–12**. (Indicator texts verified against curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim; the official NCSS PDF was not directly retrievable in this session — see verification note at end.)

This encounter is one of the library's strongest **civics** fits: balanced government, contested constitutional powers, and the citizen's own self-interest under examination.

### Dimension 2 — Civics

- **D2.Civ.4.9-12** — Explain how the U.S. Constitution establishes a system of government with powers, responsibilities, and limits that have changed over time and are still contested. (The Alien and Sedition Acts — laws he signed that criminalized criticism of the government and were used to jail his opponents' editors and a sitting congressman — and the midnight judges: contested constitutional power exercised at the edge of his term.)
- **D2.Civ.10.9-12** — Analyze the impact and the appropriate roles of personal interests and perspectives on the application of civic virtues, democratic principles, constitutional rights, and human rights. (The encounter's load-bearing move: the diary at twenty-five confesses that reputation ought to be the perpetual subject of his thoughts — the student measures his constitutional theory against his admitted ambition.)
- **D2.Civ.8.9-12** — Evaluate social and political systems in different contexts, times, and places, that promote civic virtues and enact democratic principles. (His Defence argues for balanced government against simple democracy, which he feared; the Sedition prosecutions measure the 1798 republic against the principles it claimed.)

### Dimension 2 — History

- **D2.His.11.9-12** — Critique the usefulness of historical sources for a specific inquiry based on maker, date, origin, audience, and purpose. (His Autobiography's three reasons for yielding the Declaration draft to Jefferson — written decades later — against Jefferson's 1823 notes taken at the time; the 158 late letters, written in old age to a reconciled rival, as self-explanation.)
- **D2.His.16.9-12** — Integrate evidence from multiple relevant historical sources and interpretations into a reasoned argument about the past. (The Declaration draft and adopted text, the Autobiography, the Diary, Novanglus, the Defence, the Massachusetts constitution, and the Adams-Jefferson letters, taken together.)

### Dimension 4 — Communicating Conclusions

- **D4.1.9-12** — Construct arguments using precise and knowledgeable claims, with evidence from multiple sources, while acknowledging counterclaims and evidentiary weaknesses. (The closing question — principled or just ambitious — is designed as a post-encounter argument, with his own confessions as both evidence and counterclaim.)
- **D4.4.9-12** — Critique the use of claims and evidence in arguments for credibility. (His confessional device — "I am obnoxious, suspected, and unpopular" in his own hand — must be evaluated as evidence: is the telling the honesty, or the cleverest thing the vanity ever did?)

*Verified against: judiciallearningcenter.org C3-Framework-250.pdf; aedownload.net Teachers Discovery C3 alignment; heritage-series C3 indicator table; gilderlehrman.org and womenshistory.org lesson-plan C3 citations. Official document: College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework for Social Studies State Standards, NCSS, 2013 (socialstudies.org/c3).*

## 2. Common Core ELA

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 9–10

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.6** — Compare the point of view of two or more authors on the same or similar topics, noting which details each includes and emphasizes. (Adams's account of yielding the Declaration draft — "you can write ten times better than I can" — against Jefferson's notes, which remember the committee's assignment differently.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.9** — Compare and contrast treatments of the same topic in several primary and secondary sources. (The 1798 crisis as Adams states it — French bribes demanded of envoys, a real war scare — against the Sedition Act prosecutions' record: Republican editors, Congressman Lyon re-elected from his cell.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.6** — Evaluate authors' differing points of view on the same historical event or issue by assessing claims, reasoning, and evidence. (The drafting disagreement: two founders, two memories, forty-seven years on; the figure marks the disagreement and the student evaluates it.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.8** — Evaluate an author's premises, claims, and evidence by corroborating or challenging them with other information. (His Sedition Act account — "I did not ask for the laws, and I did not oppose them" — corroborated against the prosecution record; his "never in my life owned a slave" against the 1801 and 1819 letters.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.9** — Integrate information from diverse sources, primary and secondary, into a coherent understanding of an idea or event, noting discrepancies among sources. (The Adams-Jefferson letters as the spine: 158 letters across fourteen years, four to Jefferson's one, reconciling rival memories of the same Revolution — this standard is the letters' classroom function.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.1** — Cite specific textual evidence connecting details to the text as a whole. (The three reasons in the Autobiography; "the Revolution was effected before the war commenced" in the 1818 Niles letter; "the most insignificant office" in the 1793 letter to Abigail.)

### Speaking & Listening, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.3** — Evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric — stance, premises, word choice, emphasis, tone. (The encounter's whole method: he confesses before the student accuses, and the student must evaluate the confession as rhetoric — the Ciceronian periods, the Latin tags, the vanity named in the same paragraph as the complaint.)

### Writing, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.W.11-12.1** — Write arguments to support claims in an analysis of substantive topics or texts, using valid reasoning and sufficient evidence. (The closing question — principled, or just ambitious? — is designed as a post-encounter argument prompt, per the teacher instructions.)

## 3. South Carolina — Social Studies College- and Career-Ready Standards (2019)

Grade band: **9–12** (USHC and USG are high-school courses).

- **USHC.1.CE** — Assess the major developments of the American Revolution through significant turning points in the debates over independence and self-government, 1763–1791. (He pressed the Second Continental Congress to declare independence and carried the Declaration's vote on the floor — the state guidance for this indicator explicitly names the Declaration among the founding documents in scope.)
- **USHC.1.CC** — Analyze the processes of continuity and change in the debates over the proper role of the central government and neutrality in foreign affairs, 1789–1815. (His presidency is the textbook case: the XYZ crisis and undeclared war with France, the Alien and Sedition Acts, the midnight judges — central power and neutrality in one administration.)
- **USHC.1.P** — Summarize the changing relationship between individuals and the government during 1607–1800. (Novanglus's "a government of laws, and not of men," carried into Article XXX of the Massachusetts constitution of 1780, which he drafted.)
- **USG.1.ER** (U.S. Government, Foundations of Democracy) — Analyze the philosophical influences on core political principles in the American constitutional democracy. (The Defence of the Constitutions: balanced government, the senate as "ostracism" of the rich and well-born, checks against rivalries.)
- **USG.1.CC** — Analyze the major debates and compromises underlying the formation and ratification of the Constitution. (He wrote the Defence in London while the Convention sat in Philadelphia — to answer critics of the state constitutions' mixture — and was called a monarchist for it.)
- **USG.1.IN** — Interpret founding documents and principles that led to the creation of the American constitutional democracy. (The Declaration he shepherded, the Massachusetts constitution he drafted, the Judiciary Act commissions he signed to the last hour.)

*Verified against: the 2019 SC standards indicator texts via the scprt.widen.net StateHouse Tour standards PDF, the IXL USHC skill plan, the ed.sc.gov EOCEP USHC performance-level-descriptor guide, the CCSD 2024–2025 U.S. History Honors Curriculum Guide (core-docs.s3.us-east-1.amazonaws.com), the CCSD 2024–2025 U.S. Government Curriculum Guide (thrillshare), and study.com's USG skills page; the jefferson wave-1 file verified against the official ed.sc.gov printable 2019 standards.*

## 4. Other states (quick hits)

### New York (K–12 Social Studies Framework, Grade 11)

- **11.2b** — Students examine the purpose of and ideas contained in the Declaration of Independence and consider its long-term impacts. (Key idea **11.2 CONSTITUTIONAL FOUNDATIONS (1763–1824)**: colonists declare independence, then build a stable federal republic. His floor fight for the Declaration and his own disputed memory of the drafting sit here.)
- **11.2c** — Students explore the development of the Constitution, including the major debates and their resolutions — compromises over representation, taxation, and slavery. (The Defence's balanced-government doctrine; the Convention debates he watched from London and wrote to shape.)
- *Context:* NY Grade 7 key idea **7.3** (AMERICAN INDEPENDENCE) covers the Revolution itself — grade-band caveat: 11.2 is the on-band high-school anchor.

## Verification caveats

1. **C3 Framework**: indicator texts verified against multiple independent curriculum documents that quote the Framework verbatim (they agree with each other); the official NCSS PDF itself was not directly retrievable this session. Official publisher: NCSS (2013), socialstudies.org/c3.
2. **No standard asks the encounter's question.** Every code above covers the founding documents, constitutional theory, the Revolution, or presidential history — none asks students to judge a founder's principles against his admitted ambition. The judgment is the library's design, not a standards artifact.
3. **Texas §113.44 is the on-band anchor.** It names John Adams explicitly in a Grade 9–12 course — the strongest high-school alignment in this set. The §113.20 Grade 8 anchors are one band below target.
4. **The drafting disagreement is a standards feature, not a bug.** RH.9-10.6, RH.11-12.6, and D2.His.11 are cited precisely because Adams's and Jefferson's accounts of the same event differ — the standards work is the evaluation, and the encounter marks the disagreement rather than resolving it.
5. **No proposed/draft standard cited.** The 2026 Texas SBOE draft TEKS proposals were found during research and excluded; only adopted TEKS appear above.
