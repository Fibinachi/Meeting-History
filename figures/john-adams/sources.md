## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — say which.

- **The June-1826 Peacefield conversation itself** — no such encounter is documented. His age (90), failing eyesight, and habit of dictating letters in his final years are documented; the conversation is a pure dramatic device.
- **His awareness of nearness to death** — documented in his 1826 letters (failing strength, the April 1826 letter). He does NOT know he will die on July 4, or that Jefferson will die the same day hours earlier; that knowledge is withheld from him by date discipline.
- **The compression of his self-accounting** — in life these confessions span sixty years of letters; the encounter lets him make them in one conversation. The words are his; the occasion is invented.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Written before the figure ships, even the ones that don't matter.

- **"Thomas Jefferson survives"** — his reported last words. Family recollection, not a documented utterance; Jefferson had died at Monticello hours earlier. The encounter does not use it (date discipline); the debrief reports it as recollection.
- **The "proudest act of my life" Marshall remark** — widely quoted, sourced via Charles Warren's *The Supreme Court in United States History* as said to Marshall's son about twenty-five years after the appointment (i.e., c. 1826). It is an attributed recollection, not a letter. The prompt marks the attribution; the figure may speak it as his own settled sentiment, which the late letters support.
- **The Declaration-drafting exchange** — Adams's account (Autobiography; letter to Timothy Pickering, 1822) and Jefferson's account (letter to James Madison, 1823, from notes taken at the time) differ on the details. The figure gives Adams's version and marks the disagreement; it does not claim Jefferson agrees.
- **"Atlas of American Independence"** — not Adams's phrase. Richard Stockton's son recalled his father calling Adams this; Jefferson called him "the pillar of [the resolution's] support" (1813) and reportedly "our Colossus on the floor" (1824). The figure may repeat others' words attributed, never as his own coinage.
- **"I regret not the repeal of the Alien or Sedition Law, which were never favorites with me"** — Adams to his son in later life (reported). The exact letter and date were not verified in research; the sentiment is consistent with his late correspondence. The figure uses it as his own late judgment, not as a contemporary 1798 statement.
- **Sedition Act prosecutions** — commonly cited as at least 25 arrested and about 10 convicted; figures vary by source. The prompt uses approximate language ("some two dozen," "about ten") deliberately.
- **The Alien Friends Act** — gave the president power to deport aliens deemed dangerous; Adams never used the deportation power. The Sedition Act expired March 3, 1801, the last day of his term; the Alien Friends Act had already expired in 1800.
- **Abigail–Jefferson 1804 exchange** — after the death of Jefferson's daughter Polly, Abigail wrote Jefferson (May 1804); the exchange soured over Jefferson's patronage of James Callender, and the families did not reconcile until 1812. The figure may refer to the breach in general terms; the details belong to the teacher's background, not the encounter.
- **"More than a thousand letters"** — the John–Abigail correspondence (1762–1801) is commonly cited at 1,100+ letters. The prompt says "more than a thousand" to stay inside the safe range.
- **Adams and slavery** — he never owned slaves (his 1801 and 1819 letters). He kept the slavery question out of national politics where he could; the encounter states this as his own prudence and lets the student name it. He did not free anyone because he held no one.
- **The vice-presidency quote** — "the most insignificant office..." is from his letter to Abigail, 19 December 1793. Genuine.
- **The White House prayer** — "I pray Heaven to bestow the best of blessings on this House..." is from his letter to Abigail, 2 November 1800, his second night in the President's House. Genuine; it is carved on the State Dining Room mantel.
- **"Two pieces of homespun"** — Adams's 1 January 1812 letter to Jefferson describes the gift as "two Pieces of Homespun lately produced in this quarter." Scholarly accounts (NYRB; Blake) identify them as the two volumes of John Quincy Adams's *Lectures on Rhetoric and Oratory* (1810); one educational source describes them as transcripts of Harvard speeches. The prompt uses the *Lectures* identification, following the scholarly accounts.
- **Birth date** — 30 October 1735 (New Style), Braintree, Massachusetts. Death: 4 July 1826, Quincy.
