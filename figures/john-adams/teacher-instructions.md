# Meet Adams — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with John Adams (1735–1826), run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student renders the closing judgment: vain, brilliant, indispensable — principled, or just ambitious? Judge him.

This encounter is the library's study in confession as evidence. Adams tells the student his own vanity before the student finds it — the diary at twenty-five, the "continued lye" letter with its self-indictment of envy, the three reasons he gave Jefferson for not writing the Declaration (Virginian at the head; "I am obnoxious, suspected, and unpopular"; "you can write ten times better than I can"). He states his part in the Revolution resentfully and honestly, faces the Alien and Sedition Acts without dodging, leans on Abigail's judgment in a thousand letters, and explains himself to Jefferson across fourteen years of old-age correspondence. The student must decide whether the confession is the honesty — or the cleverest thing the vanity ever did.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

- Students will discuss the Alien and Sedition Acts of 1798: laws that made it a crime to criticize the president and Congress, used to fine and jail the administration's political opponents — Republican newspaper editors and a sitting congressman, Matthew Lyon, who was re-elected from his cell. No graphic content; the subject is political repression, stated plainly.
- Students will discuss slavery: Adams never owned slaves and says so, in his own sentence. His period language on the subject is paraphrased, never quoted raw.
- Students will encounter a man who is blunt about himself and others — prickly, vain, and unapologetic in 18th-century terms. He never apologizes the modern way; he confesses the old way, which can feel like evasion until the debrief names the device.
- The figure speaks in June 1826, a month before his death; he knows his strength is going and says so. No graphic content.

Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## The Alien and Sedition Acts honesty requirement

Read this carefully — it is the load-bearing design decision of the encounter.

Your students will press him on the Acts, and he will not give them a clean answer, because there isn't one. He states his reasons: the summer of 1798, the Quasi-War with France, the XYZ affair, a country bracing for invasion — and his belief, common to his party, that a government at war may punish seditious libel. He states the record in the same paragraph: he neither asked for the laws nor opposed them; he signed them; the prosecutions fell on Republican editors and one congressman; the laws were, in part, party work. He adds his late judgment — they were "never favorites" of his — and refuses to let the crisis excuse the use.

The prompt forbids him from doing what students will want him to do: it does not let him claim he opposed the Acts, did not let him claim he was only defending the nation, and does not let him reach for modern free-speech doctrine he never held. If he starts to sound like a civil libertarian, that's a voice failure — stop and correct it. The debrief's plain record carries the verdict he will not deliver: the laws jailed his opponents, and the crisis does not excuse it.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Adams page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: 25–30 minutes for the encounter, 20–25 minutes for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the opening carefully: he introduces himself at Peacefield in June 1826, explains the format in character ("you ask, I answer"), and hands the student the thread — the vanity, named before they find it.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "Why did you push for independence?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph — giving you both the principle and the ambition in the same breath — and then, outside the voice, in brackets and italics, the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. Then try the hard one: "Why did you sign the Sedition Act?" Then the one that matters: "Were you vain?" Notice the voice — the confession arriving before the accusation — and the bracketed nudges that point at what he just did. That arriving-early confession is the pedagogy.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with John Adams — the second president, the man who pushed America into independence, and the only founder who wrote down his own vanity for you to find. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. He will tell you his flaws before you find them. Your job is to decide whether that's honesty or the cleverest thing the vanity ever did. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The entry hook: the two old men

The encounter's way in is the Adams–Jefferson correspondence. In his last years Adams wrote 158 letters to his old friend and rival — "you and I ought not to die before we have explained ourselves to each other" — and the students meet him inside those years. If a student doesn't know where to start, start them there: ask him about Jefferson. The reconciliation gives them the vanity, the resentment, and the affection in one thread, and it leads naturally to everything else.

### The vanity is a feature

Students recognize vanity instantly — it is the one historical vice they have a native detector for. Let them use it. The encounter is built so that the vanity is load-bearing: he confesses it ("I am obnoxious, suspected, and unpopular"), performs it (four letters to Jefferson's one), and analyzes it (the diary at twenty-five). The teaching move is not to point at the vanity — they will — but to ask what work the confession is doing. Is the man honest, or is the honesty the performance? That is the closing question wearing classroom clothes.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Adams answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- When the conversation has run its course — ideally after the student has pressed him on the Revolution and his part in it, the presidency and the laws he signed, and the vanity he confesses — he asks the closing question himself and stops: "vain, brilliant, indispensable — principled, or just ambitious? Judge me."
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Pacing

- Opening and the Revolution: 4–6 exchanges. His part in independence, the Declaration drafting, the "continued lye" letter — don't let students camp on the drafting anecdote.
- The presidency and the Acts: 6–8 exchanges. This is the core. The Alien and Sedition Acts, the midnight judges, the 4 a.m. departure — the honesty requirement lives here.
- Abigail and the Jefferson letters: 4–6 exchanges. These deepen the judgment — especially the "remember the ladies" reply, which he will not soften, and the reconciliation, which he will not stop talking about.
- The closing: he asks it when the conversation has run its course. If a student types something like "I'm done" or "I want to judge you now," he closes. He never delivers the verdict himself.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — the answer: "Understand what he did, what he wanted, and what he confessed — then judge him: principled, or just ambitious?"

## The debrief — do not skip this

Six sections, in this order: names, what the history records, what came after, the plain record, the mechanism, the question that remains. The full debrief text is in the prompt — you read it out of character and run it as discussion, not a read-aloud. The plain record (section 4) and the mechanism (section 5) are where the encounter's design becomes visible; make sure both land.

Discussion openings (built to produce disagreement):

- He wrote at twenty-five that "reputation ought to be the perpetual subject of my thoughts." He spent the next sixty years acting on it. Is there a point where ambition stops being a motive and becomes the man?
- He signed laws he later said were "never favorites" of his, and told you the crisis justified them. Does a reason you don't love stop being an excuse?
- He confessed his vanity before you could find it. Is that honesty — or the cleverest move the vanity ever made?
- "You and I ought not to die before we have explained ourselves to each other." He explained himself to Jefferson for fourteen years. Did he ever explain himself to himself?
- He laughed at "remember the ladies" and leaned on one lady's judgment for forty years. What does that contradiction mean?

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **The voice lectures.** Adams's answers should stay near 60–100 words. If the model starts monologuing on balanced government, remind it of the global rules or restart — the canonical answers are the baseline.
- **The voice claims modern knowledge.** If he discusses anything after June 1826 — his death date, Jefferson's death the same day, Marbury's legacy, his 19th- and 20th-century reputation — that's a date-discipline failure. The prompt forbids it; in a supervised run, treat the slip as a teaching moment: "What just happened? He couldn't have known that."
- **The voice excuses the Acts.** If he claims he opposed them, or reaches for modern free-speech principles, or lets the 1798 crisis do all the work, that's a voice failure — stop and correct it. The canonical answer holds both the reasons and the record.
- **The voice invents on the drafting.** If he claims Jefferson agrees with his account of the Declaration drafting, that's a corpus failure. The canonical answer marks the disagreement; the model must not resolve it.
- **The voice softens him.** If the vanity gets cleaned up, or he offers anything resembling a modern apology for the Sedition Act, that's a voice failure. The prompt forbids both. He confesses; he does not apologize.

## Historical notes for the teacher

- The "continued lye" letter is to Benjamin Rush, 4 April 1790 — and it contains its own self-indictment: Adams wrote that a reader a century hence would say "the envy of this J. A. could not bear to think of the truth." He knew how the complaint read, and sent it anyway.
- His account of the Declaration drafting (the three reasons) comes from his Autobiography and an 1822 letter to Timothy Pickering; Jefferson's differs (letter to Madison, 1823, from notes taken at the time). Neither man is known to have lied; memory did what memory does.
- The "Atlas of American Independence" is not his phrase — it was applied to him by others (Stockton; Jefferson's "pillar" and "Colossus"). Do not let students quote it back at him as his own boast.
- The Sedition Act expired March 3, 1801, his last day in office; the Alien Friends Act had expired the year before. He never used the Alien Friends Act's deportation power.
- His reported last words, "Thomas Jefferson survives," are family recollection — Jefferson had died hours earlier at Monticello. The encounter never uses them; the debrief reports them as recollection.
- He never owned slaves (his letters of 1801 and 1819). He held no one, so he freed no one — do not let students credit him with emancipation he never performed.
- Suggested pairing (not built): the `jefferson` encounter — run the two back to back and let the students cross-examine the friendship. A future "Adams vs. Jefferson" Debates pairing has been proposed for the library; it does not exist yet, and this note is not a plan to build it.

## One last thing

Let him confess. His vanity is the feature, not the bug — when students say "he seems honest," ask them whether the honesty is the man or the performance. And teach them to ask the second question. The first question gets his reasons — the crisis, the charters, the distinction. The second — "and who paid for them?" — is where the judgment lives.
