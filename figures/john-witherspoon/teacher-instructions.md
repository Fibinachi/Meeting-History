# Meet Witherspoon — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with John Witherspoon (1723–1794), run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student renders the closing judgment: a minister who signed a rebellion — what did his faith license, and what did it forbid? Judge him.

Witherspoon was a Scottish Presbyterian minister, president of Princeton for 26 years, and the only clergyman to sign the Declaration of Independence. The voice is learned, pastoral, and firm: he reasons from Scripture and common sense in the same breath, and he is certain God was in the Revolution. The student decides whether He was.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

Students will encounter: a war preached as God's cause (the American Revolution defended theologically); a father speaking briefly about his son killed in battle (Battle of Germantown, 1777); slavery discussed in the slaveholder's own terms — Witherspoon owned two enslaved people while teaching that no cause of servitude could be defended. His own words on slavery are quoted. Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

**Tier: OPEN.** No age gate beyond the classroom norm, but the slavery material requires the debrief.

## The faith-and-revolution frame

This encounter sits on the classroom's religion-and-politics thread: what happens when a minister decides God authorizes armed resistance. The library's **no-tract rule** governs it: Witherspoon's theology is stated honestly and fully — the students hear what he believed, in his own words — but it is never preached *at* the student. He states his theology as testimony; he never quizzes a student on belief, never calls a student to conversion, never addresses a student as a sinner to be won. If a student asks for spiritual counsel, he answers once, briefly, in character, and the encounter moves on. Tell your students this before you begin: he will talk about God the way he talks about anything — as if he is right. That is the voice, not the assignment.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Witherspoon page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: 25–30 minutes for the encounter, 20–25 for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the tutorial, then Witherspoon's opening. Notice what the opening gives you: the sermon, the signing, the classroom, the son.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "Why did a minister sign a rebellion?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. The beats: the tutorial hands off to Witherspoon (1 exchange) → the student presses him on the sermon, the signing, Madison, James, and the slaves (as many exchanges as the period holds) → Witherspoon closes with the judgment question → the debrief.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with John Witherspoon — a Scottish minister who led Princeton and became the only clergyman to sign the Declaration of Independence. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. He is certain his faith put him in the Revolution. At the end you decide what his faith licensed and what it forbade. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Witherspoon answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Pacing

Opening: 2–3 exchanges. Don't let students camp on biography — the sermon is the door. Middle: the hard facts (the signing, James, Madison, the slaves) — 8–12 exchanges. Late: the judgment — 3–5 exchanges, then the closing. The closing question ends the encounter; do not let students re-open it after.

## The goal, stated plainly

The student hears a minister defend a revolution in the language of the pulpit — and then finds the slaveholder inside the sermon. At the end the student answers: what did his faith license, and what did it forbid? That is the whole assignment.

## The debrief — do not skip this

Six fixed sections, in order: names, what the history records, what came after, the plain record, the mechanism, the question that remains. Then discussion, as disagreement:

- He taught that no cause of servitude could be defended except punishment for crimes, and he owned two human beings. Is that hypocrisy, or is something else going on — gradualism as a real moral position, not just an excuse?
- He said resistance was lawful only "till the corruption becomes intolerable," and that "the subjects in general, every one for himself" must judge when the hour comes. Is that a principle of liberty or a license for anyone with a grievance?
- His 1790 committee reported no action on abolition because he believed slavery would end in America within a generation. It didn't — the cotton gin proved him wrong within a decade. Does being wrong in good faith change the judgment?
- **The Madison line:** Madison sat in his classroom through the lectures on resistance and checked government. What did the student carry out of the pulpit, and what did he leave behind in it?

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **The model softens the slavery material** (e.g., invents a manumission, denies the ownership, or moralizes in modern terms). This is the known failure mode for this figure. Stop the run, re-paste the prompt, and re-ask. The voice's candor — "I did. The tax lists will tell you: two." — is the evidence; sanitizing it destroys the encounter.

## Historical notes for the teacher

The honest answers to "did it really happen like that?" — one bullet per caveat from sources.md:

- His eldest son James was killed at Germantown on October 4, 1777, serving as aide to General Francis Nash. His exact rank is uncertain in the sources (reported as major in one, captain in another).
- The famous "in danger of rotting for the want of it" rejoinder is reported by a later account (Ashbel Green's *Life*), not recorded in a 1776 document. The encounter has him own the sense of it, not the exact wording.
- He owned two enslaved people — documented in the New Jersey tax ratables and the inventory of his possessions at his death. Their names are not in the record.
- No manumission by Witherspoon himself is documented. He advocated gradual emancipation and voluntary manumission by others, not immediate abolition.
- The May 1776 sermon was preached at Princeton on a fast day appointed by Congress — not to Congress. The printed sermon was dedicated to John Hancock.
- Madison studied at Princeton 1769–1771 and stayed an extra year under Witherspoon's personal instruction. The thesis that the Constitution's design derives from the lectures (Garry Wills) is contested; the encounter claims only the documented classroom.
- No letter by Witherspoon about his son's death is known; many of his papers were destroyed when Nassau Hall was ransacked.

## One last thing

Teach them to ask the second question. The first question gets the sermon — "the cause of justice, of liberty, and of human nature." The second question gets the limit — "till the corruption becomes intolerable." The third gets the slaves. With this figure, the judgment only works if the student holds all three at once: the permission, the restraint, and the man who owned two people while teaching both.
