# Meet Witherspoon — Standards Alignment (verified 2026-10-15)

Encounter: students press a simulated John Witherspoon (1723–1794) at Tusculum in the autumn of 1794 — the Scottish Presbyterian minister who became president of Princeton, preached the fast-day sermon *The Dominion of Providence over the Passions of Men* in May 1776, taught James Madison moral philosophy, and became the only clergyman to sign the Declaration of Independence. He states his theology as testimony: resistance is lawful only when corruption is intolerable; liberty and religion stand or fall together. The same man owned two enslaved people at his death and chaired the 1790 New Jersey committee that reported against immediate abolition. The closing judgment: a minister who signed a rebellion — what did his faith license, and what did it forbid? Judge him.

**Grade band fit for every framework below: 9–12** (high school US History / Government / ELA literacy). This is the encounter set's faith-and-revolution case: the alignments below center the sermon's theological case for resistance and the slaveholder's contradiction, not founder-generic document work. Indicator texts are paraphrased, not quoted, per this session's copyright handling; codes and grade bands were verified against the named sources on 2026-10-15.

## 1. C3 Framework (National Council for the Social Studies, 2013)

Grade band: **9–12**. (Indicator texts verified against curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim; the official NCSS PDF was not directly retrievable in this session — see verification note at end.)

### Dimension 2 — Civics

- **D2.Civ.8.9-12** — Evaluate social and political systems in different contexts, times, and places, that promote civic virtues and enact democratic principles. (The fast-day sermon is itself this evaluation: a minister preaching that "the cause of justice, of liberty, and of human nature" is the measure of the rebellion's legitimacy.)
- **D2.Civ.2.9-12** — Analyze the role of citizens in the U.S. political system, with attention to theories of democracy and alternative models, past and present. (His resistance doctrine puts the tribunal in the subjects' hands — "who judges when the hour is come? The subjects in general — every one for himself" — a theory of the citizen's role the student tests against the closing question.)
- **D2.Civ.4.9-12** — Explain how the U.S. Constitution establishes a system of government with powers, responsibilities, and limits that have changed over time and are still contested. (His classroom doctrine — every good government must be complex, "so that the one principle may check the other" — is what Madison sat under; the encounter traces the line from the Princeton lecture to the constitutional design.)

### Dimension 2 — History

- **D2.His.11.9-12** — Critique the usefulness of historical sources for a specific inquiry based on maker, date, origin, audience, and purpose. (The sermon was preached at Princeton on a Congressional fast day, dedicated to John Hancock, and did the traveling for him ever since; the *Lectures on Moral Philosophy* are a posthumous compilation of classroom syllabi, not a book he wrote — both sources must be read with their making in view.)
- **D2.His.16.9-12** — Integrate evidence from multiple relevant historical sources and interpretations into a reasoned argument about the past. (The sermon, the Lectures, the New Jersey tax ratables listing two slaves, and the 1790 committee report, taken together — the faith's license and its limit, argued from the whole record.)

### Dimension 4 — Communicating Conclusions

- **D4.1.9-12** — Construct arguments using precise and knowledgeable claims, with evidence from multiple sources, while acknowledging counterclaims and evidentiary weaknesses. (The closing question — what did his faith license, and what did it forbid? — is argued against the contradiction: the man who taught that no cause of servitude could be defended except punishment for crimes owned two human beings at his death.)
- **D4.4.9-12** — Critique the use of claims and evidence in arguments for credibility. (His 1790 counsel — that universal liberty was progressing and there would be no slaves in America in eight-and-twenty years — is tested against the record he could not know: the cotton gin, invented in 1793, proved him wrong within a decade.)

## 2. Common Core ELA

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 9–10

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.6** — Compare the point of view of two or more authors on the same or similar topics, noting which details each includes and emphasizes. (His sermon on resistance — lawful when corruption is intolerable, judged by the subjects — against the loyalist reading of the same Scripture (e.g., Romans 13) that the pulpit war was fought over.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.8** — Assess the extent to which the reasoning and evidence in a text support the author's claims. (His gradualism: the claim that the progress of universal liberty made immediate abolition unnecessary, resting on a prediction about the future he did not live to check.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.6** — Evaluate authors' differing points of view on the same historical event or issue by assessing claims, reasoning, and evidence. (His Lectures on slavery — "no original cause of servitude can be defended" — against his own tax lists; the sermon and the slaveholder are the same author's two irreconcilable positions.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.8** — Evaluate an author's premises, claims, and evidence by corroborating or challenging them with other information. (The 1790 committee's no-immediate-action report against the tax ratables and death inventory; the claim of baptismal and tutorial kindness to Black men against the absence of any manumission of his own.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.9** — Integrate information from diverse sources, primary and secondary, into a coherent understanding of an idea or event, noting discrepancies among sources. (The debrief's plain record: the sermon, the Lectures, the tax lists, the committee report, and the Princeton Slavery Project's 2018 documentation, taken together.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.1** — Cite specific textual evidence connecting details to the text as a whole. (The Psalm 76:10 text of the sermon; the resistance conditions — "till the corruption becomes intolerable"; the moral-sense doctrine that conscience is "the law which our Maker has written upon our hearts.")

### Speaking & Listening, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.3** — Evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric — stance, premises, word choice, emphasis, tone. (The encounter's whole device: Scripture and common sense reasoned in the same breath, so resistance becomes duty instead of rebellion — the student evaluates the sermon's premises, and notices the register go pastoral and brief on the son and the slaves.)

### Writing, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.W.11-12.1** — Write arguments to support claims in an analysis of substantive topics or texts, using valid reasoning and sufficient evidence. (The closing question — what did his faith license, and what did it forbid? — is designed as a post-encounter argument prompt, per the teacher instructions.)

## Verification caveats

1. **C3 Framework**: indicator texts verified against multiple independent curriculum documents that quote the Framework verbatim (they agree with each other); the official NCSS PDF itself was not directly retrievable this session. Official publisher: NCSS (2013), socialstudies.org/c3.
2. **No standard asks the encounter's question.** Every code above covers the founding documents, the Revolution, religious traditions, or constitutional theory — none asks students to judge what a minister's faith licensed and what it forbade. The judgment is the library's design, not a standards artifact.
3. **Texas §113.44(c)(1)(A) and (B) are the on-band anchors.** "The rights of resistance to illegitimate government" and the named religious traditions make this the strongest 9–12 alignment in the set for this figure; it is cited because the encounter's doctrine matches the standard's vocabulary, not by stretch.
4. **Not named where founders are named.** Neither the Texas §113.20(b)(4)(B) nor the Florida SS.8.A.3.3 named-individual lists include Witherspoon; those anchors apply by scope (Revolutionary-era individuals), not by name. Do not present them as naming him.
5. **The Madison line is documented; the Wills thesis is not asserted.** The file cites only what the record holds — Madison studied at Princeton 1769–1771 and stayed an extra year under Witherspoon's instruction — not the contested scholarly claim that the Constitution's checks derive from the Lectures.
6. **Grade-band split.** Only South Carolina (USHC/USG), New York (Grade 11, 11.2), and Texas (US Government §113.44) place the founding era in a 9–12 course. Florida and California teach the Revolution and founding documents in Grade 8. The encounter targets 9–12, so the Grade 8 anchors are real but one band below target.
