## Authored devices

- **The "rotting" rejoinder.** The famous reply in the July 1776 independence debate ("not only ripe for the measure, but in danger of rotting for the want of it") is reported by Ashbel Green's *Life of the Revd. John Witherspoon* (a later account), not by any 1776 document. The prompt has the figure own the *sense* of it and explicitly leave the exact wording to the reporters. Plausible, not documented — stated as such.
- **The setting (Tusculum, autumn 1794).** Historically grounded (his farm; failing eyesight in his last decade; daily college work passed to Samuel Stanhope Smith), but the specific conversation is invented. Pure dramatic device.
- **"Preached to Congress" corrected.** Some popular accounts describe the May 1776 sermon as preached to Congress. The record: Congress appointed the fast day; Witherspoon preached at Princeton, to his own congregation, and dedicated the printed sermon to John Hancock. The prompt has the figure state this correctly.

## Historical caveats

- **James Witherspoon's rank.** Sources disagree: Wikipedia reports he served as aide to General Francis Nash "with the rank of major"; other accounts call him a captain. The figure's answers state only "aide to General Nash" — the rank is uncertain.
- **The son John.** One popular account (Online Library of Liberty) says Witherspoon "lost two sons in the Revolutionary War." The documented record: James was killed at Germantown (Oct 4, 1777); the next son, John, was "lost at sea in 1795" — after the war. The prompt's figure speaks only of James.
- **No surviving letter of grief.** No letter by Witherspoon about James's death is known in the corpus consulted; many of his papers were destroyed when Nassau Hall was ransacked. The figure does not perform private grief — the silence is the record's, not the prompt's invention.
- **The names of the two people he held.** Not given in the sources consulted (tax ratables list two slaves; the death inventory lists possessions). The figure does not invent names.
- **The 1790 committee passage.** The "28 years, as in Pennsylvania" wording is quoted via Wikipedia citing the 1912 Princeton edition of the *Lectures* (p. 74); the 1810 American edition text consulted does not contain it. The figure's committee answer is traced to the secondary account; wording is paraphrase, not claimed verbatim.
- **Did he free anyone?** No manumission by Witherspoon himself is documented. He advocated *gradual* emancipation and voluntary manumission by others. The prompt forbids the figure from claiming any manumission of his own.
- **The Madison line.** Madison studied at Princeton 1769–1771 (graduated 1771) and stayed an extra year 1771–72 under Witherspoon's personal instruction — documented. The claim that the Constitution's checks, or Federalist arguments, derive from Witherspoon's lectures (Garry Wills, *Inventing America*) is a contested scholarly thesis. The prompt and debrief ground only what is documented: Madison sat through the lectures on resistance and checked government; the debrief names the Wills thesis as disputed.
- **Eyesight.** Sources confirm his eyesight was failing in his last decade and he continued preaching nearly every Sunday. Total blindness is not documented; the prompt does not claim it.
- **Jeremiah 48:10.** A "cursed be he that doeth the work of the Lord deceitfully" line is sometimes associated with Witherspoon's war preaching, but it could not be confirmed in the *Dominion of Providence* sermon text from the sources consulted. It is not used in the prompt.
- **"Address to the Natives of Scotland" date.** Printed 1778 as an appendix to the fast-day sermon (per ThriftBooks listing of the reprint); composed 1776. Not load-bearing.
