# Meet Cicero — Standards Alignment (verified 2026-10-15)

Encounter: you meet Marcus Tullius Cicero at his villa at Formiae in December 43 BC — proscribed, with Antony's soldiers on the road — and press him on the gap between his two corpora: the speeches' thunder (the Catilinarians, the fourteen Philippics) and the letters' arithmetic (800+ letters to Atticus and friends, showing the calculations behind the principles). The encounter closes on its built-in judgment: the letters show the calculations behind the principles — was he a principled servant of the Republic, or a calculating opportunist? Judge him.

**Grade band fit for every framework below: 9–12** (high school ELA literacy / Government / World History). Note: most states' *content* anchors for classical Rome sit in Grade 6 (see caveats); the 9–12 weight of this encounter comes from skills standards — close reading of paired texts, discussion, argumentation — which are band-matched.

## 1. C3 Framework (National Council for the Social Studies, 2013)

Grade band: **9–12**. (Indicator texts verified against curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim; the official NCSS PDF was not directly retrievable in this session — see verification note at end.)

### Dimension 2 — History

- **D2.His.4.9-12** — Analyze complex and interacting factors that influenced the perspectives of people during different historical eras. (The same man's perspective across two registers: the speech for the Forum crowd vs the letter to Atticus — the 64 BC Catiline-defense calculation vs the 63 BC denunciation; the private Ad Att. 9.10 wavering between Caesar and Pompey in the spring of 49 vs the public choice of Pompey, "late, and without faith.")
- **D2.His.5.9-12** — Analyze how historical contexts shaped and continue to shape people's perspectives. (His calculations are shaped by the dying Republic's pressures — the novus homo's climb, the Senate's emergency decree behind the 5 December executions, Antony's proscription list with his name on it.)
- **D2.His.10.9-12** — Detect possible limitations in various kinds of historical evidence and differing secondary interpretations. (The letters are a *second performance*, not the unvarnished truth: Roman letters were copied, read aloud, and passed hand to hand, and he knew it — and the surviving archive was selected, arranged, and possibly edited after his death by hands we cannot name.)
- **D2.His.11.9-12** — Critique the usefulness of historical sources for a specific historical inquiry based on their maker, date, place of origin, intended audience, and purpose. (The encounter's core source question: judge the letters as evidence by maker (Cicero, performing even in private), date, audience (Atticus, with letters circulating), purpose — and by the posthumous editor who chose which mail we get.)
- **D2.His.16.9-12** — Integrate evidence from multiple relevant historical sources and interpretations into a reasoned argument about the past. (The closing judgment requires integrating both corpora — speeches *and* letters — into one verdict; the debrief warns the letters don't settle anything, so the argument must hold both.)

### Dimension 4 — Communicating Conclusions and Taking Informed Action

- **D4.1.9-12** — Construct arguments using precise and knowledgeable claims, with evidence from multiple sources, while acknowledging counterclaims and evidentiary weaknesses. (The closing question is the argument prompt: principled servant or calculating opportunist — each side has its evidence, and the letters' performance cuts against both.)
- **D4.4.9-12** — Critique the use of claims and evidence in arguments for credibility. (Students test his claims against his own evidence — "who told you he was great? He did" — and test whether the letters' candor is evidence or a second act.)

*Verified against: multiple independent curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim (2026-10-15; they agree with each other — womenshistory.org lesson plans, thinkport.org inquiry kits, curriculum.llilasbenson.utexas.edu units, electionlab.org alignment). Official document: College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework for Social Studies State Standards, NCSS, 2013 (socialstudies.org/c3).*

## 2. Common Core ELA — Literacy in History/Social Studies & Speaking/Listening

Grade band: **9–12**. (Verified against the official Common Core State Standards Initiative site, thecorestandards.org.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 9–10

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.1** — Cite specific textual evidence to support analysis of primary and secondary sources, attending to such features as the date and origin of the information. (The debrief's plain record is built from dated texts — Ad Att. 9.10 (Formiae, 18 March 49 BC), Ad Att. 14.21.3 (spring 44 BC), the Philippics (Sept 44–Apr 43 BC) — and students cite them by date and occasion in the judgment.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.6** — Compare the point of view of two or more authors for how they treat the same or similar topics, including which details they include and emphasize in their respective accounts. (Here the two "authors" are Cicero-public and Cicero-private: the In Catilinam thunder vs the 64 BC letter's calculation; the Philippics' "wolf set to guard the sheep" vs Ad Att. 14.21.3's private rehearsal — "animo virili, consilio puerili" — each version emphasizing different details.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.8** — Assess the extent to which the reasoning and evidence in a text support the author's claims. (His sworn claim "I saved the Republic" — the oath on laying down the consulship — tested against the letters' arithmetic: the wavering, the trimming, the "tollendum" pun on Octavian.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.3** — Evaluate various explanations for actions or events and determine which explanation best accords with textual evidence, acknowledging where the text leaves matters uncertain. (His explanation of the 49 BC wavering — Pompey "had used me and slighted me," Caesar "flattered me and was strangling the Republic" — weighed against the debrief's plain record; the encounter admits some of his explanations are spin.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.6** — Evaluate authors' differing points of view on the same historical event or issue by assessing the authors' claims, reasoning, and evidence. (The Ides of March: his private letter's judgment ("a man's courage and a boy's judgment") vs his public Philippics' performance — the same author's differing accounts, assessed claim by claim.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.8** — Evaluate an author's premises, claims, and evidence by corroborating or challenging them with other information. (De Officiis's premise — "nothing can be truly useful which is not also honorable" — challenged with other information: in the same months he was writing it he was "counting the Senate's votes like a moneylender" and courting the boy Caesar.)

### Speaking & Listening, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.1** — Initiate and participate effectively in a range of collaborative discussions (one-on-one, in groups, and teacher-led) with diverse partners on grades 11–12 topics, texts, and issues, building on others' ideas and expressing their own clearly and persuasively. (The encounter *is* a sustained one-on-one examination: the tutorial's brief is to "question him the way an examiner presses a witness," and he spars back.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.3** — Evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric, assessing the stance, premises, links among ideas, word choice, points of emphasis, and tone used. (Students track his rhetoric in real time — the flattery ("an excellent question"), the Greek tags, the rhetorical questions, the anxious fluency that quickens when the subject turns to his own calculations — and use it as evidence in the judgment.)

### Writing, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.W.11-12.1** — Write arguments to support claims in an analysis of substantive topics or texts, using valid reasoning and relevant and sufficient evidence. (The closing question — principled servant of the Republic, or calculating opportunist? — is designed as a post-encounter argument prompt, per the teacher instructions.)

## 3. South Carolina — Social Studies College- and Career-Ready Standards (2019)

Grade band: **9–12** encounter weight is skills-based; the content anchor is Grade 6.

- **6.1.P** (Grade 6) — Analyze the shift from early to classical civilizations and the enduring contributions of classical civilizations. (State guidance names Rome among the "four major classical civilizations" whose "turning points in the organization and transformation" students inquire into — Cicero lived through the Republic's terminal turning point — and whose "cultural and technological advancements... continue to influence modern societies": De Officiis was Europe's standard ethics text for fifteen hundred years, and his Latin prose set the school standard. Grade-band caveat: SC's only classical-civilizations content anchor sits in Grade 6; no high-school SC anchor covers ancient Rome — Modern World History begins at 1300, and USHC/USG cover American history.)

*Verified against: the official 2019 South Carolina Social Studies College- and Career-Ready Standards document on ed.sc.gov (Grade 6 standards; indicator and state guidance quoted verbatim 2026-10-15).*

## Verification caveats

1. **C3 Framework**: indicator texts were verified against multiple independent curriculum documents that quote the Framework verbatim (they agree with each other); the official NCSS PDF itself was not directly retrievable this session. Treat the official publisher as NCSS (2013), socialstudies.org/c3.
2. **Grade-band split**: the 9–12 alignments for Cicero are skills standards (RH, SL, W, D4) plus the perspective/source-analysis C3 indicators. State content anchors for classical Rome sit below the 9–12 band except NY 9.3 (Grades 9–10), TX §113.42 (World History), and FL SS.912.W.2.13 (9–12).
3. **RH fit is debrief-driven**: the encounter proper is conversational; the reading standards engage through the debrief's primary-text pairings (speech vs letter on Catiline, on the Ides judgment, on Octavian) and the teacher-supplied excerpts of the corpus.
4. **No adopted SC high-school anchor for ancient Rome**: checked the 2019 SC high-school courses — Modern World History begins at 1300; USHC and US Government cover American history. 6.1.P (Grade 6) is the only SC content anchor.
