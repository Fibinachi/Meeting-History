## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — say which.

- **The meeting itself.** No record exists of Cicero receiving a visitor to be questioned about his life in December 43 BC. Pure dramatic device — the library's standard conceit.
- **The setting (Formiae, early December 43 BC).** The facts are documented — he left Rome on 27 November 43, the proscription lists were posted, the sailing from Caieta failed, and he was at his Formian villa when the soldiers came on 7 December — but the scene of him receiving a questioner is assembled, not recorded. Historically plausible, not documented.
- **His half-knowledge of the danger.** He knows he is proscribed and that Antony demands his death (documented — Plutarch, Appian). He does NOT know the manner of his death (the beheading, the head and hands at the Rostra). The prompt preserves this: "Do not ask me to prophesy my own corpse." The death details belong to the debrief, never to him.
- **"Whatever his reasons were."** He knows Octavian did not save him; Appian's account (Octavian resisted two days, then conceded) is deliberately kept out of his mouth. It belongs to the teacher notes, not the figure. Not his knowledge — an honest boundary.
- **His spin on the Catiline-defense consideration.** The fact (he considered defending Catiline in 64, then decided against it) is documented in the historical record. The excuse — "What would you have had me do — lose the consulship to keep my letters clean?" — is the figure's performance, authored for the encounter. The fact is sourced; the spin is his.
- **The letters as "for no one's eyes but theirs."** His opening claims the letters were private. In fact Roman letters were routinely copied and read aloud, and he knew it (the debrief says so). The opening line is his self-flattery, not the history — the debrief corrects it. Deliberate.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Written before the figure ships, even the ones decided not to matter.

- **DEAD RULE — satisfied.** Marcus Tullius Cicero was murdered on 7 December 43 BC near Formiae by soldiers acting under the proscription of the Second Triumvirate, at Antony's demand. The encounter is set days before; he knows nothing after it.
- **The letters were published after his death — and were selected and arranged.** Scholars agree the surviving letters are not a random archive: an editor selected, arranged, and possibly edited them to present a particular view of Cicero as a public personality. The traditional guess is his freedman and secretary Tiro, but the publisher is not known with certainty. The collection to Atticus may not have circulated until the middle of the first century CE. Say this honestly: we are reading his mail, but someone chose which mail we get.
- **Some letters' authenticity is questioned.** A handful of letters in the Ad Brutum collection (and the letters Ad Octavium) have been doubted by scholars. The prompt draws nothing from the disputed letters.
- **The "considering defending Catiline" fact.** Documented for 64 BC (Clodius prosecuted Catiline for extortion in Africa; Cicero, then a consular candidate, considered taking the defense and decided not to). The prompt keeps the figure's self-justification clearly his own.
- **The five executions, 5 December 63 BC.** Carried out without trial, under the Senate's emergency decree (senatus consultum ultimum). Cicero maintained it was legal; his enemies called it murder, and Clodius used it to drive him into exile in 58. The legality was contested then and is contested now. The prompt states the fact without settling the law.
- **"Father of his Country."** He claimed the title by popular acclaim after the conspiracy's suppression — it is his boast, reported by him. The prompt presents it as his claim, not as a formal grant.
- **The oath.** On laying down the consulship (31 December 63), the tribune Metellus Nepos vetoed his customary valedictory speech; he swore instead that he had saved the Republic (In Pisonem 6.13). The prompt quotes the substance, not a verbatim Latin oath.
- **"Animo virili, consilio puerili."** Ad Atticum 14.21.3 — confirmed in the text ("acta illa res est animo virili, consilio puerili"; "excisa enim est arbor, non evulsa"). The prompt translates it as "a man's courage and a boy's judgment."
- **"Laudandum adulescentem, ornandum, tollendum."** Reported by Decimus Brutus to Cicero (Ad Familiares 11.20.1): Octavian resented Cicero's remark about him. The pun on tollo (raise up / do away with) is Cicero's. The prompt owns it as his.
- **The wolf line is from a speech, not a letter.** "O praeclarum custodem ovium, ut aiunt, lupum" is Philippic 3.11 — public rhetoric, not private correspondence. The prompt places it correctly in the Philippics answer. (An earlier draft risked misattributing it to the letters; corrected after checking.)
- **Philippic 2 was published, not delivered.** The standard scholarly view: composed as a written reply to Antony's attack of 19 September 44 and circulated as a pamphlet. The prompt states this.
- **De Officiis dating.** Written September–November 44 BC for his son Marcus, then studying philosophy at Athens. The overlap with the opening Philippics (from 2 September 44) is chronology, and the prompt's "in the same months" is accurate.
- **Octavian's two days.** Appian (Civil Wars 4.19) reports that Octavian argued for two days against putting Cicero on the list, then conceded him to Antony. This is kept out of the figure's mouth and out of the debrief's main line; it belongs in teacher notes for the "did Octavian betray him?" question.
- **Fulvia and the tongue.** Cassius Dio alone reports that Antony's wife Fulvia took the head and stabbed the tongue with a hairpin. Plutarch does not include it. Treat as reported-by-Dio, not certain — it appears only in the teacher notes, never in the encounter or debrief.
- **Hands and head on the Rostra.** Plutarch and Appian agree: both hands (or the right hand, in some accounts — the prompt's debrief says "head and hands") were cut off and displayed at the Rostra by Antony's order. The debrief follows Plutarch/Appian.
