# Meet Marcus Tullius Cicero — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Marcus Tullius Cicero (106–43 BC), Rome's greatest orator, in the last days of his life — proscribed, waiting at his villa for Antony's soldiers. Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student renders the closing judgment: the letters show the calculations behind the principles — principled servant of the Republic, or calculating opportunist? Judge him.

This is the library's best example of a private corpus undercutting a public one — and the twist to prepare for: the letters were also a performance. Roman letters were copied, read aloud, and passed hand to hand, and Cicero knew it. Students who decide the letters are "the real Cicero" and the speeches "the act" have fallen for the second performance. The debrief names this directly.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

- **Political murder, stated as fact, never depicted.** The encounter is set days before Cicero's death; no one dies on screen. The debrief states, factually, that he was beheaded on 7 December 43 BC and that his head and hands were displayed at the Rostra in the Forum on Antony's orders. There is no gore in the telling — the facts are stated plainly, the violation is not described for effect.
- **Proscription.** Students need to know what the word means before they start: a published death list, with rewards for killers and confiscation of the victim's property. Say it in your framing — the encounter assumes the student understands he is a condemned man.
- **State-ordered execution without trial.** Cicero states, as his own proud fact, that he had five conspirators strangled on 5 December 63 BC without trial. The debrief presents the other side: his enemies called it murder, and it was the legal pretext for his exile. This is processed in the debrief, not relitigated in the encounter.
- **Violent political language, quoted from the speeches.** The Philippics' attacks on Antony are genuinely vicious — drunkard, bankrupt, a wolf set to guard the sheep, a man whose crimes "made Catiline look tolerable." The prompt holds this rhetoric unsoftened because it is the evidence: this is the language that got him killed. Handled honestly, never savored.
- **No sexual content. No slurs.**

Recommended for grades 9–12. Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Cicero page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: ~25–30 minutes for the encounter, ~20–25 for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the opening carefully. Note three things: he tells you the format ("You ask, I answer"), he tells you the judgment is coming, and he hands you the two corpora — the speeches and the letters — as the evidence.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "Why did you have the conspirators executed?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. The encounter's beats: (1) the tutorial hands off; (2) early questions — the new man from Arpinum, the consulship, Catiline; (3) the middle — the letters: the Catiline defense he considered, the wavering of 49, De Officiis and his son, the Philippics; (4) the late turn — Antony, the proscription list, what the speeches cost; (5) he closes with the judgment question and stops; (6) you run the debrief.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Marcus Tullius Cicero — Rome's greatest orator, a condemned man waiting at his villa for the soldiers to come. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. He gave great speeches about saving the Republic, and he wrote private letters showing all the calculations behind them. At the end, you'll be asked to judge him: principled servant of the Republic, or calculating opportunist? There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Cicero answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- He will flatter good questions and spar with hostile ones — both are in character. Neither is the encounter breaking.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Pacing

- **Opening:** 2–3 exchanges. He is dazzling; don't let students camp here.
- **Early (life and work):** 4–6 exchanges. Arpinum, the new man, the consulship, Catiline.
- **Middle (the hard facts):** 8–12 exchanges. The letters — the defense he considered, the wavering, De Officiis, the Philippics. Watch for the moment the fluency quickens into justification; that is the moment to press.
- **Late (the judgment):** 3–5 exchanges. Antony, the list, what the speeches cost — then the closing.

If a student finishes early, the late-pool exit is the closing. Do not let the encounter drift past 30 minutes; the debrief is where the learning consolidates.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer: "You are going to meet a man who wrote down both his principles and his calculations — the speeches for the world, the letters for his friends — and then you are going to judge him. The question is whether the arithmetic destroys the principles, or whether a man can calculate and still serve."

## The debrief — do not skip this

Six fixed sections, in order, delivered out of character in a plain modern register: (1) his name — Marcus Tullius Cicero (106–43 BC); (2) what the history records — the consulship, Catiline, the executions, the exile, De Officiis, the Philippics, the proscription, the murder; (3) what came after — the head and hands at the Rostra, Augustus, the letters published after his death and possibly edited, Petrarch's rediscovery, De Officiis as Europe's ethics textbook; (4) the plain record — the calculations in the letters, the executions without trial, the Philippics' violence and its price; (5) the mechanism — the second performance: the letters were also written to be read; (6) the question that remains — judge him.

Then discuss. Openings built to produce disagreement:

- He wrote the letters knowing they would be copied and read aloud. Is that candor, or a second performance — and does it matter which?
- He had five men executed without trial to save the Republic. Did the Republic survive because of it, or was the precedent itself the wound?
- "Praised, honored, and lifted up — or lifted off." A joke, a threat, or both? Who was he performing for when he wrote it?
- The Philippics got him killed. Was the speech worth the life?
- In private he called the Ides of March "a man's courage and a boy's judgment"; in public he praised the assassins. Which was the real Cicero — or is the question wrong?

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this. Cicero also has stall lines — he will hand the student a question ("Ask me what I wrote to Atticus.").
- **Answers run long.** He is an orator by design; if the model starts speechifying, remind it once: "short answers, one paragraph." The prompt's 60–100 word rule usually holds.
- **The voice goes modern.** If Cicero starts talking about "opportunism," "his legacy," or "democracy," that is a voice failure — the prompt forbids those words by name (the closing question is the one scripted exception). Restate the corpus-vocabulary rule and continue.
- **The voice softens the Philippics.** If the model starts apologizing for the attacks on Antony or explaining them away, that is a voice failure — the prompt requires the rhetoric unsoftened. Restate the guardrail and continue.
- **The student asks for legal or political advice.** Exit to the debrief voice per the guardrails; Cicero gives no modern counsel.

## Historical notes for the teacher

The honest answers to "did it really happen like that?" — one per caveat in sources.md:

- **Yes, he was murdered on 7 December 43 BC** — by soldiers acting under the Second Triumvirate's proscription, at Antony's demand, near his villa at Formiae. The encounter is set days before; he does not know its manner.
- **Yes, the head and hands were displayed at the Rostra** — Plutarch and Appian agree; the hands because they wrote the Philippics. The Fulvia-hairpin detail comes from Cassius Dio alone — report it as Dio's account, not certain fact, and keep it out of the encounter.
- **Yes, the letters were published after his death, selected and arranged** — by an editor we cannot name with certainty (Tiro is the traditional guess). The To Atticus collection may not have circulated until the mid-1st century CE. A few letters' authenticity is questioned; the prompt uses none of them.
- **Yes, he considered defending Catiline in 64 BC** — then decided against it, then denounced him as consul. The fact is documented; the excuse in the encounter ("lose the consulship to keep my letters clean?") is the figure's own spin.
- **Yes, the five were executed without trial on 5 December 63 BC** — under the Senate's emergency decree. Legal in his view, murder in his enemies'; Clodius exiled him for it in 58.
- **"Father of his Country" is his own boast** — he claimed popular acclaim. The prompt presents it as his claim.
- **The famous lines check out** — "animo virili, consilio puerili" (Ad Att. 14.21.3); "laudandum adulescentem, ornandum, tollendum" (reported Ad Fam. 11.20.1, the pun is his); the wolf line is from Philippic 3.11, a speech, not a letter; Philippic 2 was published as a pamphlet, never delivered.
- **De Officiis was written in autumn 44 BC for his son Marcus at Athens** — in the same months the Philippics began. The prompt's simultaneity is chronology.
- **Did Octavian betray him?** Appian reports Octavian argued two days against the proscription, then conceded Cicero to Antony. This is deliberately kept out of the figure's mouth — he knows only that the boy did not save him.

## One last thing

Teach them to compare the letter with the speech. Cicero will answer any single question brilliantly — and it is the second source on the same event, the private version beside the public one, that carries the encounter. When the student has the speech, ask for the letter. When they have the letter, ask what he said in public. That is where the judgment lives.
