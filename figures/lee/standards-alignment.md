# Meet Robert E. Lee — Standards Alignment (verified 2026-10-15)

Encounter: students interview Lee in his Washington College office (summer 1870) about the resignation decision, the war, his slaveholding, the Arlington estate, Wesley Norris's testimony, and his postwar counsel. The tutorial and debrief state the plain record he will not volunteer; his courtesy is the seduction and his silences are the evidence. Closing question (asked by the modern docent, never in his voice): *"He was honorable, dignified, beloved — and he fought for slavery. Judge him."*

Key beats: hearing his full stated reason for resigning and noticing he never names what Virginia seceded to protect; comparing his 1856 paternalism letter against his sworn 1866 freedmen testimony; testing his Arlington account ("I did my duty as executor") against the will's terms and the court petitions; weighing Norris's 1866 testimony against Lee's private denial; evaluating his postwar reconciliation counsel beside his opposition to black suffrage; checking the provenance of everything attributed to him (the forged "Duty" letter, the reported Leyburn statement, the Marshall-drafted farewell order).

**Grade band fit for every framework below: 9–12**, with the grade-band caveat below — Texas and California place Civil War content in Grade 8; the 9–12 work for this encounter lives in the historiography and source-evaluation standards.

## 1. C3 Framework (NCSS, 2013)

Strongest first — the Lost Cause guardrail is itself alignable. (Texts verified against curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim; the official NCSS PDF was not directly retrievable this session — see caveats.)

- **D2.His.6.9-12** — Analyze the ways in which the perspectives of those writing history shaped the history that they produced. The Lost Cause's "Marble Man" is the case study: who made Lee the centerpiece, and what history did that make?
- **D2.His.7.9-12** — How present perspectives shape interpretations of the past: the re-examination of his veneration, Charlottesville 2017, the recontextualized monuments.
- **D2.His.3.9-12** — How the significance of his actions changed over time: the marble saint of the early 20th century vs. the slaveholder of the documented record.
- **D2.His.11.9-12** — Critique source usefulness by maker, date, place of origin, intended audience, and purpose: Norris's testimony (the enslaved witness) vs. Lee's private denial; the forged "Duty" letter fails the date-and-origin test outright; the Leyburn statement arrives as reported speech, not his pen.
- **D2.His.8.9-12** — How current interpretations are limited by whose perspectives the available sources represent — the enslaved people's voices in (and absent from) the record.
- **D2.His.14.9-12** — Multiple and complex causes and effects: his personal decision set against the political cause (slavery, named by the Confederacy's own leaders).
- **D4.1.9-12** — The closing judgment, built from multiple sources with counterclaims acknowledged — including the counterclaim his courtesy invites.

## 2. Common Core ELA — Literacy in History/Social Studies & Speaking/Listening

- **RH.11-12.6** — Evaluate differing points of view on the same issue: his 1856 letter on slavery vs. his 1866 sworn testimony vs. the Cornerstone Speech's account of the cause.
- **RH.11-12.8** — Evaluate his claims by corroborating or challenging them: "I did my duty as executor" against the will's five-year term and the two court petitions; "rejoiced that slavery is abolished" (reported speech) against the "savage and brutal policy" letter.
- **RH.11-12.1 / RH.9-10.1** — Cite textual evidence "attending to such features as the date and origin of the information": exactly the skill the forged Duty letter, the Marshall-drafted General Order No. 9, and the Leyburn reported speech demand.
- **RH.11-12.9** — Integrate letters, testimony, the plain record, and secondary accounts into a coherent understanding, noting discrepancies.
- **SL.11-12.3** — Evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric: his courtesy *is* rhetoric, and the load-bearing silences are its most important feature.
- **SL.11-12.1** — Structured debrief discussion ("Some of you liked him. Say so out loud, and then say what the record says.").

## 3. South Carolina (2019 Social Studies College- and Career-Ready Standards, US History and the Constitution)

South Carolina's high school US History course covers the Civil War era directly (Standard 2, 1803–1877):
- **USHC.2.CE** — Evaluate the causes and consequences of economic and geographic expansion through significant turning points from 1803–1865.
- **USHC.2.CX** — Contextualize the perspectives on the role of the federal government in securing natural rights during the period 1830–1877 — the secession/slavery frame, stated in the standard's own terms.
- **USHC.2.CC** — Differentiate the patterns of continuity and change within the development of sectionalism and reunion.

## 4. Other states

**Texas (TEKS).**
- **§113.20(b)(8)(B)** (Grade 8 U.S. History) — "Explain the causes of the Civil War, particularly the central role of the expansion of slavery (with sectionalism and states' rights as contributing factors), and significant events of the Civil War." The encounter's plain record in the standard's own language.
- *Grade-band caveat:* as with the Trial alignment, Texas places Civil War content in Grade 8; the high school course (§113.41) begins at 1877 and has no Civil War anchor. The 9–12 classroom value is the historiography and source-work above.

**Florida (State Academic Standards).**
- **SS.912.A.2** — Understand the causes, course, and consequences of the Civil War and Reconstruction and its effects on the American people.
- **SS.912.A.2.1** — Review causes and consequences of the Civil War.
- **SS.912.A.1.2** — Utilize primary and secondary sources to identify author, historical significance, audience, and authenticity — the forged-letter and reported-speech provenance work.
- **SS.912.A.1.5** — Evaluate the validity, reliability, bias, and authenticity of resources — the guardrail in standard form.

**California (HSS Content Standards).**
- **9-12.HR.3** — "Evaluate major debates among historians concerning alternative interpretations of the past, including an analysis of authors' use of evidence and the distinctions between sound generalizations and misleading oversimplifications." The Lost Cause guardrail, nearly verbatim.
- **9-12.HR.4** — Construct and test hypotheses; collect, evaluate, and employ information from multiple primary and secondary sources; apply it in oral and written presentations.
- **8.10.2** (Grade 8) — Trace the origins and development of slavery and identify strategies tried to both overturn and preserve it.
- **11.1.4** (Grade 11) — Examine the effects of the Civil War and Reconstruction.
- *Grade-band caveat:* California's slavery-specific content sits in Grade 8 (8.10); the 9–12 anchors are the historical-research skills strand (9-12.HR.3/4).

**New York (K–12 Social Studies Framework, Grade 11).**
- **11.3** — EXPANSION, NATIONALISM, AND SECTIONALISM (1800–1865): growing sectional tensions, especially over slavery, culminating in the Civil War.
- **11.3b** — Different perspectives on constitutional, political, economic, and social issues contributed to the growth of sectionalism.

## Verification caveats

1. **C3:** texts verified against verbatim-quoting curriculum documents (Teacher's Discovery / aedownload.net correlations; judiciallearningcenter.org chart); official NCSS PDF not directly retrievable. Publisher: NCSS (2013), socialstudies.org/c3.

3. **South Carolina / Florida / California / New York Civil War codes:** USHC.2.x, SS.912.A.2.x, 8.10.2/11.1.4/9-12.HR.x, and 11.3/11.3b were verified against official documents on 2026-10-15 for the Trial of John Brown alignment and re-confirmed by code this session; the quoted 9-12.HR.3 text is from the CA standards compilation.
4. **The guardrail-as-standard:** the encounter's Lost Cause guardrails (no vindication, no modern moralizing, the forged letter disowned) are design rules, not standards — but they map directly onto D2.His.6.9-12, D2.His.7.9-12, RH.11-12.8, and FL SS.912.A.1.5, which is why the alignment claims them as served rather than as cited authority.
