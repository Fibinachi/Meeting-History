# Sources — Robert E. Lee (1807–1870)

The corpus this figure's voice is bounded by. The voice may claim only what these texts support. List everything the canonical answers draw on; link where the text is public.

Lee wrote no memoir. The voice is built from correspondence — which means it is courteous, controlled, and often silent on the hardest things. The prompt must not fill those silences with exoneration.

## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — say which.

- **The setting: summer 1870, his office at Washington College, Lexington.** No direct evidence of such a conversation; historically plausible, not documented. The date is chosen deliberately: the war, Appomattox, the 1866 testimony, the 1869 McConaughy letter, and the May 1870 Leyburn statement are all behind him — and his death that October is not yet known to him.
- **The conversation itself.** A student interviewing Lee never happened — the standing dramatic device of the library.
- **The closing question is asked by the modern docent voice, not by Lee.** Lee never framed his life as "fighting for slavery," and putting those words in his mouth would be a voice break. The scripted close has him hand over the judgment in his own words ("The judgment of it is not mine to make. It is yours"); the docent then asks the closing question verbatim. This is a deliberate craft decision, documented here.
- **Abridgment and plain rendering of the canonical answers.** The letters are compressed into 60–100-word answers in accessible diction. Lee's actual prose is plainer than most figures' in this library, so the simplification is light. Compression is authored; nothing in the substance is invented.
- **The tutorial states facts Lee would not volunteer** (his slaveholding, the court petitions, the Cornerstone Speech). This is required by the library's Lost Cause guardrails for this figure — see teacher instructions. It is framing, not foreshadowing: facts, not conclusions.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Write these before the figure ships, even the ones you decided don't matter.

- **The January 23, 1861 "secession is nothing but revolution" letter was to his son, not his wife.** It was written from Fort Mason, Texas, to his son (the Lee Family Archive's edition is titled "A Letter of R. E. Lee to His Son"). Some popular accounts misattribute it to Mary. The encounter states it correctly.
- **The "Duty is the sublimest word in our language" letter is a forgery.** Purportedly to his son G. W. Custis Lee, dated 5 April 1852, published in the *New York Sun* on 26 November 1864 — it was repudiated within weeks by "a source entitled to know," and UVA law professor Charles A. Graves demonstrated in 1914 (Proceedings of the Virginia State Bar Association, 17:176) that it contradicts Lee's biography and letter-writing style; Lee's son said he never received it. The figure disowns it, and the prompt forbids it by name. This is one of the most-quoted fake Lee quotes in American life.
- **The Leyburn "rejoiced that slavery is abolished" statement is reported speech, not a letter.** It comes to us through Douglas Southall Freeman's 1934 biography, from Pastor John Leyburn's account of a conversation on 1 May 1870. Freeman is a sympathetic biographer. The encounter renders it as Lee's spoken statement and marks the provenance here. Do not cite it as a letter Lee wrote.
- **General Order No. 9 was drafted by Colonel Charles Marshall and edited by Lee.** The farewell order's famous sentences are Lee's as finalized, but the draft was Marshall's. The encounter notes this; the figure speaks the order as his own, which it was when issued.
- **The McConaughy letter was about Gettysburg battlefield markers specifically.** Lee declined an invitation from the Gettysburg Battlefield Memorial Association (a Union-led body) to mark positions on that field. Some historians argue this was not a blanket opposition to all Confederate monuments. The encounter uses his words as written and does not claim more for them.
- **"All this has been my fault" to Pickett at Gettysburg is secondhand.** The famous scene of Lee taking blame on the field comes through postwar recollections, not Lee's own pen. The encounter does not use it as canonical; it uses the documented August 8, 1863 letter to Davis instead.
- **Did Lee personally whip anyone?** The 1859 anonymous letters to the *New-York Tribune* alleged he did; Wesley Norris's 1866 testimony says the overseer and a county constable did the whipping while Lee stood by urging them on, and ordered the brine. Norris did not confirm Lee struck anyone himself. Lee privately called the account false and refused public comment. The debrief states Norris's testimony as Norris gave it, with this uncertainty marked.
- **How many enslaved people?** Custis's estate held nearly 200 enslaved people across three plantations (Arlington, White House, Romancoke). The December 29, 1862 deed of manumission names 63 — many had escaped to Union lines during the war or been hired out far from the estates. Both numbers are on the record; they describe different things.
- **The courts refused Lee twice.** State courts in 1858 and 1862 denied his petitions to extend his control of the Custis enslaved people beyond the will's five-year term. (American Civil War Museum; Encyclopedia Virginia.)
- **Lee's oath of allegiance (2 October 1865) was set aside and lost**; it resurfaced in the National Archives in 1970. Congress restored his full citizenship by joint resolution, signed by President Gerald Ford at Arlington House on 5 August 1975 — 105 years after his death. He knew he had taken the oath; he knew nothing after.
- **Lee's personal slaveholding.** He inherited enslaved people from his mother (d. 1829); his 1846 will provided for their liberation. The Lee family account says he freed them before the war; treat the timing as the family's claim, not as independently documented.
- **"It is history that teaches us to hope"** (letter to Lt. Col. Charles Marshall, September 1870) is authentic but postdates the encounter's setting (summer 1870). The figure must not quote it. It belongs in the debrief's "what came after" if the teacher wants it.
- **Lee's health in 1870.** He suffered from heart disease and was visibly failing through 1870; he died October 12, 1870, after a stroke on September 28. The encounter does not dwell on this — he speaks as a working college president, which he was.
- **The "rejoiced" statement and the 1866 testimony sit side by side.** Both are his. The encounter does not reconcile them for the student. That is the design.
