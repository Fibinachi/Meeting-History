# Meet Robert E. Lee — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Robert E. Lee (1807–1870), run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student answers the closing question: "He was honorable, dignified, beloved — and he fought for slavery. Judge him."

Lee is the figure most likely to seduce a classroom. He speaks from complete conviction about duty, honor, and Virginia, in the most gracious voice in the library — and the encounter is built so that his courtesy cannot do the work of innocence. The student must hold his dignity in one hand and the plain record in the other, and render the judgment themselves. This figure carries the library's firmest Lost Cause guardrails, explained below.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

Students will encounter slavery discussed as a system Lee participated in and defended: his personal slaveholding, his management of nearly two hundred enslaved people as executor of his father-in-law's estate, and his petitioning the courts to delay their freedom. They will encounter Wesley Norris's 1866 testimony that Lee had three runaways — Norris, his sister Mary, and their cousin — stripped and lashed (fifty lashes each, twenty for Mary), stood by urging the whipper to "lay it on well," and ordered their backs washed with brine. They will encounter the Civil War's mass death, Lee's description of emancipation as a "savage and brutal policy," and his sworn 1866 testimony opposing black suffrage and wishing Virginia rid of its black population. The debrief's "what came after" touches white-supremacist violence at Charlottesville in 2017. Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Lee page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: 25–30 for the encounter, 20–25 for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Lee opens with a scripted greeting and tells the student what will be asked at the end. Read it carefully — then read the tutorial that precedes it, which states the hard facts (his slaveholding, the court petitions, the Cornerstone Speech) that he will not volunteer. That tutorial is the guardrail. It is load-bearing.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "Why did you resign your commission?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. The encounter runs in one conversation: tutorial hands off to Lee; the student questions him through the decision (1861), the war (Gettysburg, Appomattox), and the hard facts (slavery, Arlington, the freedmen); Lee closes with his scripted handover and the modern voice asks the closing question verbatim; the debrief follows. There are no multi-figure transitions to manage — your job is pacing and the debrief.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Robert E. Lee — the Confederate general, and probably the most courteous man you'll ever talk to through this library. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. He will be gracious, dignified, and completely convinced he did right. Your job is to find out what he did, listen to how he talks about it — and notice what he never says. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Lee answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- If a student stalls ("I don't know what to ask"), Lee hands them a question himself — "Ask me why I resigned," "Ask me of Gettysburg."
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.
- At the close, Lee hands over the judgment in his own words, and then — in the modern voice, not his — the closing question is asked verbatim. He cannot ask it himself; he never framed his life in its terms. That is deliberate.

### Pacing

Opening: 2–3 exchanges on who he is and the resignation — don't let students camp here; his reason is stated fully and early, on purpose. Middle: 6–10 exchanges on the war — Gettysburg, his offer to resign, Appomattox, what he says the war was about. Listen for the word he never says. Late: the last third belongs to the hard facts — slavery, Arlington, Wesley Norris, the freedmen, his postwar counsel. If a student never asks about slavery, the stall lines and the suggestions will offer it — but a teacher nudge toward the suggestions is also fine. The encounter is designed so the hardest material arrives after the student has felt the courtesy. Do not rush this.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer: Hold the contradiction in both hands — the most honorable, dignified, beloved man in this library fought for slavery. Then decide: does his honor survive that fact?

## The debrief — do not skip this

Run the six sections in fixed order: (1) name him — Robert Edward Lee (1807–1870); (2) what the history records — the April 20, 1861 resignation, command of the Army of Northern Virginia, Appomattox, the Washington College presidency, death October 12, 1870; (3) what came after — the Lost Cause's Marble Man, the monuments, Arlington National Cemetery, the 1975 citizenship restoration, the re-examination, Charlottesville 2017; (4) the plain record — the slaveholding, the estate, the court petitions, the December 29, 1862 manumission, Norris's testimony, the army he commanded and the cause it served; (5) the mechanism — his graciousness was the danger; his silences were the evidence; dignity was not allowed to do the work of innocence; (6) the question that remains — "He was honorable, dignified, beloved — and he fought for slavery. Judge him."

Then open discussion. Discussion openings built to produce disagreement:

- "He said he fought for Virginia, not for slavery. Virginia seceded to protect slavery — its leaders said so. Does his reason survive that fact, or does it collapse into it?"
- "He freed the enslaved people at Arlington in December 1862. He did it because a will required it, after courts twice refused to let him delay. Is that honor? What would honor have looked like?"
- "He took the blame for Gettysburg on himself rather than his subordinates. Does that change your judgment of him — and should it?"
- "His postwar counsel — accept the result, obey the laws, don't keep open the sores of war — was real. He also testified under oath that black men could not vote intelligently and that Virginia would be better rid of them. Hold both. Now judge."
- "Some of you liked him. Say so out loud, and then say what the record says. What do you do with both being true?"
- (Suggested pairing, when it exists: run this encounter beside the Ulysses S. Grant encounter — the man who beat him, and the plainest contrast in the library between fighting against slavery and fighting for it. The Grant figure is planned, not yet built.)

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **Lee starts moralizing in modern terms** ("I understand now that slavery was a crime against humanity") or apologizing in modern confessional style. This is a voice failure and a guardrail breach: stop, re-paste the voice notes section, and continue. He speaks his 1856 paternalism if pressed, exactly as written, and no further. He never has a reckoning he never had.
- **Lee claims vindication** ("history has judged me kindly," "the South remembers"). He does not know what came after the summer of 1870. Correct him in the debrief voice and continue.
- **Lee uses the forged "Duty is the sublimest word" quote**, or a student quotes it at him and he accepts it. He must disown it: "I never wrote those words." Re-anchor with the guardrails if the model lets it stand.
- **Lee softens the Norris account** beyond his documented response, or relitigates it with new facts. He gives his private response (the statement is not true; he would not answer it in the newspapers) and stops. The debrief states Norris's testimony plainly.
- **Lee names slavery as the cause of the war** — in either direction ("I fought for slavery," "the war was about slavery"). He never framed it so. This is a voice break: re-anchor and continue. The plain record lives in the tutorial and the debrief, never in his mouth.
- **A student asks for military or legal advice** (modern conflicts, courts, causes). Exit to the debrief voice: "I'm not able to give [military/legal] advice. Would you like to continue the encounter?"

## Historical notes for the teacher

- The January 23, 1861 "secession is nothing but revolution" letter was written to his son from Texas, not to his wife. Popular accounts often misattribute it.
- The "Duty is the sublimest word in our language" letter is a documented forgery — published 1864, repudiated within weeks, exposed by UVA's Charles A. Graves in 1914. It is one of the most-quoted fake Lee quotes in American life; students will have seen it on posters.
- The "rejoiced that slavery is abolished" statement (May 1, 1870) reaches us through Douglas Southall Freeman's 1934 biography, from Pastor John Leyburn's account — reported speech, not a letter in Lee's hand. Freeman is a sympathetic biographer. Present it as reported.
- General Order No. 9 was drafted by Colonel Charles Marshall and edited/finalized by Lee — his as issued, Marshall's in draft.
- The 1869 McConaughy letter declined a specific invitation (Gettysburg battlefield markers, from a Union-led association). Historians debate how broadly his opposition to monuments extended. The encounter claims only what the letter says.
- The "all this has been my fault" scene with Pickett at Gettysburg is secondhand, via postwar recollections. The encounter uses the documented August 8, 1863 letter to Davis instead.
- On the Norris whipping: the anonymous 1859 Tribune letters alleged Lee struck people himself; Norris's 1866 testimony says the overseer and a constable did the whipping while Lee stood by urging them on, then ordered the brine. Mark the uncertainty; state Norris's testimony as he gave it.
- Lee's oath of allegiance (October 2, 1865) was set aside and lost; his citizenship was restored by Congress in 1975, signed by President Ford at Arlington House.
- "It is history that teaches us to hope" (to Charles Marshall, September 1870) is authentic but postdates the encounter's setting — the figure must not quote it.
- Lee was himself a slaveholder (inherited from his mother; his 1846 will provided for liberation). The family's account says he freed them before the war — treat the timing as the family's claim.

## One last thing

The single skill that matters most for this figure: do not let his courtesy answer the question for them. When a student says "but he seems like a good man," that is the encounter working as designed — send them back to the record, not to your opinion. The debrief's plain record exists so you never have to argue with his charm. Read it, let it sit, and then ask the question that remains.
