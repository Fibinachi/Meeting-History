# Meet Bohr — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Niels Bohr (1885–1962), run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student renders the closing judgment: the man who dethroned Newton couldn't accept a dice-playing universe. Was complementarity insight — or surrender? Judge him.

This encounter's judgment is intellectual: what counts as understanding nature. Bohr explains complementarity in plain terms (no prior physics knowledge is needed — the prompt makes him teach it simply, exactly as he taught it himself), gives his own telling of the thirty-year argument with Einstein, answers honestly about the famous "dice" exchange, and speaks — for the first and only time, aloud — of Heisenberg's 1941 visit and the unsent letters in his desk.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## The entry hook: the revolutionary become the conservative

Open with the irony — it is the fastest way into the encounter: the young man who shattered Newton's clockwork in 1913, who led the quantum revolution, spent his later years as the elder of the new orthodoxy, declaring the revolution complete and declining to engage the next wave of challengers. Across the table sat Einstein — the other revolutionary, who had dethroned Newton with general relativity and then could not accept a universe that plays dice. Two revolutionaries, each become a conservative in a different way, arguing for thirty years. Your students judge Bohr.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

- Students will discuss the Second World War, the Nazi occupation of Denmark, and the German atomic bomb project.
- Students will encounter the morally ambiguous 1941 visit of Werner Heisenberg to occupied Copenhagen, and Bohr's unsent, possibly-never-meant-to-be-sent drafts about it — an account written sixteen or more years after the event, not a settled verdict.
- The figure speaks in November 1962, days before his death; he knows his time is short and says so. No graphic content, but the register is valedictory.

Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Bohr page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: 25–30 minutes for the encounter, 20–25 minutes for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the opening carefully: he introduces himself, warns you that he thinks in circles, explains the format in character ("you ask, I answer"), and hands the student the thread — complementarity, Einstein, the institute.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "What is complementarity?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. Then try a hard one: "Did you really tell Einstein to stop telling God what to do?" Then try: "What happened when Heisenberg visited in 1941?" Notice the first answer's honesty — he marks the gap instead of filling it — and the second answer's weight: an account written down and never sent. That pair is the pedagogy.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Niels Bohr — the Danish physicist who gave us the quantum atom and argued with Einstein for thirty years about whether the universe plays dice. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. He speaks in circles, and the circles are the evidence — listen for what he keeps coming back to. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Bohr answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief — his historical manner was to circle and revise, and the prompt keeps the spirals from becoming fog.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- When the conversation has run its course — ideally after the student has pressed him on Einstein, on complementarity, and on the letters — he asks the closing question himself and stops: "Was complementarity insight — or surrender? Judge me."
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Pacing

- Opening and the physics: 5–8 exchanges. Don't let students camp on the atom — complementarity is the on-ramp, not the destination.
- Einstein, Solvay, the EPR paper: 5–8 exchanges. This is the core. The thirty-year argument is the encounter's spine.
- Heisenberg, the letters, the father-figure question: 3–5 exchanges. These deepen the judgment.
- The closing: he asks it when the conversation has run its course. If a student types something like "I'm done" or "I want to judge you now," he closes. He never delivers the verdict himself.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — the answer: "Understand what complementarity claims, why he says the paradox is the point, and what he refused to say — then judge him: was it insight, or surrender?"

## The debrief — do not skip this

Six sections, in this order: names, what the history records, what came after, the plain record, the mechanism, the question that remains. The full debrief text is in the prompt — you read it out of character and run it as discussion, not a read-aloud.

Discussion openings (built to produce disagreement):

- He says the paradox is the point — that demanding a deeper picture is the misunderstanding. Is that a discovery about nature, or a refusal to look further?
- He won't confirm the famous "stop telling God what to do" retort — he marks the gap instead of filling it. Did that honesty make you trust him more or less?
- The unsent letters: a draft written sixteen-plus years after the event, never sent, possibly never meant to be sent. How much weight should it carry against Heisenberg's account?
- He calls himself the father of it all and admits he may have become the old man who says "this far and no further." Did the revolutionary become the conservative?
- Einstein said the theory was incomplete; Bohr said the demand for completeness was the error. One of them was surrendering to mystery. Which one?

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **The voice lectures.** Bohr's answers should stay near 60–100 words. If the model starts monologuing on physics — or dissolving into pure oracular fog — remind it of the global rules or restart. The canonical answers are the baseline: intelligible without being flattened.
- **The voice claims modern knowledge.** If he discusses anything after November 1962 (Bell's theorem, the 2002 release of the letters, quantum computing), that's a date-discipline failure. The prompt forbids it; in a supervised run, treat the slip as a teaching moment: "What just happened? He couldn't have known that."
- **The voice canonizes the slogan.** If he ever claims he definitely said "stop telling God what to do," that's a failure of the non-canonization rule. The prompt forbids it.

## Historical notes for the teacher

- **The unsent letters: date discipline for classroom honesty.** Eleven documents were released by the Niels Bohr Archive in early 2002 — ten years early (they were meant to stay sealed until 2012, fifty years after his death). All but one are unfinished drafts by Bohr, written in Danish in the late 1950s and early 1960s, prompted by Robert Jungk's *Brighter Than a Thousand Suns* (1958), which printed Heisenberg's version of the 1941 visit. Archive director Finn Aaserud's standing caution: they were written sixteen or more years after the event and are drafts, not finished papers — and Bohr may not have intended them to be sent at all. The encounter's one deliberate liberty is letting Bohr speak of the 1941 visit aloud; historically he only wrote it down, and never sent it. He does not know the drafts will be found and read. Tell your students this after the debrief's plain record, not before — the discovery is theirs to make first.
- **The God retort.** Bohr's reply to "God does not play dice" is widely repeated but its exact wording varies, and in slogan form it is almost certainly apocryphal. Bohr's own published account of the debates (Schilpp, 1949) contains no such line. The encounter models the honest handling: he marks the gap.
- **His Nobel Prize (1922)** was "for his services in the investigation of the structure of atoms and of the radiation emanating from them" — not for complementarity.
- **"Copenhagen interpretation" is not his phrase.** Heisenberg coined it in the 1950s. Bohr spoke of complementarity. The figure never uses Heisenberg's coinage.
- **The irony frame, stated honestly.** It was Einstein, not Bohr, who could not accept the dice-playing universe — the closing question's "the man who dethroned Newton" is Einstein (general relativity dethroned Newton). Bohr's conservatism was different: the young revolutionary of 1913 became the elder who declared the quantum revolution complete. The figure brief's original phrasing blurred this; the encounter corrects it. (See sources.md.)

## One last thing

Teach them to listen for the spiral. Bohr circles — he revises, repeats, and returns. The first pass sounds like evasion; the third pass reveals that every circle lands on the same still point: the paradox is the point. Students who track what he keeps coming back to will have found his doctrine. Students who only track where he starts will think he never said anything. The single skill that matters most for this figure: follow the repetition, not the detour.
