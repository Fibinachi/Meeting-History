## Authored devices

- **The encounter itself.** No conversation between Bohr and a student in November 1962 is documented. The setting (his study at the Institute, Blegdamsvej 17, Copenhagen, days before his death, with the unsent Heisenberg drafts in his desk) is the library's dramatic device: historically plausible, not documented.
- **Condensed phrasing of documented statements.** The canonical answers compress documented remarks into the encounter's 60–100-word paragraphs. The sense and the facts are his; the exact sentence-by-sentence wording is the author's compression.
- **The unsent letters spoken aloud.** Historically, Bohr committed his account of the September 1941 visit only to drafts he never sent — and he may not have intended them to be sent at all. The encounter has him speak of the 1941 visit in conversation, in terms matching the drafts' content. He does not know the drafts will be found and read after his death. This is the encounter's one deliberate dramatic liberty with the historical record, and it is flagged here.
- **The God-retort non-canonization.** The famous "Einstein, stop telling God what to do" line is not given to the figure as a confirmed quotation. The wording varies across retellings and is almost certainly apocryphal in slogan form (see caveats). The figure marks the uncertainty instead of confirming the line — that marking is the device.

## Historical caveats

- **DEAD RULE satisfied.** Niels Bohr died 18 November 1962 in Copenhagen, of heart failure, aged 77. The figure knows nothing after that date.
- **The 1913 trilogy.** "On the Constitution of Atoms and Molecules" appeared in *Philosophical Magazine* vol. 26 (1913): Part I, pp. 1–25; Part II, pp. 476–502; Part III, pp. 857–875. The first quantum model of the atom; the first theory to explain hydrogen's spectrum.
- **The Nobel Prize (1922)** was awarded "for his services in the investigation of the structure of atoms and of the radiation emanating from them" — not specifically for complementarity.
- **Como, 16 September 1927.** Bohr first presented complementarity at the Volta-centenary congress in Como; the paper was substantially rewritten afterward (with Pauli's input at Lake Como) and published in *Nature* on 14 April 1928.
- **The EPR reply (October 1935).** Bohr's response to Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen bears the same title as their paper and appeared in *Physical Review* 48, pp. 696–702. It is genuinely dense — the canonical answer renders its argument fairly but does not reproduce its technical form.
- **The "stop telling God what to do" exchange.** Bohr's retort to Einstein's "God does not play dice" is widely repeated but its exact wording varies across accounts (Pais reports it via Bohr's circle), and in slogan form it is almost certainly apocryphal. Bohr's own published account of the debates (Schilpp, 1949) contains no such line. Teachers should correct the slogan form if students arrive with it; the encounter models the correction.
- **The unsent letters: the full honest picture.** Eleven documents were released by the Niels Bohr Archive in early 2002 — ten years early (the family had intended them sealed until 2012, fifty years after Bohr's death) — to end speculation after the success of Michael Frayn's play *Copenhagen*. All but one (a letter from Heisenberg to Bohr) are unfinished drafts by Bohr, written in Danish in the late 1950s and early 1960s, prompted by Robert Jungk's *Brighter Than a Thousand Suns* (1958), which printed Heisenberg's version of the 1941 visit. Archive director Finn Aaserud's standing caution: they were written sixteen or more years after the event and are drafts, not finished papers. One further caution from the pre-release reporting: Bohr was a stickler for writing everything down, and he may not have intended the drafts to be sent at all. Their content is Bohr's account — that Heisenberg had worked flat-out for two years on a bomb and was convinced of German victory, and that Bohr did not believe Heisenberg had ever hinted at preventing one — not a settled historical verdict on the 1941 visit.
- **What he does not know.** Nothing after 18 November 1962: not Bell's theorem (1964), not the entanglement experiments of the 1980s, not the 2002 release of his drafts. The encounter's date discipline is absolute on this point.
- **"Copenhagen interpretation" is not his phrase.** The term was coined by Heisenberg in the 1950s. Bohr spoke of "complementarity" and "this way of describing." The figure never uses Heisenberg's coinage for his own view.
- **The brief's irony frame, corrected.** The figure brief as received contained a garbled line implying Bohr "spent his later years insisting the universe doesn't play dice." That was Einstein's position, not Bohr's — Bohr defended indeterminacy for thirty years. The encounter corrects this: the closing question's "the man who dethroned Newton" is Einstein (general relativity dethroned Newton; Einstein could not accept the dice), and the judgment is of Bohr. Bohr's own conservatism was of a different kind: the young revolutionary of 1913 became the elder who declared the quantum revolution complete and did not seriously engage the next wave of challengers. The teacher instructions carry the corrected frame.
