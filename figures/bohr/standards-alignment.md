# Meet Bohr — Standards Alignment (verified 2026-10-15)

Encounter: the student presses Niels Bohr (November 1962) on the 1913 quantum atom, the principle of complementarity announced at Como in 1927, his thirty-year argument with Einstein (Solvay 1927, the EPR paper of 1935), the unsent drafts about Heisenberg's 1941 visit, and whether the young revolutionary became the elder who declared the revolution complete. It closes with his scripted question: *was complementarity insight — or surrender? Judge him.*

**Grade band fit for every framework below: 9–12** (high school physics / ELA literacy). The encounter's science content sits in high-school physics; the source-evaluation and argumentation work sits in the 9–12 literacy and C3 bands.

---

## 1. C3 Framework (National Council for the Social Studies, 2013)

Grade band: **9–12**. (Indicator texts verified against independent curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim; the official NCSS PDF was not directly retrievable in this session — see verification note at end.)

### Dimension 2 — History

- **D2.His.4.9-12** — Analyze complex and interacting factors that influenced the perspectives of people during different historical eras. (Einstein's 1935 "dice" objection and Heisenberg's 1941 visit under German occupation are perspectives shaped by their eras — Weimar physics, the Third Reich, wartime Los Alamos.)
- **D2.His.6.9-12** — Analyze the ways in which the perspectives of those writing history shaped the history that they produced. (Bohr's own 1949 Schilpp essay is his telling of the Solvay debates; his unsent drafts are his account of 1941, written sixteen or more years after the event — the debrief flags the archive director's caution that they are drafts, not a verdict.)
- **D2.His.11.9-12** — Critique the usefulness of historical sources for a specific historical inquiry based on their maker, date, place of origin, intended audience, and purpose. (The Heisenberg drafts: written in Danish in the late 1950s–early 1960s, never sent, possibly never meant to be sent — the encounter has the student judge what such a source proves and doesn't.)
- **D2.His.16.9-12** — Integrate evidence from multiple relevant historical sources and interpretations into a reasoned argument about the past. (The closing judgment — insight or surrender — requires weighing the 1935 EPR reply, the Solvay record, and the drafts against each other.)

### Dimension 4 — Communicating Conclusions and Taking Informed Action

- **D4.1.9-12** — Construct arguments using precise and knowledgeable claims, with evidence from multiple sources, while acknowledging counterclaims and evidentiary weaknesses. (The judgment must acknowledge what the drafts don't settle — and that Bohr himself marks the gap on the famous God-retort rather than filling it.)
- **D4.4.9-12** — Critique the use of claims and evidence in arguments for credibility. (The load-bearing nudge after the Solvay answer: he won't confirm the famous retort — evaluating what he claims vs. what he refuses to claim is the exercise.)

## 2. Common Core ELA — Science & Technical Literacy and History Literacy

Grade band: **9–12**. (Verified against the official Common Core State Standards Initiative site, thecorestandards.org.)

### Reading in Science & Technical Subjects, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RST.11-12.1** — Cite specific textual evidence to support analysis of science and technical texts, attending to important distinctions the author makes and to any gaps or inconsistencies in the account. (The 1935 *Physical Review* reply to EPR — attending to his technical distinction between the particle and "the whole arrangement that observes it"; and the 2002-released drafts, whose gaps the archive director flags.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RST.11-12.2** — Determine the central ideas or conclusions of a text; summarize complex concepts, processes, or information presented in a text by paraphrasing them in simpler but still accurate terms. (Complementarity itself — "two descriptions that exclude each other and require each other" — is exactly such a paraphrase of the 1927 Como lecture.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RST.11-12.6** — Analyze the author's purpose in providing an explanation, describing a procedure, or discussing an experiment in a text, identifying important issues that remain unresolved. (Bohr's purpose in the 1935 EPR reply — defending the completeness of the quantum description — and the issues it leaves unresolved; the debrief notes the debates never fully closed.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.6** — Evaluate authors' differing points of view on the same historical event or issue by assessing the authors' claims, reasoning, and evidence. (Bohr's and Einstein's competing 1935 accounts of what quantum mechanics says about reality — the EPR paper and Bohr's same-titled reply.)

### Speaking & Listening, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.1** — Initiate and participate effectively in a range of collaborative discussions (one-on-one, in groups, and teacher-led) with diverse partners on grades 11–12 topics, texts, and issues, building on others' ideas and expressing their own clearly and persuasively. (The encounter is a sustained one-on-one examination of a witness; the student must press him the way an examiner presses a witness.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.3** — Evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric, assessing the stance, premises, links among ideas, word choice, points of emphasis, and tone used. (The debrief explicitly teaches students to notice his circling manner — and the moment he marks a gap instead of filling it.)

### Writing, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.W.11-12.1** — Write arguments to support claims in an analysis of substantive topics or texts, using valid reasoning and relevant and sufficient evidence. (The closing judgment — was complementarity insight or surrender — is designed as a post-encounter argument prompt.)

## 3. NGSS (Next Generation Science Standards, 2013)

Grade band: **9–12**. (Performance expectations verified via NSTA and commonplanner reproductions of the NGSS; Appendix H understandings via the official Appendix H text. The physics fit is genuine and tight: the encounter's central doctrine is literally a wave-vs-particle claim.)

### Disciplinary Core Ideas

- **HS-PS4-3** — Evaluate the claims, evidence, and reasoning behind the idea that electromagnetic radiation can be described either by a wave model or a particle model, and that for some situations one model is more useful than the other. (His canonical complementarity example is light itself — waves in one experiment, particles in another, the apparatus deciding which face appears. The DCI language — "The wave model is useful for explaining many features of electromagnetic radiation, and the particle model explains other features" — is the physics underneath his doctrine.)
- **HS-PS1-8** — Develop models to illustrate the changes in the composition of the nucleus of the atom and the energy released during the processes of fission, fusion, and radioactive decay. (The Los Alamos thread: he escaped occupied Denmark in 1943 and worked on the bomb project; the encounter's 1941-visit thread is the human entry to this physics.)

### Nature of Science (Appendix H) — where the closing question lives

- **9-12.AH.7.5** — Science and engineering are influenced by society and society is influenced by science and engineering. (The occupied-Denmark setting of the 1941 visit; the institute's young men scattered by the war.)
- **9-12.AH.8.2** — Science and technology may raise ethical issues for which science, by itself, does not provide answers and solutions. (The Heisenberg visit and the bomb; the question of what physicists on both sides owed.)
- **9-12.AH.8.3** — Science knowledge indicates what can happen in natural systems — not what should happen. (His philosophy canonical answer in reverse: "it is wrong to think the task of physics is to find out how nature is. Physics concerns what we can say about nature.")

### Practice

- **Science and Engineering Practice 7: Engaging in Argument from Evidence** (Appendix F) — including "construct, use, and/or present an oral and written argument or counterargument based on data and evidence." (The closing judgment is an evidence-based argument about a scientific doctrine's status.)
