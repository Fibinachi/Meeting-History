## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — say which.

- **The eighth session: late October 1858, the two men answering the student's questions together.** No such meeting occurred after Alton. The seven debates did happen and were transcribed; the re-staging is the library's standing dramatic device. Historically plausible in form (they shared platforms for three months), not documented as an event.
- **The setting date — after Alton (Oct 15), before the November 2 legislative elections.** Chosen deliberately so neither man knows the result. The debates, the canvass speeches, and all canonical positions are behind them; the votes are not.
- **The closing question asked by the modern voice.** Neither man, in October 1858, could ask what "right" cost — they did not know the war, the presidency, emancipation, or their own deaths. The tutorial states the question upfront; the modern voice asks it at the close. This is a deliberate departure from the single-figure form, forced by date discipline.
- **The turn-taking and response-round machinery** (addressing rules, paired answers, one short rejoinder each). Delivery mechanics, not content; adapted from the rhodes-and-watkins dialogue form.
- **Abridgment and paraphrase of the canonical answers.** The debate speeches ran to hours; the answers compress them to 60–100 words. Compression is authored; the substance, and the short verbatim phrases marked as such, are the men's.
- **The "personal measure" canonical** (what each man says of the other). Characterization drawn from the debates' substance and their documented twenty-four-year acquaintance — marked in the prompt as characterization, not quotation.
- **Accessible diction.** The 1858 debate texts are already plain English; less simplification was needed than for earlier figures. Where the prompt smooths period syntax, the substance is untouched.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Written before the figure ships, even the ones decided not to matter.

- **The transcripts were taken by partisan reporters and edited for publication.** Robert R. Hitt took shorthand for the Republican *Chicago Press and Tribune*; Henry Binmore and James B. Sheridan reported for the Democratic *Chicago Times*. Each paper's version favored its man — the *Tribune* smoothed Lincoln and roughened Douglas; the *Times* did the reverse, and sometimes softened Douglas's language (Holzer documents the *Times* changing his stump slur to "negro" in print). The 1860 book was a Republican campaign document compiled with Lincoln's participation. Harold Holzer's 1993 edition restored the complete unexpurgated text. The prompt's voices are faithful to the published texts, and the slur is never restored.
- **Did Douglas really use a racial slur on the stump?** The scholarly record (via Holzer) indicates his reporters softened his language in print. The encounter keeps the printed corpus vocabulary and never reproduces the slur, per the library blacklist. This is stated in the guardrails so no classroom discovers it by accident.
- **The "conspiracy" charge — did Pierce, Buchanan, Taney, and Douglas actually concert?** This was Lincoln's *argument* at Springfield (the "framed timbers" passage), not an established historical fact. Douglas denied it angrily and pointed to his open war with Buchanan over Lecompton. Historians treat it as brilliant rhetoric, not proven concert. The encounter presents it as Lincoln's charge and Douglas's denial — the student judges the argument, not the allegation's truth.
- **The legislature vote: 54–46 for Douglas, January 1859.** The exact day is given as January 5 in some sources and January 6 in others; the encounter and debrief say "January 1859" only. Republican legislative candidates won about 24,094 more votes statewide than Democratic ones — the apportionment, not the popular vote, chose the Senator. (Senators were chosen by legislatures until the 17th Amendment, 1913.)
- **"If slavery is not wrong, nothing is wrong" is an 1864 line** (letter to Albert G. Hodges, April 4, 1864), not 1858. The encounter uses Lincoln's 1858 Quincy formulation instead ("if you admit that it is wrong, he cannot logically say that anybody has a right to do wrong"). Do not let the figure backdate the 1864 line.
- **Lincoln's 1858 race views vs. his later record.** The figure is frozen in October 1858 and speaks his 1858 words — including the Charleston denial of social and political equality. His views changed across the 1860s (the library's single-figure *Meet Lincoln*, set in March 1865, carries that arc). The debrief states the 1858 position plainly and notes the freeze; the figure must not borrow his later words.
- **Douglas's Freeport answer and 1860.** The standard historical account holds that the Freeport Doctrine cost Douglas Southern support and helped split the Democratic Party in 1860. The figure, in October 1858, does not know this; it belongs in the debrief's "what came after," never in his voice.
- **Lecompton.** Douglas's break with the Buchanan administration over the Lecompton constitution (1857–58) is well documented and was his genuine position in the debates; it is also the strongest evidence against Lincoln's conspiracy charge, and the prompt lets him use it that way.
- **Colonization.** Lincoln favored voluntary colonization in the 1850s (Peoria, 1854) but did not raise it in the seven debates. The figure answers briefly from the 1850s position if asked and does not volunteer it — marked honestly in the prompt.
- **Debate format.** At each of the seven debates, one man opened for an hour, the other replied for ninety minutes, and the first had a thirty-minute rejoinder; Douglas, as incumbent, opened four times. Audiences numbered in the thousands (Freeport drew over 15,000 by some accounts).
- **"Judge Douglas."** Lincoln called Douglas "Judge Douglas" throughout — Douglas had been an Illinois judge early in his career. It is a corpus habit, not a current title; the prompt preserves it.
