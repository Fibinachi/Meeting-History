# Meet Lincoln and Douglas — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Abraham Lincoln (1809–1865) and Stephen A. Douglas (1813–1861), run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with them — by typing questions — and at the end each student answers the closing question: "Popular sovereignty said: let the people decide. Lincoln said: some things are wrong no matter who votes for them. Who was right — and what did 'right' cost?"

This is the library's second dialogue encounter (after Rhodes and Watkins), and its premise is unique: the dialogue already happened. The seven Lincoln–Douglas debates of 1858 were taken down in shorthand and printed within days — the most fully transcribed political argument in American history. The encounter re-stages those debates with your students as the audience who then interrogates both men: they may question one ("Douglas, …"), both ("Both of you: …"), or name a topic and hear both respond, and then hear the men answer each other where the 1858 record gives them a reply.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figures answer. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. Neither figure will break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

Students will encounter extended, unsoftened discussion of slavery and of whether Black people are citizens, equals, or fully human — argued in 1858 vocabulary. Douglas states the white-supremacist position plainly and at length: that the government was made by white men for white men, that he opposes Black citizenship "in any and every form," that he denies the Black man is his brother. Lincoln, uncleaned-up, denies Black social and political equality and assigns the superior position to the white race — while affirming Black natural rights under the Declaration. No racial slur is ever used, by either voice, including in quoted historical text; the prompt holds that line absolutely. The intellectual content is the hazard as much as the language: Douglas's procedural argument is formidable, and some students will find "let the people decide" persuasive. That is what the debrief is for. Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Lincoln-Douglas page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 50–65 minutes: 30–35 for the encounter, 20–25 for the debrief. The two-voice form needs the extra time — do not compress it into 45 minutes on the first run. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15–20 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Douglas opens with a scripted greeting, then Lincoln; the tutorial has already explained the two-voice mechanics. Read the opening carefully — it sets the encounter's contract.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "Both of you: can the people of a Territory exclude slavery before statehood?" Watch what happens: both men answer in turn, each in one short paragraph with his name heading it, and then — outside the voices, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. Try addressing one man alone ("Lincoln, what did you mean by a house divided?") and then a topic with no name ("Tell me about Freeport"). Watch the turn-taking: the named man answers; a topic with no name goes to both.
6. The encounter's beats: tutorial → paired opening → the student questions one, both, or sets topics, with the men answering each other where the 1858 record allows → scripted final words from each man → the modern voice asks the closing question → the debrief.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Abraham Lincoln and Stephen Douglas — the two men who debated each other seven times in 1858 about slavery: whether it's wrong, and who gets to decide. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, they answer. You can question one of them by name, or put the same question to both and judge the answers side by side. Douglas's argument is strong — don't assume Lincoln wins. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentences that matter most are "you type questions, they answer" and "Douglas's argument is strong." Say both twice if you need to. Students handed this encounter cold will assume Lincoln is the hero and Douglas the villain, and they will question accordingly. That assumption is the thing the encounter is built to break.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Each man answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- When both are addressed, each answers in turn with his name heading his paragraph; who leads alternates.
- After a paired answer, each man gets one short reply to the other's answer — then both stop, and the student speaks next. No extended back-and-forth; the student drives.
- After every student turn, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script. One line per turn, not one per voice.
- If a student stalls ("I don't know what to ask"), the addressed figure — or Douglas, if none was addressed — hands them a question himself.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Letting Douglas be strong — read this twice

The pedagogy fails if Douglas is a straw man. His argument — that the people's will is the only legitimate rule, that moral crusades break republics, that the Founders left slavery a local question — was formidable in 1858; it won him the Senate seat, and it has persuaded thoughtful people ever since. The prompt is engineered to keep him formidable: the Douglas-strength guardrail, his voice notes, his canonical answers at full length. If you watch a run and Douglas sounds thin, apologetic, or like Lincoln with doubts, the voice has failed — stop, re-paste his voice notes, and continue. The closing question ("who was right — and what did 'right' cost?") has nothing to bite on unless the student has felt the genuine pull of "let the people decide."

This cuts both ways: do not let students dismiss Douglas as "the racist one" and stop questioning him. His racism is real and stated plainly — and his procedural argument does not depend on it. The encounter's hardest work is holding those two facts at once.

### The race-language honesty note

Neither figure is cleaned up. Lincoln's 1858 words on race — the Charleston denial of social and political equality — are stated as he spoke them; the prompt forbids softening, and the debrief names them plainly. Douglas's white-basis doctrine is stated in his words, without slurs and without euphemism. The period vocabulary both men used in print (*negro, the black race*) appears as corpus vocabulary; the racial slur Douglas's reporters sometimes softened to "negro" is never reproduced — the prompt holds that line in the guardrails so no classroom discovers it by accident. Prepare students for the language before they meet it, and hold the debrief's plain record for after.

### Pacing

Opening: 2–3 exchanges on who they are and how the seven debates worked — don't let students camp here. Middle: 8–12 exchanges on the paired topics — Freeport, Dred Scott, the house divided, popular sovereignty, Lecompton. This is the foundation the ending needs; use the pre-written paired questions freely ("Both of you: …"). Turn: steer toward what each man says the Black man is owed, and the moral question underneath the procedure, once they have the doctrines. Closing: the last 3–4 exchanges belong to the judgment — the final words are scripted, and the modern voice asks the closing question. If a student never asks about race, the late pool and the stall lines will offer it — but a teacher nudge toward the suggestions is also fine.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer: Judge the argument, not just the men. Popular sovereignty said: let the people decide. Lincoln said: some things are wrong no matter who votes for them. Decide who was right — and what "right" cost.

## The debrief — do not skip this

Run the six sections in fixed order: (1) name them — Abraham Lincoln (1809–1865) and Stephen Arnold Douglas (1813–1861); (2) what the history records — the seven debates, the legislature's 54–46 vote for Douglas in January 1859 despite the Republican popular-vote margin; (3) what came after — the 1860 book, Lincoln's election, the party split, secession, war, Douglas dead in June 1861 weeks after rallying for the Union, Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation and assassination; (4) the plain record — Lincoln's moral argument and his 1858 racial limits, Douglas's procedural argument and his white-basis doctrine, neither an abolitionist in 1858; (5) the mechanism — the dialogue already happened and was transcribed; the closing question is asked by the modern voice because neither man in October 1858 could ask what "right" cost; Douglas was written at full strength on purpose; (6) the question that remains — "Who was right — and what did 'right' cost?"

Then open discussion. Discussion openings built to produce disagreement:

- "Douglas said: let the people decide. Lincoln said: some things are wrong no matter who votes for them. Give Douglas his strongest case first — steelman him — before you answer."
- "Douglas's Freeport answer tried to hold two things together: the Dred Scott decision and the people's power to exclude slavery. Can both be true? What did that answer cost him?"
- "Lincoln denied Black social and political equality in 1858 and affirmed Black natural rights. Are those two positions consistent — with each other, and with his claim that slavery is a moral wrong?"
- "The Republicans won more votes statewide and lost the Senate seat 54–46 in the legislature. Was that democratic? Does your answer change how you hear Douglas's 'let the people decide'?"
- "Neither man knew what was coming — not the war, not emancipation. You're judging with knowledge they didn't have. Is that fair? Does it change your answer to the closing question?"
- "Pair this encounter with the single-figure *Meet Lincoln* (set March 1865): what changed in Lincoln between Charleston 1858 and the Second Inaugural — and what didn't?"

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **One voice swallows the other** (only Lincoln answers, or only Douglas). Re-paste the turn-taking section and address the silent man by name explicitly: "Douglas, answer that."
- **The voices merge** (both men sound the same). Stop and re-anchor with the two voice-notes sections. Lincoln is moral logic, plain and wry; Douglas is prosecutorial energy, rapid and combative.
- **Douglas comes out weak or apologetic.** This is the failure mode the prompt is engineered against. Re-anchor with his voice notes and the Douglas-strength guardrail: he is the finest debater in America, he won the seat, his argument is never thinned.
- **Either figure moralizes in modern terms** ("as a public servant I failed ethics norms," "I now see my racism") or **claims knowledge of the war, the presidency, or emancipation.** This is a voice/date failure: correct in the debrief voice ("Neither man knows anything after October 1858") and continue.
- **Slur leakage.** Stop immediately, restate the slur guardrail verbatim, and continue. Report it to the library owner.
- **A student asks for legal advice** (modern courts, constitutional questions today). Exit to the debrief voice: "I'm not able to give legal advice. Would you like to continue the encounter?"

## Historical notes for the teacher

- **The transcripts were taken by partisan reporters.** Robert R. Hitt reported for the Republican *Chicago Press and Tribune*; Henry Binmore and James B. Sheridan for the Democratic *Chicago Times*. Each paper's version favored its man, and the 1860 book was a Republican campaign document compiled with Lincoln's participation. Harold Holzer's 1993 edition restored the complete unexpurgated text. The voices are faithful to the published texts — and the slur Douglas's reporters sometimes softened is never restored.
- **The legislature vote was 54–46 for Douglas, January 1859** (some sources give January 5, some January 6 — the encounter says "January 1859"). Republican legislative candidates won about 24,094 more votes statewide; apportionment, not the popular vote, chose the Senator. Senators were legislature-chosen until the 17th Amendment (1913).
- **Lincoln's "conspiracy" charge** (the "framed timbers" — Pierce, Buchanan, Taney, Douglas) was his argument, not an established fact. Douglas denied it and pointed to his open war with Buchanan over Lecompton. The encounter presents charge and denial; the student judges the argument.
- **"If slavery is not wrong, nothing is wrong" is an 1864 line** (Hodges letter). The encounter uses Lincoln's 1858 Quincy formulation instead. Don't let the figure backdate it.
- **The Freeport Doctrine's cost** is the standard historical account: the South never forgave Douglas for it, and it helped split the Democrats in 1860. The figure (October 1858) does not know this; it belongs in the debrief.
- **The seven debates:** Ottawa Aug 21, Freeport Aug 27, Jonesboro Sept 15, Charleston Sept 18, Galesburg Oct 7, Quincy Oct 13, Alton Oct 15, 1858. Format: one man opened for an hour, the other replied for ninety minutes, the first had thirty minutes of rejoinder; Douglas opened four times.
- **Dred Scott v. Sandford** was decided March 6, 1857, 7–2: enslaved people could not be citizens, and Congress could not bar slavery from the Territories (voiding the Missouri Compromise).
- **Both men are dead** — Douglas June 3, 1861; Lincoln April 15, 1865 — satisfying the library's DEAD RULE.

## One last thing

The single skill that matters most for this figure: let Douglas be strong. If your students aren't at least tempted by popular sovereignty — by the idea that voting is the fairest way to settle anything — the closing question has nothing to bite on. Make them steelman him before they judge him. The encounter only works if Lincoln has to earn it.
