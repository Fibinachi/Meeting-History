DEAD RULE: satisfied. Richard Nixon died April 22, 1994, in New York, four days after suffering a stroke on April 18, 1994.

## What is Nixon's own words vs. what is the record around him

- **Nixon's own words:** the tapes (recorded, unguarded); the Public Papers (speeches, statements, the resignation address); RN (his memoir — his case, not fact); the Frost interviews (his admissions and denials, on camera). The voice's diction, cadence, and both registers come from these.
- **Around him, not his:** the House Judiciary Committee's impeachment proceedings; the special prosecutor's case; the Senate Watergate Committee testimony; Haldeman's and Dean's testimony; Goldwater and Scott's count of Senate votes; the expert panel's findings on the 18½-minute gap; later historians' verdicts. The prompt uses these for facts Nixon could state (dates, the vote count as he knew it), but the voice never claims them as his own vindication — and the debrief, not the figure, states the plain record.
- **Notably thin in Nixon's own corpus:** he never, anywhere, conceded criminality for Watergate. The Frost line — concede "a cover-up," deny "a crime" — is the furthest he ever went, and the prompt holds him exactly there. Where he lies or evades on the tapes (the "lapse of memory" statement), the prompt marks his claim and lets the suggestion machinery point the student at the tape.

## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — say which.

- **The meeting itself (a single sitting at San Clemente, summer 1978)** — pure dramatic device. No such interview occurred in this form.
- **Compression of a lifetime into an hour** — he speaks on events across thirty years as present-tense memory. Dramatic compression.
- **The two-register voice device** — the orator and the tapes' unguarded voice both come from his recorded words, but the encounter deliberately juxtaposes them so the student can hear both. The figure never remarks on the shift; that is the design.
- **The scripted June-23 and Watergate answers' phrasing** — the boundaries they mark (what the record supports vs. what he claimed) are documented; the sentences themselves are authored. His Frost admissions are paraphrased, not verbatim, to keep the boundary honest.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Written before the figure ships, even the ones that don't matter.

- **Did Nixon order the Watergate break-in?** There is no evidence that he knew of or ordered it in advance. The grand jury did not indict him for the break-in. The accepted record is that his criminal exposure was the cover-up, not the burglary. The prompt is honest about this — the figure states it, and the teacher should know it is the record, not spin.
- **What did the June 23, 1972 tape actually show?** Nixon approved Haldeman's plan to have the CIA tell the FBI to back off the Watergate investigation on false national-security grounds. Nixon's later position was that this was political containment, not obstruction. The House Judiciary Committee treated it as obstruction of justice (Article I). The prompt presents his account in voice and the record in the debrief; the student weighs them.
- **The "lapse of memory" statement (August 5, 1974).** When the tape was released, Nixon said he had misremembered when he was first told of White House involvement in the burglaries. The prompt has the figure repeat the claim as his own; the teacher should know it is widely treated as a falsehood, which is why the nudge exists.
- **The 18½-minute gap (June 20, 1972).** A court-appointed expert panel found the gap was the result of deliberate erasure performed by hand, in at least five segments. Its contents are unknown. Nixon claimed not to know what was discussed. The figure marks the gap and does not fill it.
- **"Peace with honor."** His phrase, from his own speeches. The Paris Accords (January 1973) ended America's combat role; the war continued, and Saigon fell in April 1975 — while Nixon was alive. The prompt lets him claim the ending; the debrief notes the gap.
- **The memoir is self-serving in places.** Stated plainly in the prompt's SOURCE DOCUMENTS: RN is his case, not fact. The encounter is built so the tapes' Nixon and the memoir's Nixon sit side by side.
- **The pardon (September 8, 1974).** Ford's pardon spared him prosecution; it was not an exoneration and the figure is forbidden from treating it as one — by his own logic, he said so himself.
- **Aphorisms and famous lines** ("Let me make this perfectly clear," "I am not a crook," "You won't have Nixon to kick around anymore," "a man is not finished when he is defeated…") are from his recorded public statements and press conferences — public domain, on the record. The prompt uses them sparingly and attributes nothing to him he did not say.
- **Coarse language on the tapes.** The tapes contain profanity and period bigotry. The encounter paraphrases the coarseness and never reproduces slurs (blacklist). The figure's second register signals the tapes' tone without quoting it.
