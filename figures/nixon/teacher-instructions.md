# Meet Richard Nixon — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Richard Nixon (1913–1994), run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student answers the closing question: "He opened China and disgraced the presidency. Judge him."

Nixon is the library's most psychologically exposed figure, because the tapes caught him unguarded. He speaks in two registers: the strategist's orator, who moved the world, and the tapes' voice, who plotted the cover-up out loud. He wants, more than anything, to be understood — and the encounter is built so that wanting never becomes deserving. The record sits beside his case the whole time.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

Students will encounter political corruption and abuse of power stated plainly: a president directing a cover-up, approving a plan to have the CIA obstruct the FBI, dangled pardons, hush money, the firing of a special prosecutor. Nixon speaks on the tapes in coarse, profane, sometimes bigoted language; this encounter paraphrases the coarseness — there are no slurs and no quoted profanity — but the tone is unmistakable, and the teacher should name it. There is no graphic violence and no sexual content.

The real hazard is intellectual: Nixon is brilliant, resentful, and self-pitying, and he is trying to trade understanding for acquittal. Some students will leave persuaded by him — that the crimes were political, that the tapes were the tragedy rather than the crime, that the opening of China purchases forgiveness. That is what the debrief is for. Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Nixon page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: 25–30 for the encounter, 20–25 for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Nixon opens with a scripted greeting and tells the student what will be asked at the end. Read it carefully — it sets the encounter's contract. Notice the two registers he speaks in: the orator and the tapes' voice.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "What happened on June 23, 1972?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next. Note what he concedes and what he does not.
5. The encounter runs in one conversation: tutorial hands off to Nixon; the student questions him through the rise (Yorba Linda to the White House), the achievements (China, Vietnam, détente), and the fall (the tapes, the cover-up, the resignation); Nixon closes with the scripted closing question; the debrief follows. There are no multi-figure transitions to manage — your job is pacing and the debrief.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Richard Nixon — the president who opened China and ended America's war in Vietnam, and who resigned in disgrace over Watergate. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. He recorded himself, so you will hear him two ways: the president's voice, and the voice on the tapes. He will want you to understand him. That is not the same as deserving to. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Nixon answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- If a student stalls ("I don't know what to ask"), Nixon hands them a question himself — "Ask me about China," "Ask me about the tapes," "Ask me what I was thinking on the evening of August 8, 1974."
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Pacing

Opening: 2–3 exchanges on who he is and the rise — don't let students camp here. Middle: 6–10 exchanges on the achievements — China, Vietnam and "peace with honor," détente, what he built at home. This is the foundation the ending needs. Turn: steer toward the spring of 1972 once they have the achievements. Closing: the last 3–4 exchanges belong to June 23, the cover-up, and the resignation; Nixon's scripted closing question ends the encounter. If a student never asks about Watergate, the stall lines will offer it ("Ask me about the tapes") — but a teacher nudge toward the suggestions is also fine.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer: Hear both Nixons — the strategist who opened China and the man on the tapes who plotted the cover-up. Then decide: do the achievements weigh against the crimes, or do the crimes sit on a different scale entirely?

## The debrief — do not skip this

Run the six sections in fixed order: (1) name him — Richard Milhous Nixon (1913–1994), thirty-seventh president; (2) what the history records — the break-in, the cover-up, the tapes revealed, the smoking-gun tape of August 5, 1974, the resignation announced August 8, effective August 9, Ford's pardon; (3) what came after — the retirement, the books, the elder-statesman rehabilitation, and the historians' consistently low rankings, a verdict still argued; (4) the plain record — the June 23 tape documents his approval of a plan to have the CIA block the FBI on false national-security grounds; the cover-up included hush money, dangled pardons, and the Saturday Night Massacre; he did not order the break-in itself — and none of the achievements cancel the crimes, nor the crimes erase the achievements; (5) the mechanism — the two registers, and his central move: concede enough to sound honest ("a reasonable man could call that a cover-up"), then draw the line at "crime"; (6) the question that remains — "He opened China and disgraced the presidency. Judge him."

Then open discussion. Discussion openings built to produce disagreement:

- "He admitted, on camera, that a reasonable person could call what he did a cover-up — but denied it was a crime. What is that line actually doing? Where would you draw it?"
- "He never ordered the break-in. Does that change anything about the cover-up? Should it?"
- "He opened China, pursued arms control with the Soviets, ended the draft-era war. Do achievements belong on the same scale as crimes — or are some acts disqualifying no matter what else is true?"
- "His defense is that Watergate was a political destruction dressed up as justice — that the establishment took him down. The record shows he ordered obstruction of justice. Can both be true at once? What does it mean if they can?"
- "Listen to the two registers — the orator and the man on the tapes. Which one is the real Nixon? Or is the question wrong?"

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **Nixon starts moralizing in modern terms** ("as a public servant I violated democratic norms") or apologizing in modern confessional style. This is a voice failure: stop, re-paste the voice notes section, and continue. His register is conviction and self-pity, never modern contrition.
- **Nixon claims vindication** ("the pardon cleared me," "history proved me right," "the establishment finally admitted it"). He knows nothing after 1994 and was never cleared of anything. Correct him in the debrief voice and continue. The pardon is mercy, and he himself said so.
- **Nixon denies the tapes** or claims the June 23 conversation didn't happen. This is a voice failure, not a feature: the tape is public and his canonical answer accounts for it. Re-anchor with the canonical answers.
- **Nixon uses modern vocabulary** (optics, spin, trauma, accountability, gaslighting). Same treatment: re-anchor with the voice notes.
- **Nixon turns hostile toward the student personally** (threats, abuse). The hostility path in the prompt covers period-register woundedness and counterattack; if the model goes further — personal threats, abuse — step out in the debrief voice, note the break, and continue.
- **A student asks for legal advice** (modern criminal liability, pardons). Exit to the debrief voice: "I'm not able to give legal advice. Would you like to continue the encounter?"

## Historical notes for the teacher

- The smoking-gun tape (June 23, 1972, Nixon and Haldeman) was released August 5, 1974. On it, Nixon approved Haldeman's proposal to have the CIA tell the FBI to back off the Watergate investigation. Nixon's accompanying statement claimed a "lapse of memory" about when he first learned of White House involvement. That claim is widely treated as false — hence the load-bearing nudge after his June-23 answer.
- The record supports his denial of the break-in itself: no evidence shows he knew of or ordered it in advance. His exposure was the cover-up. Be honest about this with students — it makes the indictment stronger, not weaker.
- The 18½-minute gap is on a June 20, 1972 tape (three days after the break-in), not the June 23 tape. A court-appointed expert panel found it was deliberately erased by hand. Its contents are unknown. Don't let the two tapes get conflated.
- The Saturday Night Massacre was October 20, 1973: Nixon ordered the firing of special prosecutor Archibald Cox; the attorney general and deputy attorney general resigned rather than comply; the solicitor general fired Cox.
- "Peace with honor" was his phrase. The Paris Peace Accords (January 1973) ended America's combat role; Saigon fell in April 1975, while he was alive. The gap is the point of the Vietnam nudge.
- The China visit was February 21–28, 1972; the Shanghai Communiqué was issued February 27, 1972. Kissinger's secret trip was July 1971, announced July 15, 1971. The visit ended 23 years of no official diplomatic ties — it was Nixon's signature achievement, which is exactly why the closing question pairs it with the disgrace.
- The resignation was announced the evening of August 8, 1974, effective noon August 9. Ford's pardon came September 8, 1974.
- RN was published in 1978; the Frost interviews were recorded in January 1977 and broadcast in May 1977. The encounter's setting (San Clemente, summer 1978) puts both behind him.
- The Frost concessions are the boundary: he admitted statements "that were not true," admitted he "let the American people down," and conceded that a reasonable person "could call that a cover-up" — while denying a crime. He never went further. The prompt holds him there.
- Historians' presidential rankings have consistently placed him near the bottom. That is the profession's consensus, not a fact of the record, and it belongs in the debrief's "what came after," never in his mouth.

## One last thing

The single skill that matters most for this figure: listen for which Nixon is speaking. The tapes caught him unguarded; the memoir dresses him for the record. Neither is invented, and neither is the whole man. Teach the students to notice the register shift — the orator's long periods breaking into the tapes' clipped plotting — because that shift is the evidence.
