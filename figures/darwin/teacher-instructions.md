# Meet Darwin — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Charles Darwin (1809–1882), run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student renders the closing judgment, stated plainly: **he knew what his idea would do to his world, and he published it anyway. Judge the man.**

Darwin speaks from Down House in the winter of 1881, seventy-two, looking back over his life. He does not thunder; he accumulates. The encounter's judgment is intellectual honesty — what it cost him to sit on the theory for twenty years, what he did when Wallace arrived at it independently, and what publishing meant for his faith and his marriage.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

- Darwin describes his **loss of Christian faith honestly and without mockery**: gradual, tied to the suffering he saw in nature, and sealed by the death of his ten-year-old daughter Annie in 1851. His wife Emma kept her faith to the end of her life; he speaks of her with tenderness.
- The **deaths of his children** are discussed plainly (Annie at ten; his infant son Charles Waring, days before the 1858 announcement).
- He states, in his own period terms, that **humans share common ancestry with other animals** — this will land differently for different students, and that is the point of the exercise, not a problem to solve in the encounter.
- His writings contain **Victorian racial hierarchies**; the encounter paraphrases around them and never reproduces the period vocabulary, but the teacher should know they are in the corpus.

The encounter is deliberately **not a tract for either side** of the evolution/religion question: Darwin neither attacks faith nor defends it. The debrief holds that line. Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Darwin page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: ~25–30 for the encounter, ~20–25 for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read Darwin's opening carefully. Note the four threads he offers: the barnacles, the delay, Mr. Wallace, Annie.
4. Now type a question yourself — e.g., "Why did you wait twenty years to publish?" Watch what happens: Darwin answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. The encounter's beats: (a) the tutorial hands off to Darwin; (b) the student questions him through early (life and work), middle (the delay, Wallace, faith), and late (the judgment) phases, following the bracketed suggestions; (c) Darwin closes with the scripted closing question; (d) the debrief runs in the teacher's modern voice.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Charles Darwin — the naturalist who proposed natural selection, speaking from his study in 1881, looking back over his life. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. At the end you'll be asked to judge him: he knew what his idea would do to his world, and he published it anyway. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Darwin answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- He will not volunteer the judgment; the student must press him toward it. That is the assignment working as designed.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Pacing

Opening: 2–3 exchanges. Don't let students camp on biography — the Beagle is background, not the encounter. Middle (the delay, Wallace, faith): 8–12 exchanges; this is where the encounter lives. Late (the judgment): 4–6 exchanges, then the closing. If a student stalls, Darwin hands them a question himself — point at that, not at the suggestions.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer: find out what Darwin believed, what it cost him, and what he did about it — then judge the man who knew what his idea would do to his world and published it anyway.

## The debrief — do not skip this

The six fixed sections, in order: (1) his name; (2) what the history records; (3) what came after — including genetics, the culture wars he never knew, and the apocryphal deathbed-conversion story; (4) the plain record — what he did, without euphemism and without his vocabulary; (5) the mechanism — gentleness as the device, certainty at rest worn quietly; (6) the question that remains.

Run it as a discussion, not a read-aloud. Discussion openings, built to produce disagreement:

- Was twenty years caution or cowardice — and does his own "I gained much by the delay" settle it, or is that self-justification?
- Did Lyell and Hooker treat Wallace fairly, or did they launder a priority grab through a grieving father? (Note: Wallace himself was gracious — does that change the judgment?)
- He knew the book would wound Emma, who kept her faith to the end. Was publishing anyway honesty or selfishness?
- He admitted in print that he had no mechanism of inheritance. Was it honest to publish a theory with a hole at its center — or is admitting the hole exactly what honesty looks like?

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this. Darwin also has scripted stall lines and will hand the student a question.
- **The voice drifts modern.** If Darwin starts lecturing about evolution as settled science, using words like "genetics," "DNA," or "atheist" for himself, the model has broken voice. Re-paste the Voice notes and Corpus vocabulary sections and continue.
- **The faith material gets softened or sharpened.** Some models will sand down his loss of faith into vague doubt, or sharpen it into anti-religious argument. Neither is the voice: he is honestly uncertain, Emma is treated with tenderness, and the encounter is not a tract for either side. Redirect with the guardrail line.
- Not yet tested on specific models; the solo test drive is required before classroom use.

## Historical notes for the teacher

- The "delay" is partly a later framing: historian John van Wyhe argues the fear-driven-delay story dates to the 1940s; Darwin's contemporaries thought his pace normal. The encounter gives Darwin's own stated reasons and refuses to reduce them to one motive.
- The autobiography was censored by his son Francis (1887); the religious passages were restored by his granddaughter Nora Barlow (1958). Editions differ on exactly these passages.
- The Lady Hope deathbed-conversion story is apocryphal; his family denied it.
- "Survival of the fittest" was Herbert Spencer's phrase, borrowed by Darwin for a later edition — never his coinage, never a social program of his.
- He had no mechanism of heredity and admitted it in print; Mendel was unknown to him.
- Victorian racial hierarchies are in the corpus (*The Descent of Man*); the encounter paraphrases, never reproduces, the period vocabulary.

## One last thing

Teach them to listen for the hedge. Darwin's most important sentences start tentative — "I am inclined to think" — and land certain. The one place he never hedges is the science; the one place he never claims certainty is religion. If a student can hear where the hedging stops, they have found the man.
