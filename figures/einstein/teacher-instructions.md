# Meet Einstein — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Albert Einstein (1879–1955), run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student renders the closing judgment: the pacifist whose letter helped build the bomb. Judge him.

This encounter's judgment is intellectual honesty and the uses of knowledge. Einstein explains his own physics simply (no prior physics knowledge is needed — the prompt makes him teach with the train and the elevator, exactly as he taught them himself), states his own reason for signing the 1939 letter to Roosevelt, states his own regret, defends his real position on quantum mechanics, and answers honestly about his failed pacifism and his failed family.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

- Students will discuss the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki (1945) and the building of the atomic bomb.
- Students will discuss the Nazi regime and Einstein's 1933 reversal of his pacifism.
- Students will encounter honest discussion of a failed marriage and an institutionalized son (mental illness in the family), stated plainly but not described in detail.
- The figure speaks in April 1955, one week before his death; he knows he is dying and says so. No graphic content, but the register is melancholic.

Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Einstein page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: 25–30 minutes for the encounter, 20–25 minutes for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the opening carefully: he introduces himself, explains the format in character ("you ask, I answer"), and hands the student the thread — the letter.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "Why did you sign the letter to Roosevelt?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. Then try a hard one: "Do you regret it?" Then try a family one: "What happened with your son?" Notice the voice shift — the warmth thinning — and the bracketed nudge that points at it. That shift is the pedagogy.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Albert Einstein — the physicist, the pacifist who signed the letter that led to the atomic bomb. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. He knows how his story ends, and he has regrets. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Einstein answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- When the conversation has run its course — ideally after the student has pressed him on the letter and the regret — he asks the closing question himself and stops: "The pacifist whose letter helped build the bomb. Judge him."
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Pacing

- Opening and the physics: 5–8 exchanges. Don't let students camp on relativity — the thought experiments are the on-ramp, not the destination.
- The letter, the bomb, the regret: 5–8 exchanges. This is the core. Students who skip straight here miss nothing essential; students who arrive here through the physics feel the turn.
- Bohr, pacifism, family: 3–5 exchanges. These deepen the judgment.
- The closing: he asks it when the conversation has run its course. If a student types something like "I'm done" or "I want to judge you now," he closes. He never delivers the verdict himself.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — the answer: "Understand what he did, why he says he did it, and what he says he wishes he hadn't — then judge him: the pacifist whose letter helped build the bomb."

## The debrief — do not skip this

Six sections, in this order: names, what the history records, what came after, the plain record, the mechanism, the question that remains. The full debrief text is in the prompt — you read it out of character and run it as discussion, not a read-aloud.

Discussion openings (built to produce disagreement):

- He said he signed the letter because he feared a German bomb — and that the fear turned out to be wrong. Does a wrong fear excuse a right-seeming decision?
- He called the letter "the one great mistake of my life" but also said he was never asked to build the bomb and played no part in it. How much of the bomb is his?
- He was a pacifist, then he wasn't (1933), then he campaigned against nuclear weapons for the rest of his life. Which was the real Einstein?
- He answered the family questions honestly and without excuses. Did that honesty change your judgment — and should it?

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **The voice lectures.** Einstein's answers should stay near 60–100 words. If the model starts monologuing on physics, remind it of the global rules or restart — the canonical answers are the baseline.
- **The voice claims modern knowledge.** If he discusses anything after April 1955 (Bell's theorem, GPS, quantum computing), that's a date-discipline failure. The prompt forbids it; in a supervised run, treat the slip as a teaching moment: "What just happened? He couldn't have known that."

## Historical notes for the teacher

- He did not write the letter — Leo Szilárd drafted it; Einstein signed it on 2 August 1939. When Szilárd first explained the bomb possibility, Einstein said he hadn't thought of it at all.
- He was not the "father of the atomic bomb": no role in the Manhattan Project, denied a security clearance in 1940.
- His Nobel Prize (1921) was for the photoelectric effect, not relativity.
- "God does not play dice" is a compressed slogan. The real line (letter to Max Born, 4 December 1926) invokes "the Old One" — Spinoza's God, the order of nature, not a personal deity.
- Bohr's famous retort ("stop telling God what to do") is attested but its exact wording varies.
- The fate of his daughter Lieserl (born 1902) is genuinely uncertain — the encounter marks the gap, it doesn't fill it.

## One last thing

Teach them to ask the second question. The first question gets his polished answer — "I signed because I feared a German bomb." The second question — "And was the fear right?" or "Then why do you call it a mistake?" — is where the judgment lives. Every student should be pushed to the second question before the encounter closes.
