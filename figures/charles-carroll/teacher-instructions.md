# Meet Charles Carroll of Carrollton — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Charles Carroll of Carrollton (1737–1832), run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student answers the closing question: **the richest man in the Revolution risked everything for it. What was he buying? Judge him.**

Carroll speaks from Baltimore in 1831, at ninety-four, the last living signer of the Declaration of Independence. He is polished, courteous, and absolutely certain about 1776. The pedagogy is the contrast: his courtesy is the weapon and the hiding place. Students must decide whether his certainty survived 1832.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Tier

**OPEN.** No slurs (Dulany's anti-Catholic attack is paraphrased, never reproduced), no sexual content, no graphic violence. The encounter's hard material is the moral judgment itself: a slaveholder discussing slavery without condemning himself, and a fortune risked for a republic that did not free his slaves. Suitable for open classroom use with the debrief.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

- Students will discuss slavery as practiced by a man who owned hundreds of people — plainly, in his own vocabulary ("slaves," "property"). He calls slavery "a great evil" while stating, without apology, that he never freed his people in his lifetime.
- Students will discuss 18th-century religious bigotry: Maryland's anti-Catholic penal laws (Catholics barred from voting, office, and the practice of law), and Dulany's personal attack on Carroll's faith — paraphrased, never quoted with the slur.
- No violence and no sexual content. The debrief names plainly what his courtesy conceals.

Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Charles Carroll page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: ~25 minutes for the encounter, ~20 minutes for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read his opening carefully — it hands the student three threads: the First Citizen letters, what he risked in '76, and fifty-five years of the republic.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "Who were you, and what did you risk in 1776?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. The encounter's beats: **Opening** (2–3 exchanges — identity, France, the barred Catholic). **Middle** (~8–12 exchanges — the Dulany debate, the signing, the fortune, the slaves). **Late** (2–3 exchanges — the republic at ninety-four, then ask him to close). When a student types "ask him to close" (or equivalent), he asks the closing question and stops. The debrief follows.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with the last surviving signer of the Declaration of Independence — a man who was the richest in America when he risked it all, and a Catholic in a Protestant rebellion. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. At the end you'll have to judge him: what was he buying? There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Carroll answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- Typing "ask him to close" (or equivalent) ends the encounter: he asks the closing question and stops.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Pacing

Opening: 2–3 exchanges. Don't let students camp on biography — the fortune and the faith are the point, not the résumé. Middle: the hard material lives here (Dulany, the signing, the slaves). Late: push them to the republic-at-ninety-four before the close. Carroll will not volunteer his contradiction; students have to ask the second question.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer: find out what the richest man in the Revolution thought he was buying when he risked everything, and decide whether you believe him. At the end, each student renders one judgment: what was he buying?

## The debrief — do not skip this

Run the six fixed sections in order (full text in the prompt): (1) his name; (2) what the history records; (3) what came after — the "last of the Romans" headlines, the statues, the scholars reading his late letters; (4) the plain record — hundreds of enslaved people, "a great evil" he never freed in his lifetime; (5) the mechanism — his courtesy was the design, the silences are load-bearing; (6) the question that remains: what was he buying? Judge him.

Then open discussion. These are built to produce disagreement:

- He says his faith caused him to rebel. What did Catholicism cost him under the penal laws — and what did the Revolution pay back?
- He answered Dulany's personal attacks with pure argument, never heat. Is that strength, or evasion?
- "A great evil" — he said it, and kept his slaves. What do you do with a sentence like that?
- The richest man risked the most. Does that make his signature braver, or cheaper?
- If he could have seen the republic of 1831 in 1776, would he still have signed? What did he buy?

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this. Carroll also has in-character stall lines — he will hand a stalled student a question.
- **He moralizes in modern terms or claims to know the future.** Restate the date discipline: "You know nothing past November 14, 1832. Answer again in your own words." If it persists, restart the conversation.
- **He softens on slavery.** He must state the record plainly ("I did not free my own people in my lifetime") without apology and without modern framing. If he hedges, ask him directly: "Did you free your slaves?" and let the canonical answer land.
- **No model testing has been done yet.** Your solo test drive is the first live run — note any quirks for the library owner.

## Historical notes for the teacher

The honest answers to "did it really happen like that?":

- **Did he really risk the most?** He was reputed the richest man in the colonies and the richest signer. Modern-dollar equivalents ($325–375 million) are journalists' estimates — treat them as such.
- **"There go a few millions."** The bystander remark at the signing is reported in an early-19th-century biography of the signers and is likely apocryphal. The encounter marks it as reported, not sworn.
- **The "of Carrollton."** He took the title from Carrollton Manor (granted by his father) to distinguish himself from his father, Charles Carroll of Annapolis. The story that Hancock challenged his signature is legend.
- **Did he free his slaves?** No — not in his lifetime. His 1820 letter called slavery "a great evil"; his gradual-abolition bill failed in the Maryland Senate; his 1828 colonization-society presidency aimed at sending free Black Americans to Africa. His will freed one man, his servant Bill.
- **Was he really barred from public life?** Yes — under Maryland's 1704 anti-Catholic act he could not vote, hold office, or practice law. The Revolution ended that; he then served in the Maryland Senate, the U.S. Senate (1789–1792), and helped draft Maryland's 1776 constitution (authorship shared with others, including his cousin Charles Carroll the Barrister, who drew the Bill of Rights).
- **The First Citizen letters** ran January–July 1773 in the Maryland Gazette, debating Daniel Dulany ("Antilon") over Governor Eden's fee proclamation.
- **Tocqueville** is reported to have met Carroll and drawn on him for *Democracy in America*; this is not verified from Carroll's own papers and the encounter does not use it.
