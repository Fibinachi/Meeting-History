## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — say which.

- **The setting (Baltimore, autumn 1831; the conversation itself).** No such conversation is documented. Pure dramatic device; historically plausible for his last full year of life.
- **"Most willingly" and "There go a few millions."** The signing anecdote — Hancock asking whether he would sign, Carroll answering "Most willingly," a bystander remarking "There go a few millions" — first appears in an early-19th-century biography of the signers and is likely apocryphal. The figure is scripted to mark it as reported ("They say… I cannot swear to the words"), not sworn.
- **The "of Carrollton" distinction.** Documented: he took the title from Carrollton Manor (granted by his father) to distinguish himself from his father, Charles Carroll of Annapolis. The story that Hancock challenged him to distinguish his signature from his father's is legend; the figure states the documented reason and does not claim the anecdote.
- **Late-life phrasing about the republic ("the love of money crowding out virtue").** Paraphrase of biographers' summaries of his late letters (Birzer), rendered in Carroll's voice. Not a verbatim quote; marked as such.
- **"I rebelled to become a citizen."** In-voice compression of his documented position (a man barred from citizenship by law, fighting for a republic that would grant it). His stance, our phrasing.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Written before the figure ships.

- **DEAD RULE satisfied:** Charles Carroll of Carrollton died in Baltimore on November 14, 1832, aged 95. After John Adams and Thomas Jefferson died on July 4, 1826, he was the last surviving signer of the Declaration.
- **The brief's "late manumission advocacy" was wrong and is corrected here.** Carroll did NOT free his own slaves in his lifetime. He called slavery "a great evil" (letter, 1820), introduced a gradual-abolition bill in the Maryland Senate that failed, and at 91 served as president of Maryland's Auxiliary State Colonization Society (1828) — whose plan was to send free Black Americans to Africa, not to free his own people. His will freed one man, his servant Bill. The encounter states both the advocacy and the non-manumission, honestly.
- **Wealth estimates:** "Richest signer" / "richest man in the colonies" is the standard description. Modern-dollar equivalents ($325–375 million) are journalists' estimates; the encounter does not quote them as fact.
- **Slave numbers vary by source and date:** "around one thousand slaves" (Maryland Center for History and Culture), "reputedly the largest single slave owner at the Revolution," "nearly 300" on the family estate (Quill Project). The encounter says "hundreds" — marked as the honest middle.
- **First Citizen dating:** the letters ran January–July 1773. Some summaries say the debate began in late 1772; 1773 is the documented span.
- **Maryland constitution authorship:** some sources say Carroll "primarily authored" the 1776 constitution; his cousin Charles Carroll the Barrister drew up the Bill of Rights guaranteeing religious freedom. The encounter does not overclaim; authorship was shared.
- **Tocqueville:** reported to have met Carroll and used him as an informant (Birzer). Not verified from Carroll's own papers in this build — the figure does not mention him.
- **Anti-Catholic law:** Catholics were barred from voting, holding office, and practicing law in Maryland under the 1704 act "to prevent the growth of Popery in this Province."
- **Jackson:** Carroll lived into Jackson's presidency (elected 1828); the debrief may use the span. His personal view of Jackson is not established from the corpus — the encounter does not assign him one.
- **Dulany's pseudonym:** spelled "Antilon" in the 1902 Riley edition (from "anti" + an old English word for unfair taxes); some sources print "Antillon." The encounter uses "Antilon."
