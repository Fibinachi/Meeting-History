# Meet Charles Carroll of Carrollton — Standards Alignment (verified 2026-10-15)

Encounter: students press a simulated Charles Carroll of Carrollton (1737–1832) in Baltimore in the autumn of 1831 — the last surviving signer of the Declaration, reputedly the richest man in the colonies when he signed, and the only Catholic signer. They question him on the "First Citizen" letters of 1773, on what a Catholic barred from voting risked by rebelling, on the slaves he owned and never freed, and on what fifty-five years of the republic looked like to the man who outlived it. It closes with his question: *the richest man in the Revolution risked everything for it — what was he buying? Judge him.*

**Grade band fit for every framework below: 9–12** (high school U.S. History / Government / ELA literacy). Note: most states' *content* anchors for the Revolution sit in grades 7–8 (see caveats); the 9–12 weight comes from South Carolina's USHC placement and from skills standards — source critique, discussion, argumentation — which are band-matched.

## 1. C3 Framework (National Council for the Social Studies, 2013)

Grade band: **9–12**. (Indicator texts verified against curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim; the official NCSS PDF was not directly retrievable in this session — see verification note at end.)

### Dimension 2 — Civics

- **D2.Civ.8.9-12** — Evaluate social and political systems in different contexts, times, and places, that promote civic virtues and enact democratic principles. (The Catholic penal-law Maryland he grew up in — barred from voting, office, and the courts — against the republic he bought: his canonical claim that religious liberty is "a right, not a favour granted by princes.")
- **D2.Civ.10.9-12** — Analyze the impact and the appropriate roles of personal interests and perspectives on the application of civic virtues, democratic principles, constitutional rights, and human rights. (The encounter's load-bearing move: weighing his fortune, his faith, and his slaveholding against the republican virtue he claims — "I will not prevaricate. I owned slaves.")

### Dimension 2 — History

- **D2.His.11.9-12** — Critique the usefulness of historical sources for a specific historical inquiry based on their maker, date, place of origin, intended audience, and purpose. (The "First Citizen" letters were written under a pseudonym in the *Maryland Gazette*; the "There go a few millions" signing anecdote is reported, not sworn — and the figure is scripted to mark it as such.)

### Dimension 4 — Communicating Conclusions and Taking Informed Action

- **D4.1.9-12** — Construct arguments using precise and knowledgeable claims, with evidence from multiple sources, while acknowledging counterclaims and evidentiary weaknesses. (The closing judgment: what the richest man in the Revolution was buying — liberty, conscience, posterity, or a cleaner name for the contradiction he kept.)
- **D4.4.9-12** — Critique the use of claims and evidence in arguments for credibility. (His 1773 answer to Dulany — "when a man turns to invective, you may fairly presume that reasons are wanting him" — is this standard enacted; the student tests his claims against the plain record of the debrief.)

*Verified against: uk.everfi.com C3 curriculum guide; go.boarddocs.com AP Government curriculum document; cdn.fairfieldschools.org district C3 documents. Official document: College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework for Social Studies State Standards, NCSS, 2013 (socialstudies.org/c3).*

## 2. Common Core ELA — Literacy in History/Social Studies & Speaking/Listening

Grade band: **9–12**. (Verified against the official Common Core State Standards Initiative site, thecorestandards.org.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 9–10

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.6** — Compare the point of view of two or more authors for how they treat the same or similar topics, including which details they include and emphasize in their respective accounts. (The "First Citizen" / "Antilon" exchange in the *Maryland Gazette* — Carroll's taxation argument against Dulany's, and against Dulany's attack on Carroll's religion.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.8** — Assess the extent to which the reasoning and evidence in a text support the author's claims. (His claims about what he risked and why — against the debrief's plain record: hundreds held, none freed in his lifetime.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.9** — Compare and contrast treatments of the same topic in several primary and secondary sources. (The 1826 anniversary statement against Rowland's biography against the apocryphal signing anecdote — three treatments of the same man, one of them marked as legend.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.8** — Evaluate an author's premises, claims, and evidence by corroborating or challenging them with other information. (His claim that he rebelled "to become a citizen" against the Maryland penal laws that barred Catholics from citizenship — and against his will, which freed one man.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.1** — Cite specific textual evidence to support analysis of primary and secondary sources, connecting insights gained from specific details to an understanding of the text as a whole. (The 1826 statement's "best earthly inheritance" commended to future generations; the exact words matter to the judgment.)

### Speaking & Listening, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.1** — Initiate and participate effectively in a range of collaborative discussions (one-on-one, in groups, and teacher-led) with diverse partners on grades 11–12 topics, texts, and issues, building on others' ideas and expressing their own clearly and persuasively. (The encounter *is* a sustained one-on-one examination of a witness.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.3** — Evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric, assessing the stance, premises, links among ideas, word choice, points of emphasis, and tone used. (The encounter's whole method: the patrician courtesy, the balanced sentences, the one place he will not go — reconciling his slavery.)

### Writing, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.W.11-12.1** — Write arguments to support claims in an analysis of substantive topics or texts, using valid reasoning and relevant and sufficient evidence. (The closing question — what was he buying? — is designed as a post-encounter argument prompt, per the teacher instructions.)

## 3. South Carolina — Social Studies College- and Career-Ready Standards (2019), US History and the Constitution

Grade band: **9–12** (high school US History). South Carolina places the founding era at 9–12, which makes it the cleanest on-band state fit.

- **Standard 1** — Demonstrate an understanding of the influence of the Atlantic World on the regional and national development of republicanism and federalism from 1607–1815.
- **USHC.1.CE** — Assess the major developments of the American Revolution through significant turning points in the debates over independence and self-government from 1763 to 1791. (The signing of August 2, 1776; the state guidance explicitly names the Declaration of Independence among the founding documents in scope — and Carroll's "First Citizen" letters are the 1773 debate over taxation by proclamation.)
- **USHC.1.CX** — Contextualize significant republican developments within North America's connection to the Atlantic World. (State guidance names "religious liberty" among the legacies that fed republican ideas — Carroll is the Catholic case: barred from voting, office, and law practice under Maryland's anti-Catholic laws, educated at St. Omer and Louis le Grand in France.)
- **USHC.1.P** — Summarize the changing relationship between individuals and the government during the period 1607 to 1800. (His arc in one line: from a subject the law said could not be a citizen to the man who signed — "I rebelled to become a citizen.")
- **USHC.1.E** — Utilize primary and secondary sources to investigate the impact of Atlantic influence on the development of republicanism and federalism. (The First Citizen letters, the 1826 statement, and Rowland's biography as the source set behind the encounter.)

*Verified against: the official SC Department of Education US History and the Constitution Alignment Guide (August 2024), an official interpretation of the 2019 standards (ed.sc.gov).*

## 4. Other states (quick hits)

- **7.3.C.II** — Students will examine the Declaration of Independence and the arguments for independence stated within it. (His signature — "Charles Carroll of Carrollton," that it might not be mistaken for his father's — is on that document.)
- **7.3** key idea — AMERICAN INDEPENDENCE: growing tensions over political power and economic issues sparked a movement for independence from Great Britain. (The 1773 fee proclamation and the "First Citizen" taxation argument sit inside this key idea.)

### Texas (TEKS)

- **§113.44(b)(7)(G)** (US Government, high school — on band) — Examine the reasons the Founding Fathers protected religious freedom in America and guaranteed its free exercise by saying that "Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof," and compare and contrast this to the phrase, "separation of church and state." ## Verification caveats

1. **C3 Framework**: indicator texts were verified against three independent curriculum documents that quote the Framework verbatim (they agree with each other); the official NCSS PDF itself was not directly retrievable this session. Treat the official publisher as NCSS (2013), socialstudies.org/c3.
2. **Grade-band split.** Only South Carolina (USHC) and Texas's high-school Government course (§113.44) place founding-era content at 9–12. New York, Florida, and California teach the Revolution and founding documents in grades 7–8; Texas's high-school U.S. History course (§113.41) begins at 1877. The encounter targets 9–12, so those anchors are real but one band below target.
3. **Texas §113.41**: the task brief suggested §113.41 might hold founding-era anchors; it does not — it is "United States History Studies Since 1877." The on-band Texas anchor is §113.44 (US Government), verified above.
4. **No standard asks the encounter's question.** Every code above covers the Revolution, religious liberty, or source evidence — none asks students to judge what the richest man in the Revolution was buying. The judgment is the library's design, not a standards artifact; the standards supply the evidence base.
5. All verifications in this document were performed on 2026-10-15.
