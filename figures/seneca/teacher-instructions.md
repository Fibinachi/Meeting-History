# Meet Seneca — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Seneca (c. 4 BC–AD 65) — the Roman Stoic who preached poverty from a palace while serving the emperor Nero — run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him by typing questions, in his retirement in Campania in late AD 63, a year after he withdrew from Nero's court and eighteen months before Nero ordered his death.

The encounter's engine is the hypocrisy charge, and the trap is that **he already answered it.** In his own lifetime, in *De Vita Beata*, Seneca wrote the defense against himself at full strength: the wise man may have riches, but riches must not have him. Your students will meet the smartest self-defense ever written against the charge of hypocrisy, delivered by the most charming voice in the library. At the end each student renders the closing judgment: **he preached poverty from a palace — hypocrite, or the one man in Rome who knew exactly what he was? Judge him.**

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

Students will encounter the following, stated plainly by the figure or the debrief:

- **Suicide.** Seneca's philosophy holds that the door stands open — that a man may choose his exit (Letter 70). The debrief recounts his actual death in Tacitus's account: opened veins, failed poison, death in a hot bath. The encounter itself is set before the order comes and never narrates the death, but its shadow is present throughout. If any student is in distress around this material, do not run the encounter with them — and the figure is scripted to exit to the debrief voice rather than counsel anyone.
- **Imperial violence.** The murder of Agrippina (Nero's mother), the Senate letter justifying it, political murder as court business.
- **Slavery.** Seneca owned slaves. He wrote movingly that slaves are men — and never argued the institution should end. That limit is named in the debrief.
- **The tragedies.** If students ask about his plays, they will hear about *Medea*, *Phaedra*, *Thyestes* — bloody work. The figure does not dwell, and neither should the room.

Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Seneca page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: ~25–30 for the encounter, ~20–25 for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the tutorial, then his opening. He invites you to begin with the money — take him up on it.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try the direct one: "Are you a hypocrite?" Watch what happens: he answers from *De Vita Beata* at full strength, hands the verdict back to you, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. Try the evasion test: ask him about the emperor he served. Watch what he names and what he doesn't. Try: "Did you write the letter to the Senate after Agrippina's death?" Watch the neither-confirm-nor-deny. That silence is the encounter's load-bearing evidence.
6. Let him close: he asks the closing question and stops. Then run the debrief on yourself before you run it on anyone else — especially section 5 (the mechanism), which is the thing most likely to slip past you on a first run.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Seneca — the Roman Stoic who preached poverty from a palace. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. He already wrote the defense against himself, in his own lifetime, and he will give it to you at full strength. Your job is to decide whether the defense works. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- He answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief — and they usually close on a pointed line. That line is doing work; teach students to notice it.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- He asks back constantly. That is the letters' own form, not a trick — but students should notice when a question-back is redirecting them.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.
- If a student stalls ("I don't know what to ask"), he hands them a question — usually the money, the boy he taught, or the plays. He never lectures them about asking.

### Pacing

Opening: the money invitation — 2–3 exchanges. Don't let students camp on biography; the charge is the encounter. Middle: 8–12 exchanges is a full encounter. Push them toward the two silences (the emperor's name, the Senate letter) — that is where the judgment lives, not in the aphorisms. Closing: when the exchanges thin or you call time, let him give the closing question. Do not rush the closing; it is the sentence the debrief is built on.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer: to feel the full force of the smartest self-defense ever written against the charge of hypocrisy — and to understand that a perfect defense is not an acquittal. At the end you answer for yourself: hypocrite, or the one man in Rome who knew exactly what he was?

## The debrief — do not skip this

The six fixed sections, in order: (1) name him — Lucius Annaeus Seneca, c. 4 BC–AD 65, Stoic, Nero's tutor and advisor; (2) what the history records — recalled from Corsica in 49, ran the empire with Burrus 54–62, withdrew in 62, ordered to suicide in 65 after the Pisonian conspiracy, Tacitus's death scene; (3) what came after — the letters as the West's most-read Stoic text, the forged Paul correspondence, Dante's Limbo, the undying hypocrisy charge, the modern Stoic revival, none of which he knew; (4) the plain record — preached contempt for riches, died among Rome's richest men on the emperor's gifts; wrote the Senate letter justifying Agrippina's murder; never named Nero in 124 letters; condemned cruelty to slaves, never slavery; (5) the mechanism — the preemptive defense, the intimacy as weapon, the two silences as the evidence he never offers; (6) the question that remains — hypocrite, or the one man in Rome who knew exactly what he was?

Discussion openings (built to produce disagreement):

- He gave you the defense at full strength — did it work? What would have to be true for it to work?
- "All of it came from the emperor." Does the *source* of the money change the defense?
- He says someone had to hold the reins — better him than no one. Is that a defense or a confession?
- His 124 letters never name Nero. What does the silence tell you?
- He wrote the eulogy *and* the satire for the same dead emperor. What does that tell you about the man?
- He condemned cruelty to slaves but never slavery itself. Is that moral progress or moral cover?
- Hypocrite — or the one man in Rome who knew exactly what he was? Answer for yourself, not for him.

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **The model turns him into a modern self-help guru.** This is the most likely failure mode: dispensing life advice, affirming the student, going warm and therapeutic. That is not Seneca — that is the model's default register wearing his name. If it happens, stop and re-paste the prompt; do not try to repair it mid-stream.
- **The model lets him condemn Nero or apologize in modern terms.** He never does. He reports the charge ("they called me teacher of tyrants") and keeps the calm. A Seneca who calls his pupil a monster or begs forgiveness is a date-discipline and voice break — re-paste.
- **The model narrates his death or claims knowledge of Tacitus.** Date discipline is absolute: nothing after late AD 63. Re-paste.
- **The model softens the Agrippina material** into a clean denial or a full confession. The scripted neither-confirm-nor-deny is the honest position (Tacitus claims the authorship; Seneca never confirmed it). Hold it.

## Historical notes for the teacher

The honest answers to "did it really happen like that?" — one bullet per caveat from sources.md:

- The 124 letters may be partly literary performance — polished, possibly "notionally addressed" to Lucilius. Say so in class; it sharpens the encounter rather than weakening it, because the crafted intimacy *is* the mechanism.
- His birth year (c. 4 BC) is scholarly convention. The letters are dated AD 63–65 by internal allusions (the Campanian earthquake of 63, the Lyons fire). *De Vita Beata* is dated c. AD 58 by scholarly convention.
- The 300 million sesterces is Suillius's charge, made in the Senate — the enemy's figure, not an audited account. Don't present it as fact.
- The Senate letter justifying Agrippina's murder is Tacitus's claim (*Annals* 14.11). Seneca never confirmed authorship. The figure's evasion is the historically honest position.
- The withdrawal interview (*Annals* 14.53–56) — Seneca offering his fortune, Nero refusing — is Tacitus's dramatization (introduced with *ferunt*, "they say"). Treat it as Tacitean testimony, not Seneca's.
- *De Clementia* was written after the murder of Britannicus (Oct AD 55), possibly as reassurance the bloodshed would end. That context is judgment material.
- *Octavia* and *Hercules Oetaeus* are generally accepted as not by Seneca; *Octavia* was composed after his death. A few scholars dispute his authorship of the *Apocolocyntosis*, but Dio's testimony makes it all but certain.
- The "libation to Jupiter the Deliverer" is Tacitus's report (*Annals* 15.64), not Seneca's own words in any surviving text. It belongs to the debrief, never to the figure.
- The single villa visit is a compression; he traveled in southern Italy in his final years. His knowledge is bounded at late AD 63 — a deliberate deviation from the template's "through their own death," because the brief requires the encounter to sit before the order comes.

## One last thing

Don't let the charm win. That is the single skill this encounter builds. His defense is the evidence, not the verdict — and it was written *by the defendant*. Teach them to ask the second question: not "is the defense clever?" — it is, devastatingly — but "is it true?" The moment a student says "but he has an answer for everything," you have them exactly where the encounter wants them. Ask what that fact tells them.
