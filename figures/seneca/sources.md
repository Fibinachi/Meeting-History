## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — said plainly here.

- **Knowledge bounded at late AD 63, not through his death.** The template's default is "through their own death"; this encounter deliberately deviates. The brief requires the encounter to be set before the order comes, with the figure knowing the danger but not the outcome. He does not know the conspiracy, the order, his death, or Tacitus's account.
- **The student stands where Lucilius stands.** The letters' second-person address is redirected to the student. No young questioner visiting Seneca is documented; but the letters themselves are written to a "you," which makes this the historically closest available device.
- **The single villa visit in Campania, winter AD 63.** A compression. He traveled in southern Italy in his final years and wrote letters from several places; no one visit at one villa is documented.
- **The opening's invitation to begin with the money.** Pedagogical. The letters open with observations, not invitations; the invitation serves the encounter's judgment engine.
- **The closing's "ask me again in a year."** Dramatic irony, not foreknowledge. He does not know what the year holds.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Written before the figure ships, even the ones that don't matter.

- **The letters may be partly literary performance.** Gummere's introduction notes the polish beneath the informality; Romm calls them "notionally addressed" to Lucilius. Say so in class — it sharpens rather than weakens the encounter: the intimacy is crafted, which is exactly why the student must notice what it is doing.
- **Birth c. 4 BC is scholarly convention** (some give 3 BC, some "between 4 BC and 1 AD"). No source states it; the figure never offers it.
- **De Vita Beata c. AD 58 is scholarly convention**, addressed to his brother Gallio, written in answer to attacks like Suillius's. The figure's "I answered it in a book, in my own lifetime" is the documented fact.
- **The 300 million sesterces is Suillius's charge** — the enemy's figure, made in the Senate (Tacitus, Annals 14.52), not an audited account. The figure does not haggle over it, and neither should the teacher present it as fact.
- **The Agrippina Senate letter is Tacitus's claim.** Tacitus says Seneca wrote it (Annals 14.11); Seneca never confirmed authorship in any surviving text. The figure's neither-confirm-nor-deny is the honest position.
- **The withdrawal interview (Annals 14.53–56) may be Tacitus's dramatization.** Tacitus introduces the surrounding material with *ferunt* ("they say") — he reports without vouching. The offer to return the fortune and Nero's refusal are Tacitean, not Senecan, testimony.
- **Apocolocyntosis: a few scholars dispute Seneca's authorship**, but Cassius Dio's testimony makes the attribution all but certain (Romm). The encounter treats it as his, with the dissent noted here.
- ***Octavia* and *Hercules Oetaeus* are generally accepted as NOT by Seneca** (*Octavia*'s author unknown; composed after his death). The figure's ignorance of the *Octavia* is date discipline, not a dodge.
- **The tragedies' dates are uncertain** (see the Senecan tragedy chronology). The figure does not dwell on them, and the encounter does not date them.
- **De Clementia was written after the murder of Britannicus** (Oct AD 55) — possibly as reassurance that the bloodshed would end. That context is judgment material; the figure presents it as a mirror held up to a young ruler.
- **He was not the only rich Stoic, but he was the richest.** The scale of the fortune — whatever the true figure — is what made the charge stick in his lifetime.
- **The "libation to Jupiter the Deliverer" is Tacitus's report** (Annals 15.64), not Seneca's words in any surviving text of his own. It belongs to the debrief, never to the figure.
- **DEAD RULE satisfied:** Lucius Annaeus Seneca died AD 65 in Rome, by forced suicide on Nero's order after the Pisonian conspiracy.
