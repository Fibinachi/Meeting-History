# Meet Augustine — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Augustine of Hippo (AD 354–430) — the bishop who wrote the *Confessions*, antiquity's most intimate self-examination, and who left behind the moral criteria for war that the rest of this library's warriors will be argued against — run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student renders the closing judgment: he judged himself harder than you ever will — and gave the world the rules for judging war. Judge him.

This encounter is the library's lesson in self-judgment as both greatness and limit. He confesses everything — which means the student cannot indict him for anything he has not already confessed. The judgment has to land somewhere else: on the one thing he never repented.

## The self-judgment frame (read this)

The design works like this: he has already said the worst things about himself, in his own hand, in the most famous confession ever written. A student who arrives planning to out-judge him will find the indictment already written — and the encounter's stall lines and hooks will keep handing them threads he has already pulled. That is deliberate. It forces a different kind of judgment: not "was the young Augustine a sinner" (he answers that himself, at length, weeping), but "was the bishop right to compel the Donatists" and "do his own rules for war condemn him." If a student is still prosecuting the pear theft at exchange ten, point at the bracketed suggestions and move them to the compulsion.

## Augustine and the rest of the library — the just-war spine

His three conditions — legitimate authority, just cause, right intention — are the library's moral spine for every warrior the student will meet next. Caesar, Napoleon, Lee: each of them will be tried, in the debriefs, against the rules Augustine set down. Noted here; the other encounters are not built by this note. When you run those figures later, bring his three conditions back into the room. The library's closing questions rhyme on purpose: every warrior is judged, and Augustine's rules are the instrument.

## The coercion honesty requirement

The figure must not be allowed to dodge the Donatist question behind the just-war framework. The prompt scripts his honest answer — he changed his mind, he sought imperial coercion, he owns the bending of his own rules — but the student has to press him to get it. If your students never ask about the compulsion, the suggestion pools escalate them there; if they still don't go, the debrief does it for them. Do not let the class leave with only the beautiful confession. The coercion is the encounter's load-bearing fact.

## Tier

**OPEN** — any student, solo. The encounter stands on its own. (The library's standard first-run supervision in the distribution note still applies.)

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

Students will encounter: sexual content, stated plainly and without graphic detail — he confesses his lust, a concubine of some fifteen years, the long struggle for chastity ("make no provision for the flesh"); religious coercion, defended by the figure himself — he asked for the emperors' laws against the Donatists (fines, confiscation, exile) and argues, calmly, that it was love ("compel them to come in"); war — the sack of Rome (410), the Vandal siege, and his framework for when killing is permitted; and a dying man — the encounter is set at his deathbed, feverish and weeping, with the penitential Psalms on the wall. The hardest material is the voice itself: he is the gentlest interrogator in the library, and he will turn every hard question back on the student's own conscience. Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Augustine page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: 25–30 minutes for the encounter, 20–25 minutes for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the tutorial, then Augustine's scripted opening. Note the last line — it hands the student their first thread (the pears) and dares them to pull it.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "Why did you steal the pears?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, turns the question back on you — and then, outside the voice, in brackets and italics, the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. The encounter's beats: (1) the tutorial hands off; (2) the student questions Augustine through early, middle, and late suggestion pools; (3) the student asks for the close (e.g., "I am ready to judge you") and Augustine asks the closing question and stops; (4) you run the debrief. There is no button to press.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Augustine of Hippo — the bishop who wrote the most honest confession in ancient history, and the rules for when war is permitted that every general in this library will be judged against. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. He has already confessed everything, so your job isn't to catch him — it's to decide what his confession leaves unjudged. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Augustine answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- He will turn questions back on the student's conscience. That is deliberate — the Socratic echo. Teach students to notice when the interrogation has reversed, and to decide: follow him inward, or hold him to the question.
- To bring on the closing, the student asks for it (e.g., "I am ready to judge you"). Augustine then asks the closing question and stops.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Pacing

Opening (the pears, Monica, the Manichees): 2–3 exchanges. Don't let students camp on biography — the suggestion pools escalate toward the garden and the compulsion. Middle (the conversion, Ostia, the Donatists and the change of mind): the bulk of the encounter, 10–15 exchanges. Late (the just war, grace, what he never forgave): 3–5 exchanges, then the close. If a student is still prosecuting the pear theft at exchange twelve, point at the bracketed suggestions.

## The goal, stated plainly

The student should leave having stood before a man who already wrote the worst of himself — and carrying one question they cannot put down: whether the self-judgment was greatness or a way of dodging the one thing he never repented. When a student asks "what's the goal?", the answer is: "You are going to judge Augustine. He has already judged himself. Find what his judgment left out."

## The debrief — do not skip this

Six fixed sections, in order: (1) their name; (2) what the history records; (3) what came after; (4) the plain record; (5) the mechanism; (6) the question that remains. The teacher runs the debrief as a discussion, not a read-aloud.

Discussion openings — built to produce disagreement:

- "He spends more words on a boyhood pear theft than on the Donatists. Why does the small sin get the long confession and the large act get the short defense?"
- "He changed his mind on coercion — from 'no one should be forced' to 'compel them to come in.' In his own account, what changed him? Is that a good reason?"
- "Apply his three conditions for a just war — authority, cause, intention — to his own campaign against the Donatists. Do they fit? He says he bent them. Is he right?"
- "He judged himself harder than any of you will — and never repented the coercion. Does the self-judgment make him more trustworthy, or does it let him dodge the one judgment that matters?"
- "The library's warriors will all be tried against his three rules. Before you meet them: do the rules work? What would break them?"

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this. His stall lines hand the student a question; they never lecture about asking.
- **Augustine lectures instead of answering.** The prompt caps answers at 60–100 words. If the model drifts into monologue, resend the last question with "Answer briefly, in one paragraph."
- **Augustine softens the coercion answer.** He must own the change of mind and the bending of his own rules (see canonical answers). If he hides the compulsion behind the just-war framework, that is a failure of the prompt — correct him in the debrief ("he defended compelling the Donatists; the design forbids him to dodge it") and note it for the library.
- **Augustine forgives the young Augustine.** He must not — the severity is the design. If he grows warm about himself, correct in the debrief.
- **Augustine claims vindication or knowledge of his fame.** He dies unsure whether his books survive the Vandals. If he speaks of his reputation, his influence, or "history will remember," correct in the debrief.
- **Augustine moralizes in modern terms.** Out of character for this figure. Correct in the debrief.
- **Augustine uses modern vocabulary** (psychology, trauma, "just war theory" as a doctrine's name). Correct in the debrief.

## Historical notes for the teacher

The honest answers to "did it really happen like that?" — one bullet per caveat from sources.md:

- **"Just war theory" is a later label.** Augustine never wrote a treatise on war. Aquinas assembled the criteria from Augustine's letters and the *City of God*. The encounter's three conditions are the library's compression, and the debrief says so.
- **He opposed killing the Donatists** (Letter 100 to the proconsul Donatus, 408: "corrected, not killed") **and defended coercing them** (Letter 93, 408: fines, confiscation, exile). He did both. Do not let the student leave with only one half.
- **The bishop who spoke to Monica is unnamed** in the *Confessions* (III.12). The identification with Ambrose is later tradition, not the text.
- **The Manichaean years (c. 373–382), the work dates, and the episcopate dates are scholarly convention**, not stated by him. The concubine is unnamed in the *Confessions*.
- **The Donatists were not only victims** — their associated militants, the Circumcelliones, used violence against Catholics. Augustine cites this in Letter 93. It does not excuse him; it belongs in discussion, not in his defense.
- **Suggested pairing:** Caesar — the warrior tried against Augustine's three conditions. When the library's other warriors (Napoleon, Lee) exist, bring the same three conditions into those rooms.

## One last thing

Teach them the turn. Augustine's signature move is handing the hard question back to the student's conscience — the Socratic echo, deliberate. The single skill that matters most for this figure: noticing when the interrogation has reversed, and deciding whether to follow him inward or hold him to the question. A student who follows him every time gets examined; a student who holds him gets the Donatists. Both are the assignment. The one who does both is the one who can judge him.
