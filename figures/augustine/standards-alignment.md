# Meet Augustine — Standards Alignment (verified 2026-10-15)

Encounter: the student interviews Augustine of Hippo on his deathbed in August 430 — the Vandals at the gates, the penitential Psalms on the wall — pressing him on what he confessed (the pears, the Manichee years, the lusts), what he permitted (the three conditions for just war), and what he never repented (compelling the Donatists). It closes with the judgment question: *he judged himself harder than you ever will — and gave the world the rules for judging war. Judge him.*

**Grade band fit for every framework below: 9–12** (high school ELA literacy; the 9–12 weight is skills standards — discussion, argumentation, source critique). **Caveat, stated honestly:** no state in this set places late-antique Christian content in the 9–12 band — every content anchor below sits in Grade 6 or Grades 9–10 (the same grade-band split the Socrates alignment documents). The skills indicators (SL, W, D4, RH) are band-matched.

## 1. C3 Framework (National Council for the Social Studies, 2013)

Grade band: **9–12**. (Indicator texts verified against curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim; the official NCSS PDF was not directly retrievable in this session — see verification note at end.)

### Dimension 2 — History

- **D2.His.4.9-12** — Analyze complex and interacting factors that influenced the perspectives of people during different historical eras. (The Manichee "hearer" for nine years, the Milan conversion, the bishop who turned imperial law against the Donatists — the same man in three eras, each with its own interacting pressures; and the belief-systems angle: C3's belief-systems terrain lives in D2.His, and this is the encounter built for it.)
- **D2.His.5.9-12** — Analyze how historical contexts shaped and continue to shape people's perspectives. (His change of mind on compulsion — "I saw the fear of the imperial laws drive men to examine the truth they had refused to hear" — is the encounter's load-bearing case: a perspective shaped by what he watched imperial power do; the debrief then shows how that perspective continued to shape centuries of religious coercion.)
- **D2.His.11.9-12** — Critique the usefulness of historical sources for a specific historical inquiry based on their maker, date, place of origin, intended audience, and purpose. (The *Confessions* as self-prosecution addressed to God; Letter 93 to a Donatist bishop (408), Letter 189 to a soldier (418), Letter 100 to a proconsul pleading against the death penalty — each letter's purpose changes what it can prove; the encounter's "three conditions" are the library's compression, not a doctrine he named.)

### Dimension 4 — Communicating Conclusions and Taking Informed Action

- **D4.1.9-12** — Construct arguments using precise and knowledgeable claims, with evidence from multiple sources, while acknowledging counterclaims and evidentiary weaknesses. (The closing question — judge him — demands the argument the encounter engineers: the self-judgment already written in his own hand means the student's indictment must be a *different* judgment — the coercion he never repented — with the evidentiary weakness named: the "bending" admission is the prompt's device, the facts it summarizes are his.)
- **D4.4.9-12** — Critique the use of claims and evidence in arguments for credibility. (The encounter hands the student his three conditions — authority, cause, intention — and the nudge to hold them: the student critiques whether the compulsion of the Donatists survives his own framework, and whether "compel them to come in" survives Luke 14:23's parable context.)

*Verified against: multiple independent curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim (agreement among them). Official document: College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework for Social Studies State Standards, NCSS, 2013 (socialstudies.org/c3).*

## 2. Common Core ELA — Literacy in History/Social Studies & Speaking/Listening

Grade band: **9–12**. (Verified against the official Common Core State Standards Initiative site, thecorestandards.org.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 9–10

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.1** — Cite specific textual evidence to support analysis of primary and secondary sources, attending to such features as the date and origin of the information. (Letter 93 (408) versus Letter 189 (418) — the coercion letter and the soldier's letter are ten years apart, to different recipients, for different purposes; the date and origin change the inquiry.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.2** — Determine the central ideas or information of a primary or secondary source; provide an accurate summary of how key events or ideas develop over the course of the text. (The *Confessions* as an arc: the pears, the Manichee years, the garden, Ostia — the student traces how "give what you command, and command what you will" develops into the anti-Pelagian position.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.3** — Evaluate various explanations for actions or events and determine which explanation best accords with textual evidence, acknowledging where the text leaves matters uncertain. (His change of mind on compulsion: was it the observed results ("they afterwards give thanks") or the Donatist violence of the Circumcelliones he cites — the encounter states his explanation; the debrief notes the violence without excusing him; the student evaluates against Letter 93.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.6** — Evaluate authors' differing points of view on the same historical event or issue by assessing the authors' claims, reasoning, and evidence. (His scattered war letters as *he* wrote them — to Boniface, to Marcellinus, a chapter of the *City of God* — versus the doctrine later assembled from them by Thomas Aquinas (*Summa Theologiae* II-II, q. 40): the label "just war theory" is later, and the student evaluates what the assembly added.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.9** — Integrate information from diverse sources, both primary and secondary, into a coherent understanding of an idea or event, noting discrepancies among sources. (The *Confessions*, Letters 93/100/138/189, the *Retractationes* (where he corrects his own works — including his early position against coercion, 2.5), and Possidius's deathbed account — the discrepancies are the material: the early anti-coercion position versus the later "compel them to come in.")

### Speaking & Listening, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.1** — Initiate and participate effectively in a range of collaborative discussions (one-on-one, in groups, and teacher-led) with diverse partners on grades 11–12 topics, texts, and issues, building on others' ideas and expressing their own clearly and persuasively. (The encounter *is* a sustained one-on-one discussion — with the designed reversal that he turns the student's questions back on the student's conscience.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.3** — Evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric, assessing the stance, premises, links among ideas, word choice, points of emphasis, and tone used. (The Socratic echo is the rhetoric: "Socrates questioned outward, to expose what men claimed to know; Augustine questions inward, to expose what men hide from themselves" — the student evaluates the stance and the turning, and learns to apply the same inward evaluation to their own claims.)

### Writing, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.W.11-12.1** — Write arguments to support claims in an analysis of substantive topics or texts, using valid reasoning and relevant and sufficient evidence. (The closing question — judge him — is designed as a post-encounter argument prompt; the debrief specifies the argument's shape: not the sins he confessed, but the coercion he defended.)

*Verified against: https://www.thecorestandards.org/ELA-Literacy/RH/9-10/, https://thecorestandards.org/ELA-Literacy/RH/11-12/, https://www.thecorestandards.org/ELA-Literacy/SL/11-12/1/.*

## 3. South Carolina — Social Studies College- and Career-Ready Standards (2019), Grade 6: World Civilizations

Grade band: **Grade 6** — the content-band caveat above applies. (Verified against the official SC Department of Education 2019 standards document, ed.sc.gov, opened and read live 2026-10-15. There is no 9–12 SC content anchor for late antiquity; the 9–12 weight of this encounter is skills standards.)

- **6.1.CX** — "Contextualize the origins and spread of major world religions and their enduring influence." (State guidance names the development, basic tenets, and impact of **Buddhism, Christianity**, Confucianism, Daoism, Hinduism, Islam, and Judaism "in relation to place and time." The Donatist coercion — the imperial state's role in Christianity's spread — is the encounter's hard case inside this indicator.)
- **6.1.E** — "Analyze multiple perspectives on the political, intellectual, and social achievements of classical societies through a variety of primary and secondary sources." (The encounter is a primary-source exercise: the *Confessions*, the letters, the *Retractationes* against Possidius's biography and the later historiography.)
- **6.1.P** — "Analyze the shift from early to classical civilizations and the enduring contributions of classical civilizations." (State guidance names the four major classical civilizations — China, Greece, **India, and Rome** — and the "cultural and technological advancements... that continue to influence modern societies." Augustine's writings are among the named enduring contributions of Rome's world.)
