## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — said plainly here.

- **The deathbed conversation.** The setting is documented — the Vandal siege of Hippo (begun May 430), the penitential Psalms fixed to the wall, the weeping, the death on August 28, 430 (Possidius, *Vita* 31). The conversation with a modern student is a pure dramatic device; no such interlocutor existed.
- **Compressed phrasing of the letters.** The canonical answers compress letter chapters into single paragraphs. The positions are his, in his words where marked; the compression and the voice's cadence are authored.
- **The three-condition summary.** "Authority, cause, intention" is the encounter's plain-language compression of material spread across Letter 138, Letter 189, the City of God, and the *Questions on the Heptateuch*. Augustine never presented them as a numbered list. The compression is the library's, so the student can carry it into the other warriors' encounters; the debrief says so.
- **The "bending" admission.** Augustine never wrote the sentence "I bent my three conditions to cover the Church's discipline." He wrote everything the sentence summarizes (Letters 93 and 100, the change of mind, the penalties sought). The first-person ownership of the tension is the encounter's device; the facts it owns are his.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Written before the figure ships, even the ones that don't matter.

- **DEAD RULE satisfied:** Augustine died August 28, 430, at Hippo Regius, during the Vandal siege.
- **"Just war theory" is a later label.** Augustine never wrote a treatise on war. Thomas Aquinas (*Summa Theologiae* II-II, q. 40) assembled the criteria drawing heavily on Augustine's letters and *City of God*. The encounter's three conditions are a compression, not a doctrine Augustine named. He would not recognize the phrase "just war theory."
- **Which bishop spoke to Monica is uncertain.** *Confessions* III.12 attributes "it is not possible that the son of these tears should perish" to a bishop Monica consulted at Carthage. Later tradition sometimes identifies him with Ambrose; the text does not name him.
- **The Manichaean years are approximate.** Nine years as a "hearer" (auditor), c. 373–382. The dates are scholarly convention, not stated by him.
- **The concubine is unnamed.** She was with him some fifteen years (c. 372–387); their son Adeodatus died young (c. 390). The figure names neither, as the *Confessions* does not.
- **He opposed killing the Donatists.** Letter 100 to the proconsul Donatus (408) asks that they be "corrected, not killed," and he repeatedly pleaded against the death penalty. What he did seek and defend was imperial coercion short of blood: fines, confiscation, exile, loss of civil standing. The encounter states both, because he did both.
- **The Donatists were not only victims.** Their associated militants, the Circumcelliones, used violence against Catholics; Augustine cites this in Letter 93. The encounter's canonical answers do not excuse him with it; the teacher may raise it in discussion.
- **Work dates are approximate.** *Confessions* c. 397–400; *City of God* 413–426 (begun after the sack of Rome, 410); *Retractationes* c. 426–427; consecrated bishop 395 (coadjutor, succeeding Valerius c. 396).
- **The siege's outcome.** The Vandals lifted the siege of Hippo in 431; they took the city in 439. His cathedral and library were spared. He died before any of this; the figure knows none of it.
