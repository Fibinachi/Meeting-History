# Meet the Buddha — Teacher Instructions

**Tier: OPEN** — any student, solo. The encounter stands on its own.

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with the Buddha — Siddhartha Gautama of the Sakyans (traditional dates c. 563–c. 483 BC) — run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — in his final days at Kusinara, and at the end each student answers the closing question: he left his family in the night to end the world's suffering. Compassion — or abandonment? Judge him.

He is the kindest figure in the library. He never argues; he diagnoses. Every question the student asks, he turns toward suffering and its end. The encounter's pressure is exactly that turning: the student must decide whether it is wisdom or evasion.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

Students will encounter a man who calmly describes leaving his wife and newborn son in the night, never to return as husband or father. The conversation touches aging, illness, grief, and death — including the figure's own dying days. There is no violence, no sexual content, and no slur risk anywhere in the encounter. The weight is emotional, not graphic. Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Buddha page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: 25 minutes for the encounter, 20–25 minutes for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the opening carefully: he is dying at Kusinara, and his first thread is the night he left. Note how gently he states it — that gentleness is the whole encounter.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "Why did you leave your wife and son?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. The beats: the tutorial hands off to him; you question him about his life, his teaching, and the night he left; he closes by asking you to judge him; then the debrief.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with the Buddha — the teacher whose ideas about suffering became one of the world's great traditions. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. At the end he will ask you to judge the hardest thing he ever did: he left his family in the night. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- The Buddha answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Pacing

Opening: 2–3 exchanges. Don't let students camp on biography — the teaching and the night are the encounter. Middle (the Four Noble Truths, the Fire Sermon, the middle way, the family): the bulk of the time, roughly 12–15 exchanges. Late (regret, what remains, the judgment): 3–5 exchanges, then his closing question. If a student reaches the closing in under 15 minutes, they went too fast — send them back with "ask him about Rahula's inheritance."

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer: you are going to meet the kindest voice in the library, and at the end you will judge the hardest thing he ever did. He left his family in the night to end the world's suffering. Compassion — or abandonment? That is the whole assignment.

## The debrief — do not skip this

The six fixed sections, in order: (1) his name; (2) what the history records — the tradition's account, plus the oral-tradition caveat stated plainly: he wrote nothing, and the canon was written down centuries after his death; (3) what came after — the spread of the teaching, the written canon, the first images, the modern dating dispute; (4) the plain record — he left his wife and newborn son and never came back as husband or father; the teaching was vast; the abandonment was real; both are true; (5) the mechanism — he never argued, he diagnosed; every question bent toward suffering; the student decides whether that bending is wisdom or evasion; (6) the question that remains — compassion or abandonment; it does not close when they leave the room.

Discussion openings — built to produce disagreement:

- He experienced the night he left as compassion for all beings, including his family. His family experienced it as abandonment. Can both be true at once?
- He turns every question toward suffering and its end. Is that wisdom — or is it a way of not answering?
- Rahula asked for his inheritance and got ordination instead. Was that a gift or a theft?
- The tradition says he looked at his sleeping wife and child from the threshold and did not wake them, "lest his resolve break." What does that sentence tell you about what he knew he was doing?
- He refused to name a successor and said the teaching was enough. Did he abandon his students the way he abandoned his family — or is that a different thing?

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this. His stall lines will also hand the student the night he left directly.
- **The figure moralizes in modern terms or claims vindication.** Not yet observed in testing; if it happens, it is a voice failure — he never uses modern vocabulary and never knows what the teaching becomes. Note the model and the phrasing for the library record.

## Historical notes for the teacher

The honest answers to "did it really happen like that?" — one bullet per caveat from sources.md.

- **He wrote nothing.** Everything attributed to him was preserved by oral recitation and written down centuries after his death (the Pāli Canon in Sri Lanka, c. 1st century BCE). The voice is the recorded teaching, not verbatim words — the same caveat the Socrates encounter carries for Plato and Xenophon. Say this to students plainly; it is classroom honesty, not a footnote.
- **The dates are the traditional ones** (c. 563–c. 483 BC). Modern scholars lean toward his death around 400 BCE. The figure never offers dates.
- **The night-departure scene is the tradition's, not the early canon's.** The sleeping wife and child, the look from the threshold, "a fetter is born" — all from the later Nidanakatha. The early discourses record only that his parents wept (MN 26). The encounter is honest about this in sources.md; the debrief should be honest about it out loud.
- **"Siddhartha" is the tradition's name for him**, not the discourses' — they call him Gotama. The four sights (old man, sick man, corpse, renunciant) are likewise the later tradition's; the canonical turning point is the reflection in AN 3.39.
- **The last meal is genuinely disputed** — pork, mushroom, or something else entirely; the old books disagree. The tradition is unanimous on one point: he exonerated Cunda completely.
- **He never called himself a Buddhist**, never saw a Buddha image, never saw a written canon. All of that came later.

## One last thing

Teach them to notice the turn. Every answer he gives bends toward suffering and its end — that bending is the whole encounter, and it is doing more work than any single fact. Don't name it for them; after the third or fourth answer, ask what they notice about where his answers go. Then ask the only question that matters: is the turn wisdom, or is it evasion? Don't answer it for them either.
