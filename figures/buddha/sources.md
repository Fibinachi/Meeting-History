# Sources — the Buddha

The corpus this figure's voice is bounded by. The voice may claim only what these texts support. The Buddha wrote nothing; every word attributed to him was preserved by oral recitation and first written down centuries after his death. That is the central caveat of this encounter and it is stated plainly below — the voice is the recorded teaching, not verbatim words, said here exactly as the Socrates figure's sources say it for Plato and Xenophon.

## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — said plainly here.

- **The Kusinara final-days setting** — the encounter holds the whole conversation at the sal grove between the twin sal trees. DN 16 spans a final journey across many towns; the encounter compresses it to the grove. Dramatic compression of documented material.
- **The figure speaks the Nidanakatha's night-departure account as his own memory** — the sleeping wife and newborn son, the look from the threshold, "a fetter is born." The early discourses do not record this scene (MN 26 records only that his parents wept). The figure presents it as the tradition keeps it, and the prompt marks it as the tradition's account — but the student hears it in his voice. This is the encounter's load-bearing device and its load-bearing honesty problem; it is listed here so the teacher knows exactly what is tradition and what is canon.
- **He answers modern students** — pure dramatic device. The historical teacher knew nothing of any library and died unsure what would become of the teaching.
- **The closing question put in his mouth** — he never asked anyone to judge him. The judgment is the library's design, not the tradition's.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Written before the figure ships, even the ones that don't matter.

- **The oral-tradition caveat (the Socrates precedent).** He wrote nothing. Every word attributed to him was preserved by communal oral recitation and first written down centuries after his death — the Pāli Canon in Sri Lanka around the first century BCE, roughly four hundred years later. Scholars debate how much of the canon goes back to the man and how much is the community's composition. The encounter leans on the early discourses; the voice is the recorded teaching, not verbatim words.
- **The dates are uncertain.** The figure uses the traditional Theravada dates, c. 563–c. 483 BC. Modern scholarship, after the 1988 Göttingen symposium and subsequent work, leans toward his death around 400 BCE (within roughly twenty years either side) — nearly a century later. Some traditions give 544 BCE. The figure never offers dates; the debrief states the dispute.
- **"Siddhartha" is not his name in the discourses.** The early texts call him Gotama (his clan name), the Tathagata, or the Blessed One. "Siddhattha/Siddhartha" is the given name of the later commentarial tradition. The figure never calls himself Siddhartha.
- **The four sights are the later tradition's.** The old man, the sick man, the corpse, and the renunciant come from the Nidanakatha, not the early discourses. The canonical basis for the sheltered youth and the turning is the Sukhamala Sutta (AN 3.39).
- **Yasodhara's name in the canon.** Her name appears in the canonical Apadāna (Therī-apadāna verses), a late stratum of the canon — not in the Therīgāthā. Everything else about her — the marriage at sixteen, the night of the departure, her grief — is the tradition's, not the early discourses'.
- **Rahula's birth on the night of the departure** is the Pāli tradition's account. The early discourses say nothing about the timing.
- **Rahula ordained at seven** is the tradition's; the Vinaya records the ordination without giving his age.
- **Sūkaramaddava is genuinely disputed.** Buddhaghosa's commentary reads it as tender boar's flesh (the Theravada position); others have read it as mushroom, truffle, or bamboo shoots (the Mahayana-leaning position). The old books disagree and the figure honestly does not settle it.
- **He never saw his own face in stone.** The first Buddha images were carved centuries after his death (Gandhara/Mathura, c. 1st century CE). No image, no monastery, no written canon existed in his lifetime.
- **He never used the word "Buddhism."** It is a modern category. His word was the teaching.
- **DEAD RULE satisfied:** the Buddha died c. 483 BC (traditional dating) at Kusinara, aged eighty, between the twin sal trees, as the Mahaparinibbana Sutta records.
