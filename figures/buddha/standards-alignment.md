# Meet the Buddha — Standards Alignment (verified 2026-10-15)

Encounter: the student interviews the Buddha in his final days at Kusinara (traditional dating, c. 483 BC) — pressing him on the night he left his wife and newborn son, the Four Noble Truths, the middle way, and whether his relentless turning of every question toward suffering is wisdom or evasion. It closes with the judgment question: *he left his family in the night to end the world's suffering. Compassion — or abandonment? Judge him.*

**Grade band fit for every framework below: 9–12** (high school World History / ELA literacy; the 9–12 weight is skills standards — discussion, argumentation, source critique). **Caveat, stated honestly:** no state in this set places classical India in the 9–12 band — every content anchor below sits in Grade 6 or Grades 9–10 (the same grade-band split the Socrates alignment documents). **The source caveat is load-bearing for this encounter and is stated plainly:** he wrote nothing; every word attributed to him was preserved by communal oral recitation and first written down centuries after his death (the Pāli Canon in Sri Lanka, c. first century BCE) — the same precedent as Socrates-via-Plato, noted in the encounter's own sources. The voice is the recorded teaching, not verbatim words.

## 1. C3 Framework (National Council for the Social Studies, 2013)

Grade band: **9–12**. (Indicator texts verified against curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim; the official NCSS PDF was not directly retrievable in this session — see verification note at end.)

### Dimension 2 — History

- **D2.His.8.9-12** — Analyze how current interpretations of the past are limited by the extent to which available historical sources represent perspectives of people at the time. (The encounter's central historiographical fact: the canon was written down ~400 years after his death; the Nidanakatha's night-departure scene — the threshold look, "a fetter is born" — is the tradition's account, not the early discourses'. What the student can know of the man is limited by what the reciting community preserved.)
- **D2.His.10.9-12** — Detect possible limitations in various kinds of historical evidence and differing secondary interpretations. (The kinds of evidence are the material: early discourses (SN, MN, DN, AN), the commentarial tradition (Nidanakatha, c. 5th century CE), the Vinaya's monastic history, the modern scholarly dating dispute (traditional c. 483 BC versus ~400 BCE) — each kind with its own limitation.)
- **D2.His.4.9-12** — Analyze complex and interacting factors that influenced the perspectives of people during different historical eras. (The tradition's "fetter is born" against the debrief's modern judgment — perspectives on the same night across twenty-five centuries; the encounter marks which layer of the record each detail comes from.)
- **D2.His.5.9-12** — Analyze how historical contexts shaped and continue to shape people's perspectives. (The oral-recitation context shaped what survives as "his" words; the community that preserved the teaching shaped the teaching's record — the student analyzes the context that made the sources, not just the context that made the man.)

### Dimension 4 — Communicating Conclusions and Taking Informed Action

- **D4.1.9-12** — Construct arguments using precise and knowledgeable claims, with evidence from multiple sources, while acknowledging counterclaims and evidentiary weaknesses. (The closing question — compassion or abandonment — is a designed argument prompt with the evidentiary weakness stated up front: the night-departure as he speaks it is the tradition's account presented as his memory, and the prompt's sources.md lists it as the encounter's "load-bearing honesty problem.")
- **D4.4.9-12** — Critique the use of claims and evidence in arguments for credibility. (The student critiques exactly the claims the tradition asks them to accept: the sleeping wife and newborn son, the look from the threshold, Rahula ordained at seven — against the early discourses, which record only that his parents wept (MN 26) and say nothing about the timing of the birth.)

*Verified against: multiple independent curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim (agreement among them). Official document: College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework for Social Studies State Standards, NCSS, 2013 (socialstudies.org/c3).*

## 2. Common Core ELA — Literacy in History/Social Studies & Speaking/Listening

Grade band: **9–12**. (Verified against the official Common Core State Standards Initiative site, thecorestandards.org.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 9–10

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.2** — Determine the central ideas or information of a primary or secondary source; provide an accurate summary of how key events or ideas develop over the course of the text. (The Four Noble Truths as given in SN 56.11 — suffering, its origin, its cessation, the way — and how the eightfold path develops out of the fourth.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.6** — Compare the point of view of two or more authors for how they treat the same or similar topics, including which details they include and emphasize in their respective accounts. (The going-forth as told in the early discourses (MN 26: the noble search, his parents weeping) versus the commentarial tradition (Nidanakatha: the threshold scene, the sleeping wife and son) — what each telling includes and emphasizes.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.2** — Determine the central ideas or information of a primary or secondary source; provide an accurate summary that makes clear the relationships among the key details and ideas. (The Fire Sermon (SN 35.28) — "all is burning" — and its relationship to the middle way (MN 36): the most uncompromising statement of the diagnosis held against the path it demands.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.8** — Evaluate an author's premises, claims, and evidence by corroborating or challenging them with other information. (The encounter models this on itself: the sukaramaddava dispute (DN 16) — "the old books disagree, and he does not pretend to settle it" — and the dating dispute in the debrief; the student corroborates or challenges the tradition's claims with the scholarly record.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.9** — Integrate information from diverse sources, both primary and secondary, into a coherent understanding of an idea or event, noting discrepancies among sources. (The night of the departure across MN 26, the Vinaya Mahavagga (Rahula's ordination, no age given), and the Nidanakatha (the age of seven, the threshold look) — the discrepancies are the assignment, and the encounter's sources.md tells the teacher exactly which is which.)

### Speaking & Listening, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.1** — Initiate and participate effectively in a range of collaborative discussions (one-on-one, in groups, and teacher-led) with diverse partners on grades 11–12 topics, texts, and issues, building on others' ideas and expressing their own clearly and persuasively. (The encounter *is* a sustained one-on-one discussion — with the designed pressure that he turns every question toward suffering, and the student must decide whether to follow the turn or hold the original question.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.3** — Evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric, assessing the stance, premises, links among ideas, word choice, points of emphasis, and tone used. (His diagnosing rhetoric is the evidence: "he never argues; he diagnoses" — the load-bearing nudge asks the student to notice when a question about his family gets answered with suffering, and to evaluate whether that turning is wisdom or evasion.)

### Writing, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.W.11-12.1** — Write arguments to support claims in an analysis of substantive topics or texts, using valid reasoning and relevant and sufficient evidence. (The closing question — compassion or abandonment — is designed as a post-encounter argument prompt, per the teacher instructions; the debrief states both halves plainly: "The abandonment was real too. Both are true; neither cancels the other.")

## 3. South Carolina — Social Studies College- and Career-Ready Standards (2019), Grade 6: World Civilizations

Grade band: **Grade 6** — the content-band caveat above applies. (Verified against the official SC Department of Education 2019 standards document, ed.sc.gov, opened and read live 2026-10-15. There is no 9–12 SC content anchor for classical India; the 9–12 weight of this encounter is skills standards.)

- **6.1.CX** — "Contextualize the origins and spread of major world religions and their enduring influence." (State guidance names the development, basic tenets, and impact of **Buddhism**, Christianity, Confucianism, Daoism, Hinduism, Islam, and Judaism "in relation to place and time." The Four Noble Truths and the eightfold path are the tenets; the encounter's "he never used the word Buddhism" is the origins half.)
- **6.1.P** — "Analyze the shift from early to classical civilizations and the enduring contributions of classical civilizations." (State guidance names the four major classical civilizations — China, Greece, **India**, and Rome — and the "cultural and technological advancements... that continue to influence modern societies." The teaching is India's named enduring contribution.)
- **6.1.E** — "Analyze multiple perspectives on the political, intellectual, and social achievements of classical societies through a variety of primary and secondary sources." (The oral-tradition caveat makes this the honest indicator: the "primary sources" are the community's recitation, and the perspectives include the tradition's, the scholars', and the student's.)
