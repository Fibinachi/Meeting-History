# Meet Victor Klemperer — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Victor Klemperer (1881–1960), professor of Romance philology at the Technical University of Dresden, who kept a diary of Nazi Germany day by day from 1933 to 1945. Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student answers the closing question: He watched, he wrote, he survived. He didn't resist. Judge him.

This is the library's witness encounter, not its actor encounter. Klemperer did not lead, resist, or flee. He recorded. The pedagogy lives in that distinction: the student must decide what bearing witness is worth when it changes nothing for anyone but the record.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

Students will encounter the Holocaust directly: state antisemitism, the legal stripping of a Jewish man's livelihood, home, name, and freedom; deportations of his friends to Theresienstadt; the yellow star; forced labor; the firebombing of Dresden. Klemperer speaks of these things plainly and without euphemism, in dated diary sequence. He does not describe atrocities for effect, and the prompt forbids it — but the facts themselves are the material. Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Klemperer page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: 25–30 for the encounter, 20–25 for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the tutorial, then the figure's opening. Note what he volunteers (the professorship, the twelve years, the notebooks, his wife hiding them) and what he withholds (he never calls the diary "resistance").
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "Why didn't you leave Germany?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. The beats: the tutorial hands off to Klemperer → the student questions him (the noose, the words, Eva, the bombing) → the student asks for, or is given, the closing question → the debrief. When the room is ready for the close, any student types "Please ask me the closing question."

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with a German professor who lived through the Nazi years as a man the state classified as a Jew. He didn't resist. He didn't flee. He wrote everything down, day by day, for twelve years. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. At the end you'll be asked to judge him — not the regime, him. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Klemperer answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- At three load-bearing moments a "did you notice" nudge appears, pointing at something in the answer just given. They are observational, never conclusions.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.
- The closing question comes when the student asks for it, or when you move the room to it. After the closing, run the debrief.

### Pacing

- Opening: 2–3 exchanges. Don't let students camp on biography.
- The noose (1933 → 1935 → 1938 → 1940 → 1941): 6–8 exchanges. This is the core teaching sequence — the chronology is the lesson. Let it run.
- LTI and the poisoned words: 3–4 exchanges. Suggested classroom thread below.
- Eva and the bombing: 3–4 exchanges.
- The closing: move the room to it while energy is still up, not after it fades.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer: Understand, in dated sequence, how a regime narrows one man's life law by law and word by word — and then decide what his twelve years of writing are worth, given that he never resisted.

## The debrief — do not skip this

Run it as a discussion, not a read-aloud. The six fixed sections, in order: (1) name him — Victor Klemperer (1881–1960); (2) what the history records — dismissed 1935, Judenhaus 1940, star 1941, survived via his wife and the Dresden bombing; (3) what came after — diaries published 1995, a sensation; LTI a classic on language and power; (4) the plain record — a witness, not an actor: no resistance, no sabotage; twelve years of daily record kept at mortal risk; (5) the mechanism — his flatness was the design; he never called the diary "resistance" and never asked for sympathy; the judgment was always yours; (6) the question that remains: He watched, he wrote, he survived. He didn't resist. Judge him.

Discussion openings — built to produce disagreement:

- Was the diary an act of resistance? Does writing count when the regime never noticed it?
- He asks "what would you have had me do?" — was there really nothing to do? Name his actual options and what each one cost, in 1941, wearing the star.
- He stayed in East Germany, joined the party, took its prizes. Does that change your judgment of the witness?
- The language thread: he says words were "tiny doses of arsenic," doing their work before anyone agreed with a single idea. If that's true, who is responsible for what words do — the speaker, or the million repetitions?

**Suggested classroom thread (LTI — language and power):** Klemperer's *LTI* is the most teachable part of this encounter beyond the history itself. After the debrief, put three of his words on the board — *fanatisch* (fanatical, turned into praise), *total*, *Volk* — and ask students to find one word in their own public life whose value has been changed by repetition rather than by argument. The point is his, not yours: he tracked what words do before anyone agrees with them.

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **The figure moralizes in modern terms** (uses words like "Holocaust," "bystander," "trauma," or apologizes for himself). That is a voice failure — the prompt forbids it explicitly. Steer with: "Answer as Klemperer would have in 1959, in his own vocabulary." If it persists, note it and report it to the library owner.
- **The figure claims to know about the diaries' publication.** Date-discipline failure. Same steer: he does not know what came after the winter of 1959.
- No model testing has been run on this figure yet. Report quirks to the library owner.

## Historical notes for the teacher

- He died 11 February 1960 in Dresden. (An erroneous July date circulates in some blurbs.)
- Baptized Protestant in 1912 (re-baptism); the Nazi racial definition made it irrelevant.
- English title: *I Will Bear Witness* (Martin Chalmers translation). Vol. 2 is published as *To the Bitter End*.
- Sources disagree on the exact day he removed the star (the night of the bombing vs. 19 February); the encounter deliberately does not pin the day.
- His forced labor is documented as factory work; the encounter names no specific factory.
- Eva Schlemmer (pianist, married 1906, died 1951) carried the diary pages to a friend's house in Pirna. She could have divorced him and been safe.
- Postwar: SED member, professor again (Dresden, Greifswald, Halle, Humboldt Berlin), Volkskammer 1950–1958, DDR National Prize 1952. The encounter states this without moral gloss.

## One last thing

Don't let them settle the verdict in the first ten minutes. Students will want to make him a hero ("he resisted with his pen!") or a coward ("he did nothing!") before they've heard the chronology. Klemperer is neither in his own telling — hold the room in the noose sequence, law by law, year by year, and let the closing question land on the full weight of it. The judgment comes last; that is the whole design.
