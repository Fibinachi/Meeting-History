# Meet Epicurus — Standards Alignment (verified 2026-10-15)

Encounter: the de-mythologizing encounter. The student arrives expecting a glutton and meets an ascetic of joy — bread, water, a pot of cheese, and his friends. He never argues; he dissolves the question. Nearly everything he wrote is lost — the entire voice is bounded by what Diogenes Laertius chose to copy (the three letters, the Principal Doctrines) — and the slander began in his own lifetime. It closes with the handoff question: *two thousand years called him a glutton; his own letter says otherwise. Who do you believe? Judge him.*

**Grade band fit for every framework below: 9–12** (high school ELA literacy / World History). Note: most states' *content* anchors for classical Greece sit in lower grades (see caveats); the 9–12 weight of this encounter comes from skills standards — source limitation analysis, reputation-vs.-evidence evaluation, argumentation — which are band-matched.

## 1. C3 Framework (National Council for the Social Studies, 2013)

Grade band: **9–12**. (Indicator texts verified against curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim; the official NCSS PDF was not directly retrievable in this session — see verification note at end.)

This encounter is the library's thesis in miniature — *reputations are made by enemies as well as by evidence* — and its C3 engine is source limitation.

### Dimension 2 — History

- **D2.His.8.9-12** — Analyze how current interpretations of the past are limited by the extent to which available historical sources represent perspectives of people at the time. (The headline indicator for this encounter. Some three hundred works are lost; what survives is what one ancient biographer copied — and the hostile tradition (Timocrates, Diotimus's forged letters) survived alongside it. Our interpretation of "Epicurean" is limited by exactly this source bottleneck, which the encounter's dying-day setting makes visible.)
- **D2.His.10.9-12** — Detect possible limitations in various kinds of historical evidence and differing secondary interpretations. (The forged letters, the apostate pupil's revenge account, the secondhand dying-day report via Hermarchus and Hermippus — the student learns to detect the limitations in each kind of evidence before trusting any of it.)
- **D2.His.9.9-12** — Analyze the relationship between historical sources and the secondary interpretations made from them. (The *Letter to Menoeceus* vs. two millennia of "epicurean" meaning — the student traces how a primary source (pleasure = absence of pain) became a secondary interpretation (indulgence) that is its precise opposite.)
- **D2.His.16.9-12** — Integrate evidence from multiple relevant historical sources and interpretations into a reasoned argument about the past. (The closing question — whom do you believe? — demands integrating the letter, the cheese, the will's care provisions, and the hostile tradition into one reasoned verdict.)

### Dimension 4 — Communicating Conclusions and Taking Informed Action

- **D4.1.9-12** — Construct arguments using precise and knowledgeable claims, with evidence from multiple sources, while acknowledging counterclaims and evidentiary weaknesses. (The student's verdict must acknowledge the counterclaim — the hostile tradition is the counterclaim — and the evidentiary weakness that nearly his whole corpus is gone, so the argument rests on a narrow residue.)
- **D4.4.9-12** — Critique the use of claims and evidence in arguments for credibility. (The encounter's discipline: testing the slander's evidence — hostile witnesses, forged letters — for credibility against the letter's, and refusing to let a word swallow a man without an audit.)

*Verified against: resources.finalsite.net (Greenwich Public Schools C3 curriculum); mnhs.org and historicfortsnelling.com (Minnesota Historical Society standards-connections documents); learninglab.si.edu; nrhp.mnhs.org. Official document: College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework for Social Studies State Standards, NCSS, 2013 (socialstudies.org/c3).*

## 2. Common Core ELA — Literacy in History/Social Studies, Speaking/Listening & Writing

Grade band: **9–12**. (Verified against the official Common Core State Standards Initiative site, thecorestandards.org.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 9–10

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.6** — Compare the point of view of two or more authors for how they treat the same or similar topics, including which details they include and emphasize in their respective accounts. (Epicurus's own letters vs. Timocrates's and Diotimus's hostile accounts — same man, opposed treatments: the cheese letter vs. the vomiting-twice-a-day slander, compared detail by detail.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.1** — Cite specific textual evidence to support analysis of primary and secondary sources, attending to such features as the date and origin of the information. (The *Letter to Menoeceus* §§131–132 on pleasure, §§124–125 on death — cited as primary evidence with attention to its survival route through Diogenes Laertius, c. 3rd century AD.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.6** — Evaluate authors' differing points of view on the same historical event or issue by assessing the authors' claims, reasoning, and evidence. (The full de-mythologizing exercise: assessing the slanderers' claims and evidence (forged letters, an apostate's revenge) against the Garden's own documentary residue (the will, the letters, the monthly twentieth).)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.3** — Evaluate various explanations for actions or events and determine which explanation best accords with textual evidence, acknowledging where the text leaves matters uncertain. (Why the name became the word for indulgence: the student evaluates explanations — hostile schools' propaganda vs. popular misunderstanding — against the textual evidence, acknowledging what the lost corpus leaves uncertain.)

### Speaking & Listening, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.1** — Initiate and participate effectively in a range of collaborative discussions (one-on-one, in groups, and teacher-led) with diverse partners on grades 11–12 topics, texts, and issues, building on others' ideas and expressing their own clearly and persuasively. (The encounter is a sustained one-on-one discussion on his last day — the student questions a witness whose reputation arrived before him.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.3** — Evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric, assessing the stance, premises, links among ideas, word choice, points of emphasis, and tone used. (His signature move is the evidence: he never argues, he dissolves — asked about feasting he defines pleasure, and the nudge beats train the student's ear for how the calm does the work.)

### Writing, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.W.11-12.1** — Write arguments to support claims in an analysis of substantive topics or texts, using valid reasoning and relevant and sufficient evidence. (The closing question — whom do you believe? — is designed as a post-encounter argument prompt, per the teacher instructions.)

## 3. South Carolina — Social Studies College- and Career-Ready Standards (2019)

Grade band: **Grade 6** for the classical-civilizations anchors. (No SC high-school standard names Epicurus or Hellenistic philosophy; the 9–12 weight of this encounter is carried by the skills standards above.)

- **6.1.P** (Grade 6) — Analyze the shift from early to classical civilizations and the enduring contributions of classical civilizations. (Hellenistic Athens's enduring contribution: Epicurean philosophy — the Garden, the physics of atoms and void, the ethics of pleasure rightly understood. Grade-band caveat: this is SC's only classical-Greece content anchor, and it sits in Grade 6.)
- **6.1.E** (Grade 6) — Analyze multiple perspectives on the political, intellectual, and social achievements of classical societies through a variety of primary and secondary sources. (The *Letter to Menoeceus* vs. the hostile Stoic tradition — primary and secondary sources offering directly opposed perspectives on the same intellectual achievement.)

*Verified against: IXL's South Carolina standards pages (grade-6 social studies); commonplanner.com SC Grade 6 compilation. (The 2019 standards are sometimes dated "adopted 2020" in compilations — same document.)*

## 4. Other states (quick hits)

- **9.3c** — A period of peace, prosperity, and cultural achievements can be designated as a Golden Age. (Students "examine the achievements of Greece" to determine whether the civilization experienced a Golden Age — Hellenistic philosophy, and the Garden as an achievement of it, belongs to the evidence set.)

*Note: 9.3b (empires consolidating power via belief systems, law, and military force) is a poor fit here — Epicurus's teaching is the opposite of power-consolidation — and is not cited. This encounter's NY anchor is 9.3c alone.*

- **WH.1.B** (§113.42) — Identify major causes and describe the major effects of the following events from 500 BC to AD 600: the development of the classical civilizations of Greece, Rome, Persia, India (Maurya and Gupta), China (Zhou, Qin, and Han), and the development of major world religions. (Epicurus's Athens (341–270 BC) sits inside the band; the encounter's cause-and-effect terrain is how a philosophy survived its founder — the Garden under Hermarchus, the physics crossing to Rome in Lucretius.)

*Verified against: commonplanner.com compilation of the 2022-adopted World History Studies TEKS — not the TEA-hosted PDF. Citation of the code is exact; the indicator text should be rechecked against tea.texas.gov before publication use.*

- **SS.6.W.3.6** (Grade 6) — Determine the impact of key figures from ancient Greece. (The tighter content anchor; Epicurus is not among the named examples (Aristotle, Hippocrates, Homer, Plato, Socrates) — he sits in the "key figures" category. Grade-band caveat.)

(Epicurus is not named in the parenthetical — the "e.g." list is not closed, but the fit is via the "important Greek figures" category; the Garden's ethics and physics are the enduring contribution.)
