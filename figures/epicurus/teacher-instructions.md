# Meet Epicurus — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Epicurus (341–270 BC), the Athenian philosopher who founded the Garden, run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — on his last day, and at the end each student answers the closing question: two thousand years have called him a glutton; his own letter says otherwise. Who do you believe? Judge him.

The encounter is the library's de-mythologizing figure in miniature: the student arrives expecting the "epicurean" of the modern word — indulgent, gluttonous — and meets the actual man, who taught that pleasure is the absence of pain, lived on bread and water, and called a pot of cheese a costly meal. He does not argue; he dissolves the question. The legend ate the man, and the student watches it happen to a reputation in real time.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

Students meet a man on his dying day. He speaks calmly about his own death — "death is nothing to us" is his doctrine, and he is living it. His fatal illness is described plainly and briefly when asked (a urinary blockage and dysentery; the hot bath he steps into at the end). No graphic detail — but a dying man is the setting, and grief is in the room. Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Epicurus page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: 25–30 minutes for the encounter, 15–20 for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the opening carefully: he names the slander in his first breath and hands the student the first thread. Watch how the tutorial and the opening work together.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "They say you taught people to feast. Is that true?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. The encounter's beats, in four steps:
   - The tutorial opens and hands off (read-only; it sets the closing question from the start).
   - You question him — pleasure, death, the gods, the atoms, the Garden, the slander.
   - He closes with the closing question and stops.
   - You run the debrief.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Epicurus — the philosopher whose name became the word "epicurean." This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. At the end he will ask you to judge him: two thousand years called him a glutton; his own letter says otherwise. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Epicurus answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- Three bracketed nudges fire once each, at load-bearing moments (the cheese, the dying day, the gods). They point at what the student just read.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Pacing

Opening: 2–3 exchanges. Don't let students camp on biography — the cheese is the doorway, and it's in the early pool. Middle: 8–12 exchanges across death, the gods, the atoms, friendship. Late: the slander, then the closing. If a student reaches the closing in under 15 exchanges, they rushed; the suggestions will slow them down. Total encounter: 25–30 minutes.

## The goal, stated plainly

The student meets the man the legend ate, and leaves carrying one question they cannot put down: two thousand years called him a glutton; his own letter says otherwise — who do you believe? Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer.

## The debrief — do not skip this

Six fixed sections, in order: his name; what the history records; what came after (the slander's two-thousand-year career, Lucretius carrying the physics to Rome); the plain record (the slander was false — an ascetic of joy); the mechanism (he dissolves rather than argues; the dying-day setting as proof); the question that remains. Then discussion, not read-aloud — open with these, built to produce disagreement:

- He says the gods exist but don't care. Is that belief — or atheism wearing a disguise?
- He says pleasure is the absence of pain. Does that describe a life worth wanting, or a life of avoidance?
- "Death is nothing to us." Did he prove it, or just define it away?
- Is the Garden a philosophy or a commune — and does it matter which?
- Two thousand years of slander versus his own letter: what evidence would settle it for you?

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this. His stall lines are also in the prompt — he will hand the student a question himself.
- **The model moralizes or breaks calm.** The voice is scripted as the calmest in the library; if it lectures, restate: "Answer as Epicurus, in one short paragraph."
- **The model claims he knows the future** (Lucretius, the word "epicurean," Christianity). That is a date-discipline failure. Restate: "You know nothing after your death."

## Historical notes for the teacher

- **Almost everything he wrote is lost** — some three hundred works gone. The voice is bounded by what Diogenes Laertius copied in full: three letters and the Principal Doctrines. Tell students this; it explains why the figure is so spare.
- **The Letter to Menoeceus is short enough to read whole.** Assign it as the classroom text — the ethics complete in a few pages. It is the single best pre- or post-reading for this encounter. (R.D. Hicks translation is public domain; the Inwood & Gerson *Epicurus Reader* is the recommended modern classroom text.)
- **The slander started in his lifetime.** Timocrates, an apostate pupil, wrote that Epicurus vomited twice a day from feasting; Stoic opponents circulated forged letters. The modern meaning of "epicurean" is the endpoint of a two-thousand-year smear campaign, not a description of the man.
- **"Epicurean" then vs. now is the entry hook.** Put the dictionary definition on the board before the encounter; take it down after the debrief. The word-history does the teaching.
- **The swerve is not his.** The atomic "swerve" (clinamen) is Lucretian. Epicurus's surviving words say nothing of it; the figure never names it.
- **Lucretius is the bridge, not the figure.** Lucretius's *De rerum natura* carried Epicurean physics to Rome a century and a half after Epicurus died. He is a suggested future library figure — do not build him from this encounter.
- **Dates wobble by a year** (341–270 vs. 341–271) depending on the chronology source. Noted in sources.md; it does not affect the encounter.

## One last thing

The moment to watch for is the cheese. When he answers the gluttony charge with a pot of cheese, don't move on — stop the room and ask what just happened to the accusation. That is the whole encounter in one exchange: a definition dissolving a slander. Teach them to notice it.
