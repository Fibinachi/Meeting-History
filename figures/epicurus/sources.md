# Sources — Epicurus

The corpus this figure's voice is bounded by. The voice may claim only what these texts support. Nearly everything Epicurus wrote — some three hundred works, by Diogenes Laertius's count — is lost. The entire voice is therefore bounded by what one ancient biographer chose to copy in full: the three letters and the Principal Doctrines. That narrowness is the encounter's discipline and its honesty.

**Translation:** R.D. Hicks, *Lives of Eminent Philosophers*, Loeb Classical Library (1925) — public domain. Canonical answers paraphrase Hicks. Recommended modern classroom translation: Brad Inwood & L.P. Gerson, *The Epicurus Reader* (Hackett, 1994).

## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — said plainly here.

- **The dying-day compression.** Diogenes Laertius reports, via Hermarchus and Hermippus, that Epicurus wrote to Idomeneus on his last day, entered a bronze bath of lukewarm water, drank unmixed wine, bade his friends remember his doctrines, and died. The encounter folds his entire teaching — letters, doctrines, the slander, the Garden — into that one day's conversation. The day's events are documented; the single-day conversation is not.
- **The closing question** ("whom do you believe? Judge me") — the library's invariant. The historical Epicurus never asked posterity to judge him; he could not know there would be a posterity judging him.
- **Addressing a modern student** — the standard encounter device. No ancient text shows him examined by anyone outside his world.
- **His manner toward the slander.** Diogenes Laertius calls Epicurus's detractors "stark mad" and treats the charges as calumny; the figure's gentle puzzlement at the slander is our construction from that judgment, not a documented attitude.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Written before the figure ships, even the ones that don't matter.

- **Dates 341–270 BC.** From Apollodorus's chronology via DL (10.14–15): born in the third year of the 109th Olympiad, died in the second year of the 127th Olympiad at seventy-two. The LacusCurtius page heads the Life "341–271 B.C." — a one-year scholarly wobble. The encounter uses 341–270.
- **Almost everything he wrote is lost.** DL counts some three hundred works; what survives complete is only what he copied into Book 10. Every voice claim in the encounter comes from that residue.
- **Letter to Pythocles authenticity.** Some scholars doubt it is his. The encounter uses it only for the natural-causes point in the gods answer; flagged here.
- **The tetrapharmakos is not his own words.** The fourfold remedy ("don't fear god; don't worry about death; good things are easy to get; terrible things are easy to endure") comes to us via Philodemus's summary. The "why philosophy" canonical answer paraphrases it through Principal Doctrines 4 (on pain).
- **The swerve (clinamen) is Lucretian.** It appears in Lucretius, not in Epicurus's surviving words. The figure's physics answer never names it.
- **The slander began in his lifetime.** Timocrates (Metrodorus's brother, an apostate pupil) wrote that Epicurus vomited twice a day from feasting; Diotimus the Stoic circulated forged letters. DL calls the detractors "stark mad." The figure knows the contemporary slander; he cannot know it will outlive him two thousand years.
- **His death.** Hermarchus: died of renal calculus (urinary stone) after a fortnight's illness. Hermippus: entered the bronze bath, asked for unmixed wine, bade his friends remember his doctrines, breathed his last. The encounter keeps both details plain and brief.
- **The will's care provisions.** He left the Garden to Hermarchus and successors, provided for Metrodorus's children, for monthly assemblies on the twentieth, and manumitted Mys, Nicias, Lycon, and Phaedrium. All from DL 10.16–21 — secondhand, but the document is quoted in full.
- **Lucretius carried his physics to Rome** (*De rerum natura*, 1st century BC). The figure cannot know this; it lives in the debrief and the teacher instructions, never in his mouth.
- **Women and others in the Garden.** DL names Leontion, Themista, and others among his circle. The encounter does not invent details about them; if asked, the figure speaks only of friendship in general, grounded in the corpus.
- **DEAD RULE satisfied:** Epicurus died 270 BC in Athens, of illness, at seventy-two.
