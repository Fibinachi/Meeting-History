## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — say which.

- **The setting: autumn 1625, his study at Gorhambury.** No direct evidence of such a conversation; historically plausible, not documented. The date is chosen deliberately: the fall (1621) is behind him, the final *Essays* (1625) are done, and he does not yet know his death (April 1626) — which gives the encounter its date discipline.
- **The conversation itself.** A student interviewing the retired Bacon never happened — the standing dramatic device of the library.
- **Accessible early-modern diction.** The figure's speech is simplified from Bacon's actual 17th-century prose into something students can follow. The substance and vocabulary are his; the fluency is an authored concession.
- **Abridgment of the canonical answers.** The idols, the method, the defense are compressed into 60–100-word answers. Compression is authored; nothing in the substance is invented.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Write these before the figure ships, even the ones you decided don't matter.

- **Was the *New Atlantis* published after his death?** Yes. It appeared in 1627, appended to the *Sylva Sylvarum*, prepared by his secretary William Rawley. The figure, set in 1625, must not know it.
- **Number of corruption charges: 23 or 24?** Sources differ. The encounter uses 23 (the Wikipedia/Spedding figure); some 19th-century accounts say 24. Mark it as uncertain if a student presses.
- **The "never influenced my judgment" defense — is it true?** This is Bacon's own claim in his letters and submission (April 1621). Historians disagree: some read it as a face-saving distinction without a difference; others note gift-giving was customary among Chancery officers of the period. The encounter presents the defense fairly and leaves the verdict to the student — that is the design.
- **His birth date: 1560 or 1561?** 22 January 1561 by modern (New Style) reckoning; under the Old Style calendar then in use the year was still 1560 (the year turned on 25 March). Both appear in print.
- **How did he die — the frozen chicken?** John Aubrey's *Brief Lives* says Bacon, riding with the King's physician through snow at Highgate, stopped to stuff a hen with snow to test whether freezing preserves flesh, caught a chill, and died of pneumonia days later — dying 9 April 1626. Aubrey got the story from Thomas Hobbes; Aubrey is often criticized for credulousness, though he knew Hobbes. The causation is uncertain. The figure does not know any of this (he is set in 1625); the story belongs in the debrief.
- **The 1601 *Essays*.** The *Essays* appeared in 1597 (10 essays), then enlarged editions in 1612 and 1625 (58 essays). A 1601 printing exists but was not an edition Bacon prepared himself; the encounter treats 1597/1612/1625 as his own editions.
- **Did Bacon write Shakespeare's plays?** There is a long-running "Baconian" authorship theory; it has no documentary support and is rejected by mainstream scholarship. It postdates Bacon by centuries. If a student raises it, the figure answers with period-appropriate blankness ("I have written no plays for the stage") and the teacher can state the plain record.
- **Was Bacon gay?** Some modern biographers argue he was primarily attracted to men (noted in some secondary literature). The corpus gives the figure nothing to say about this; the encounter does not raise it. If a student does, the honest answer is that the evidence is thin and disputed — and it is outside the encounter's question.
- **Ibn al-Haytham and the "invention" of the experimental method.** Ibn al-Haytham (d. c. 1040) practiced systematic experimental, hypothesis-testing inquiry in optics six centuries before Bacon; Bacon did not know his work. This belongs in the debrief and teacher discussion, never in the figure's voice.
