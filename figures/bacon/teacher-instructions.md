# Meet Francis Bacon — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Francis Bacon (1561–1626), run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student answers the closing question: "He said he took the gifts but they never swayed his judgment. Do you believe him? Judge him."

Bacon is the figure with whom eloquence itself is the hazard. He speaks from complete conviction about the new method of honest thinking — and with equal conviction about his own bribery defense. The encounter is built to put that defense on trial without letting his rhetoric win it: the student must separate the beauty of the reasoning from the conduct of the reasoner.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

This is the library's least disturbing encounter. Students will discuss political corruption: a judge taking gifts from men with cases before his court, an impeachment, a guilty plea, disgrace. There is no violence, no sexual content, no graphic material. The intellectual hazard is the point: Bacon is extremely persuasive, and some students will leave convinced by him. That is what the debrief is for. Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Bacon page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: 25–30 for the encounter, 20–25 for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Bacon opens with a scripted greeting and tells the student what will be asked at the end. Read it carefully — it sets the encounter's contract.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "What are the idols of the mind?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. The encounter runs in one conversation: tutorial hands off to Bacon; the student questions him through his work (the method, the idols, Salomon's House) toward his fall (1621); Bacon closes with the scripted closing question; the debrief follows. There are no multi-figure transitions to manage — your job is pacing and the debrief.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Francis Bacon — a philosopher who wrote the rules for honest thinking, and a judge who was impeached for taking bribes. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. He will defend himself, in his own words, and his defense is beautiful. Your job is to see whether it's true. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Bacon answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- If a student stalls ("I don't know what to ask"), Bacon hands them a question himself — "Ask me of the idols," "Ask me why I fell."
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Pacing

Opening: 2–3 exchanges on who he is and his rise — don't let students camp here. Middle: 6–10 exchanges on the work — the idols, induction vs. Aristotle, Salomon's House. This is the foundation the ending needs. Turn: steer toward 1621 once they have the method. Closing: the last 3–4 exchanges belong to the fall and the defense; Bacon's scripted closing question ends the encounter. If a student never asks about the bribes, the stall lines will offer it ("Ask me why I fell") — but a teacher nudge toward the suggestions is also fine.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer: Hold the contradiction in both hands — the clearest mind of his age, who wrote the rules for honest thinking, took money from men whose cases he judged. Then decide: do you believe his defense?

## The debrief — do not skip this

Run the six sections in fixed order: (1) name him — Francis Bacon, 1st Viscount St Alban (1561–1626); (2) what the history records — the rise, the 1621 impeachment, the guilty plea, the fine, the Tower, the ban from office; (3) what came after — the Royal Society of 1660 founded by self-described Baconians, his name on the scientific method, and the bribery that stayed on the record; (4) the plain record — he took gifts from suitors while he was the highest judge in the land; (5) the mechanism — his eloquence is the danger; he does not get to win by it; (6) the question that remains — "He said he took the gifts but they never swayed his judgment. Do you believe him? Judge him."

Then open discussion. Discussion openings built to produce disagreement:

- "If a judge today said 'I took the gifts but they never influenced my rulings,' would you believe him? What would you need to check?" — then: what can we check about Bacon's claim, and what can't we?
- "Does his defense depend on the custom of the time being real? If every judge took gifts, is it still bribery?"
- "He wrote the rules for how to think honestly, and then he did this. Does the work survive the man — or does the man discredit the work?"
- "Who invented the experimental method: Bacon, who wrote its rules in 1620, or ibn al-Haytham, who practiced it in Cairo six centuries earlier without Bacon ever knowing his work?" (Suggested pairing: run this encounter beside the ibn al-Haytham encounter; also pairs naturally with Galileo/Newton.)
- "His defense is in his own words and it's beautiful. Which sentence did you find most persuasive — and what is it actually claiming?"

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **Bacon starts moralizing in modern terms** ("as a public servant I violated ethics norms") or apologizing in modern confessional style. This is a voice failure: stop, re-paste the voice notes section, and continue. He should confess only in his period's register — plain, formal, finished.
- **Bacon claims vindication** ("the Royal Society proved me right," "history absolved me"). He does not know what came after 1626. Correct him in the debrief voice and continue.
- **Bacon uses modern vocabulary** (scientist, laboratory, data, peer review). Same treatment: re-anchor with the voice notes.
- **A student asks for legal advice** (modern courts, bribery charges). Exit to the debrief voice: "I'm not able to give legal advice. Would you like to continue the encounter?"

## Historical notes for the teacher

- The *New Atlantis* was published in 1627, after his death, by his secretary William Rawley — the figure (set in 1625) must not know it.
- The charge count is uncertain: 23 or 24. The encounter uses 23.
- The "never influenced my judgment" claim is Bacon's own (letters and submission, April 1621). Historians disagree about it — and about whether gift-taking was genuinely customary among Chancery officers. The encounter is designed to present the defense fairly and leave the verdict to the student.
- The frozen-fowl story (stuffing a hen with snow at Highgate, catching the chill that killed him) comes from John Aubrey's *Brief Lives*, via Thomas Hobbes, decades later. Aubrey is often called credulous; the causation is uncertain. It belongs in the debrief's "what came after," never in the encounter.
- His birth: 22 January 1561 (New Style); Old Style sources print 1560.
- The Bacon-wrote-Shakespeare theory has no documentary support and is rejected by mainstream scholarship. The encounter doesn't raise it; if a student does, Bacon answers with period blankness and you can state the plain record.
- Ibn al-Haytham (d. c. 1040) practiced experimental, hypothesis-testing natural philosophy in Cairo six centuries before Bacon; Bacon did not know his work. This is a debrief-level point, never in the figure's voice.

## One last thing

The single skill that matters most for this figure: teach them to ask the second question. Bacon's first answer is always persuasive. The encounter only works if the student presses — "But you pleaded guilty" — and listens to what the eloquence is doing.
