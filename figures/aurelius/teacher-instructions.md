# Meet Marcus Aurelius — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Marcus Aurelius (AD 121–180) — the Roman emperor who wrote his private philosophy in Greek between battles on the Danube — run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him by typing questions, in his winter quarters at Carnuntum in AD 178, in the second year of the second Marcomannic war, two years before his death on the frontier.

The encounter's engine is the collision the brief names: the philosopher-king whose reign was endless war — and the Christian persecutions happened under him. Your students will meet the most private man in the library: the *Meditations* were never meant for publication, and the figure is not performing for the student — the student is overhearing him. The philosophy he offers is complete, beautiful, and airtight. It never touches the persecutions. At the end each student renders the closing judgment: **the philosopher on the throne, writing about detachment between battles — while the persecutions went on in his name. Judge him.**

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

Students will encounter the following, stated plainly by the figure or the debrief:

- **Persecution and martyrdom.** The encounter states the Lyon rescript plainly: the obstinate die, those who recant go free. The debrief describes the Lyon martyrdoms of AD 177 in Eusebius's account — imprisonment, torture, beheading of citizens, condemnation to the beasts. The figure never describes these for effect, and neither should the room.
- **War.** The Danube campaigns as the background of everything; killing as imperial duty, stated plainly and without glory.
- **Plague and mass death.** The Antonine Plague as the reign's constant background; the figure's philosophy of death is beautiful and absolute, and some students will find it cold.
- **The persecutions honesty requirement.** This encounter must not become an exoneration. The figure's position — the rescript went in his name; what his hand weighed is not in the record — is the historically honest boundary, not a defense. The debrief states the scholarly dispute outright and leaves it unresolved. If your room cannot hold that unresolvedness, do not run the encounter.

Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Marcus Aurelius page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: ~25–30 for the encounter, ~20–25 for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the tutorial, then his opening. He invites you to begin with the pages or the river — take him up on one of them.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try the philosophy first: "What is in my control?" Watch what happens: he answers from 8.47 in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. Try the hard turn: ask him "What did you write about the Christians?" Watch the 11.3 line land at full strength. Then ask: "What happened at Lyon in 177?" Watch the administrative answer — and watch what he does not say. That silence is the encounter's load-bearing evidence.
6. Try the son: "Will your son be a good emperor?" Watch the gap — the few words, the unnamed heir. Then let him close: he asks the closing question and stops. Run the debrief on yourself before you run it on anyone else — especially section 5 (the mechanism), which is the thing most likely to slip past you on a first run.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Marcus Aurelius — the Roman emperor who wrote philosophy to himself, in Greek, in a war camp on the Danube. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. He wrote one line about the Christians in twelve books, and he never wrote about Lyon. Your job is to decide what that means. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- He answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief — and they often close on a line from the notes. That line is doing work; teach students to notice it.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- He asks back rarely, and bluntly. That is the journal's own form — he wrote to himself — not a trick. But students should notice when a blunt question is doing the work of an evasion.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.
- If a student stalls ("I don't know what to ask"), he hands them a question — the war, Fronto, or the Christians. He never lectures them about asking.

### The private-journal frame — say this out loud

Your students are reading what was never meant for them. That is the encounter's power and its problem, and the ethics of it are discussable: is it fair to judge a man by a private journal he never meant you to read? Does the privacy of the writing make it more honest evidence, or less fair evidence? Let them argue it in the debrief. Do not resolve it for them.

### Pacing

Opening: the pages or the river — 2–3 exchanges. Don't let students camp on biography; the philosophy is the encounter's surface and the silences are its depth. Middle: 8–12 exchanges is a full encounter. Push them toward the two silences (Lyon, which the journal never mentions; Commodus, whom it never names) — that is where the judgment lives, not in the aphorisms. Closing: when the exchanges thin or you call time, let him give the closing question. Do not rush the closing; it is the sentence the debrief is built on.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer: to feel the full force of the most beautiful philosophy ever written by a man in power — and to notice what it leaves untouched. At the end you answer for yourself: the philosopher on the throne, writing about detachment between battles — while the persecutions went on in his name. Judge him.

## The debrief — do not skip this

The six fixed sections, in order: (1) name him — Marcus Aurelius (born Marcus Annius Verus), AD 121–180, Roman emperor, author of the *Meditations*; (2) what the history records — ruled 161–180, most of it on the Danube fighting the Marcomannic Wars, plague hollowing the legions, the Cassius revolt of 175, death on the frontier 17 March 180, Commodus made co-emperor 177 and succeeding him, the private Greek journal surviving by manuscript luck; (3) what came after — Commodus ending the wars, the arena, the strangling in 192, civil war; the journal copied c. 900 by Arethas, surviving in two 14th-century manuscripts (one lost, known through the 1558–59 first printing), becoming the world's most-read Stoic text; the philosopher-king reputation; the forged rain-miracle letter; none of which he knew; (4) the plain record — Lyon 177: mobbed, imprisoned, tortured, executed after a referral answered in his name (the obstinate die, those who recant go free); no evidence of a general persecution ordered by him, the standing Trajanic law already covering it, his one written line contemptuous, the silence on Lyon total; he named his son heir and the succession undid the reign; (5) the mechanism — the overheard journal, the inwardness as evidence, the two silences as the evidence he never offers, the complete philosophy that leaves the persecutions untouched; (6) the question that remains — the philosopher on the throne, writing about detachment between battles, while the persecutions went on in his name. Judge him. That question does not close when you leave this room.

Discussion openings (built to produce disagreement):

- He gives you the philosophy at full strength — did it touch the persecutions? Should it have?
- "The law did what the law does." Is that a defense or a confession?
- He never wrote about Lyon — not one line in twelve books. What does the silence tell you?
- He never names Commodus in the journal. What does that tell you?
- Is it fair to judge a man by a private journal he never meant you to read?
- The philosopher on the throne, writing about detachment between battles — while the persecutions went on in his name. Judge him.

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **The model turns him into a modern self-help guru.** This is the most likely failure mode: dispensing life advice, affirming the student, going warm and therapeutic. That is not Marcus Aurelius — that is the model's default register wearing his name. His voice is weary, terse, and inward; he never performs warmth. If it happens, stop and re-paste the prompt; do not try to repair it mid-stream.
- **The model lets him apologize for the persecutions or condemn them in modern terms.** He never does. He states the 11.3 line and the administrative record, marks the boundary of what is known, and hands back the verdict. A Marcus who calls the persecutions wrong — or who claims he tried to stop them — is inventing beyond the record. Re-paste.
- **The model invents remorse, deliberation, or personal weighing of the Lyon referral.** The scripted boundary is the honest position: the reply went in his name; what his hand weighed is not in the record. Hold it.
- **The model narrates Commodus's reign or claims knowledge of anything after 17 March 180.** Date discipline is absolute. Re-paste.
- **The model uses the viral paraphrase** ("You have power over your mind — not outside events") as his words. It is a modern paraphrase, not a translation; the genuine anchor is 8.47. Correct it once, out of character if needed, and move on.

## Historical notes for the teacher

The honest answers to "did it really happen like that?" — one bullet per caveat from sources.md:

- The 12-book division is probably not his; the chapter numbers date to the 17th century. The rubrics sit at different book boundaries depending on the edition.
- The text survives on two witnesses: the 14th-century *Codex Vaticanus Graecus* 1950 (corrupt, ~42 lines lost) and the lost *Codex Palatinus*, known through Xylander's first printed edition (Zurich, 1558–59). The earliest evidence for the work is c. 900 (Arethas of Caesarea).
- 11.3 is his only mention of Christians. Whether it proves personal hostility or only philosophical disdain is debated; it is the only evidence in his own hand.
- His personal responsibility for the persecutions is genuinely disputed among historians. No source shows him ordering a general persecution; the persecutions were local, under standing Trajanic law. The reply at Lyon went in his name. State the dispute; do not resolve it.
- The rain-miracle letter attributed to him (in Justin Martyr's *First Apology*) is considered inauthentic by modern scholars. Dio credits an Egyptian magician, Harnuphis.
- Fronto died c. 166/167; the plague cause is unproven. His anti-Christian speech survives only as a fragment in Minucius Felix; whether Marcus knew of it is undocumented.
- The *Meditations* never name Commodus. Commodus was made Caesar in 166 and co-emperor in 177 (a few sources say 176).
- He died 17 March 180, age 58, of unknown causes, at Vindobona or near Sirmium — the place is disputed.
- The *Historia Augusta*'s Life of Marcus is unreliable; don't build on any single anecdote from it (including the Faustina gossip).
- The winter at Carnuntum in AD 178 is a compression for the encounter; the rubrics place some writing there, but no single winter is documented.

## One last thing

Don't let the beauty of the philosophy do the judging. That is the single skill this encounter builds. The philosophy is the evidence, not the verdict — and it was written by the defendant, to himself, never meant for you. Teach them to ask the second question: not "is it wise?" — it is, devastatingly — but "what did it leave untouched?" The moment a student says "but he has an answer for everything," you have them exactly where the encounter wants them. Ask what that fact tells them.
