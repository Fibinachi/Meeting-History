# Sources — Marcus Aurelius

The corpus this figure's voice is bounded by. The voice may claim only what these texts support. The voice comes from two sources only: the *Meditations* (the anchor) and the Fronto correspondence (the young Marcus, and the teacher). Everything else below is context for facts the figure states about his own life — never voice.

Named translation: **Gregory Hays, *Meditations: A New Translation* (Modern Library, 2002)** — the recommended classroom translation. Verbatim English quotations in the prompt are from the public-domain **George Long translation (1862)**, except 5.20, quoted briefly from Hays and attributed.

## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — said plainly here.

- **The winter at Carnuntum, AD 178.** A compression. The manuscript rubrics place some of the writing "among the Quadi, on the Gran" and "at Carnuntum," and Marcus campaigned from Carnuntum in the 170s — but no single winter is documented as the encounter's setting.
- **The student as overhearer.** The *Meditations* are addressed to himself (*Ta eis heauton*); the encounter redirects that address to the student. No ancient source documents such a conversation. This is the encounter's central device and its central ethical problem (see teacher instructions).
- **The opening's invitation ("you ask, I answer") and the closing question.** Pedagogical. The notes open with observations, not invitations; the invitation serves the encounter's judgment engine.
- **Knowledge through his death (17 March 180).** The template default, kept deliberately: he knows Commodus succeeds him, but nothing of Commodus's reign, the journal's survival, or his reputation.
- **The Lyon rescript as stated by the figure.** Follows Eusebius's contemporary letter (5.1): the referral, the reply (the obstinate die, those who recant go free). The figure's boundary — "whether my hand weighed it, the record does not say" — is the honest position: no source shows Marcus's personal engagement with the referral.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Written before the figure ships, even the ones that don't matter.

- **The 12-book division is probably not his.** The joins are editorial; the familiar chapter numbers date only to the 17th century. The rubrics ("among the Quadi, on the Gran"; "at Carnuntum") sit at the end of Book 1 / start of Book 2 and end of Book 2 / start of Book 3 depending on the edition.
- **The text rests on two witnesses.** The *Codex Vaticanus Graecus* 1950 (A, 14th century, corrupt, ~42 lines lost) and the lost *Codex Palatinus* (T), reconstructed through Xylander's *editio princeps* (Zurich, 1558–59). The earliest concrete evidence for the work's existence is c. 900, when Arethas of Caesarea had a copy made. The history of the text before the 14th century is largely obscure.
- **11.3 is his only mention of Christians in the corpus.** The tone is dismissive ("mere obstinacy... without tragic show"). Whether the passage proves personal hostility or only philosophical disdain is debated; it is the only evidence in his own hand.
- **His personal responsibility for the persecutions is genuinely disputed.** The standard view: the reply at Lyon went in his name, and 11.3 shows his attitude. The minority view (e.g. Bodek, summarized in Schaff): the persecutions did not originate with him and were local initiatives under standing law. No source shows him ordering a general persecution; Trajan's framework (not sought out; punished if accused and obstinate) remained the law. The encounter states the dispute and does not resolve it.
- **The rain-miracle letter is considered inauthentic.** A letter attributed to Marcus, appended to Justin Martyr's *First Apology*, credits Christian prayers for the rain that saved his army; modern scholars consider it a Christian composition. Cassius Dio credits an Egyptian magician, Harnuphis. The Column of Marcus Aurelius in Rome depicts the miracle.
- **Fronto died c. 166/167** (Haines) or in the late 160s, possibly of the Antonine Plague — the plague cause is unproven.
- **Fronto's anti-Christian speech survives only as a fragment** preserved in Minucius Felix's *Octavius*. Whether Marcus ever knew of it is undocumented; the figure never mentions it.
- **The *Meditations* never name Commodus.** The standard reading; the figure's few words about his son are the marked gap.
- **Commodus: Caesar in 166, co-emperor in 177** (a few sources give 176 for the elevation). He was 15/16 — the youngest consul Rome had seen.
- **Death: 17 March 180, age 58, of unknown causes** — possibly the plague; the cause is not established. The place is disputed: Vindobona (modern Vienna) or near Sirmium (modern Sremska Mitrovica). He was deified; his ashes went to Hadrian's mausoleum.
- **The *Historia Augusta*'s Life of Marcus is unreliable** — useful for chronology, not for voice or for any single anecdote (including the Faustina gossip, which the figure does not touch).
- **"You have power over your mind — not outside events. Realize this, and you will find strength" is a modern paraphrase**, not a translation of any single passage. The genuine anchor is 8.47. Teachers should not present the viral line as his words.
- **Lucius Verus died in 169**; the plague is the common attribution but not certain.
- **DEAD RULE satisfied:** Marcus Aurelius died 17 March 180, on the Danube frontier.
