# Meet Marcus Aurelius — Standards Alignment (verified 2026-10-15)

Encounter: the student overhears the most private man in the library. The *Meditations* were notes to himself, in Greek, never meant for another reader — and the philosophy is complete, beautiful, and airtight, and it never touches the persecutions. The two load-bearing silences: Lyon (never mentioned in twelve books) and Commodus (never named). His reign was endless war on the Danube; Christians were executed in his name at Lyon in 177. It closes with the handoff question: *the philosopher on the throne, writing about detachment between battles — while the persecutions went on in his name. Judge him.*

**Grade band fit for every framework below: 9–12** (high school ELA literacy / World History). Note: most states' *content* anchors for classical Rome sit in lower grades (see caveats); the 9–12 weight of this encounter comes from skills standards — private-source evaluation, competing-evidence judgment, argumentation — which are band-matched.

## 1. C3 Framework (National Council for the Social Studies, 2013)

Grade band: **9–12**. (Indicator texts verified against curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim; the official NCSS PDF was not directly retrievable in this session — see verification note at end.)

This encounter is the library's flagship **private-source-as-evidence** exercise: a journal addressed to no one, read against the public record of what happened under its author's name.

### Dimension 2 — History

- **D2.His.11.9-12** — Critique the usefulness of historical sources for a specific historical inquiry based on their maker, date, place of origin, intended audience, and purpose. (The headline indicator for this encounter. The *Meditations*: maker (the emperor himself), date (the 170s, written between dispatches at Carnuntum), intended audience (none — *Ta eis heauton*, "to himself"), purpose (self-exercise, not record). The student tests its usefulness for the specific inquiry — what did he know about Lyon? — and finds the source's most important feature is what it never mentions.)
- **D2.His.9.9-12** — Analyze the relationship between historical sources and the secondary interpretations made from them. (*Meditations* 11.3 ("mere obstinacy") vs. Eusebius's *Ecclesiastical History* 5.1 (the contemporary letter from the churches of Vienne and Lyons) → the genuinely disputed secondary interpretations of his personal responsibility: the standard view (the reply went in his name, 11.3 shows his attitude) vs. the minority view (local initiatives under standing law). The encounter states the dispute and does not resolve it.)
- **D2.His.10.9-12** — Detect possible limitations in various kinds of historical evidence and differing secondary interpretations. (The *Meditations* rest on two late witnesses with a largely obscure transmission history; the *Historia Augusta*'s Life of Marcus is flagged unreliable throughout; no source shows his personal engagement with the Lyon referral — the student maps the limitations of each kind of evidence before judging.)
- **D2.His.16.9-12** — Integrate evidence from multiple relevant historical sources and interpretations into a reasoned argument about the past. (The closing question — judge him — demands integrating the journal, the 11.3 line, the Lyon reply, Trajan's standing rescript, and the disputed interpretations into one reasoned verdict.)

### Dimension 4 — Communicating Conclusions and Taking Informed Action

- **D4.1.9-12** — Construct arguments using precise and knowledgeable claims, with evidence from multiple sources, while acknowledging counterclaims and evidentiary weaknesses. (The student's verdict must acknowledge the counterclaim the encounter holds honestly: the standing-law defense — Christians not sought out, the obstinate punished once accused — and the evidentiary weakness that no source shows his hand on the Lyon referral.)
- **D4.4.9-12** — Critique the use of claims and evidence in arguments for credibility. (The encounter's discipline: treating the philosophy as evidence rather than verdict — the complete, airtight Stoicism never exonerates him, and 11.3 is his own hand's evidence of his attitude toward the Christians.)

*Verified against: resources.finalsite.net (Greenwich Public Schools C3 curriculum); mnhs.org and historicfortsnelling.com (Minnesota Historical Society standards-connections documents); learninglab.si.edu; thinkport.org. Official document: College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework for Social Studies State Standards, NCSS, 2013 (socialstudies.org/c3).*

## 2. Common Core ELA — Literacy in History/Social Studies, Speaking/Listening & Writing

Grade band: **9–12**. (Verified against the official Common Core State Standards Initiative site, thecorestandards.org.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 9–10

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.6** — Compare the point of view of two or more authors for how they treat the same or similar topics, including which details they include and emphasize in their respective accounts. (*Meditations* 11.3 vs. Eusebius 5.1 on the Christians: Marcus emphasizes "obstinacy... without tragic show"; Eusebius emphasizes the martyrs' suffering and the imperial reply — compared detail by detail, including what each omits.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.1** — Cite specific textual evidence to support analysis of primary and secondary sources, attending to such features as the date and origin of the information. (8.47 on the ruling part, 11.3 on the Christians, the Carnuntum rubric — cited with attention to the manuscript's late and partial transmission.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.6** — Evaluate authors' differing points of view on the same historical event or issue by assessing the authors' claims, reasoning, and evidence. (The Lyon inquiry at full strength: the emperor's single dismissive passage vs. the churches' contemporary letter — same events, opposed points of view, each assessed on claims, reasoning, and evidence.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.3** — Evaluate various explanations for actions or events and determine which explanation best accords with textual evidence, acknowledging where the text leaves matters uncertain. (His responsibility for the persecutions: personal hostility vs. standing imperial law operating without his engagement — the encounter marks the honest position ("whether my hand weighed it, the record does not say") and the standard asks students to hold that uncertainty rather than resolve it by assumption.)

### Speaking & Listening, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.1** — Initiate and participate effectively in a range of collaborative discussions (one-on-one, in groups, and teacher-led) with diverse partners on grades 11–12 topics, texts, and issues, building on others' ideas and expressing their own clearly and persuasively. (The encounter is a sustained one-on-one discussion with a man who believes he is writing to himself — the student examines the witness by watching what he writes about and what he doesn't.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.3** — Evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric, assessing the stance, premises, links among ideas, word choice, points of emphasis, and tone used. (The weary, inward register is the evidence: the philosophy's completeness, stated as settled fact, set against the two silences — Lyon and Commodus — that the tone never acknowledges.)

### Writing, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.W.11-12.1** — Write arguments to support claims in an analysis of substantive topics or texts, using valid reasoning and relevant and sufficient evidence. (The closing question — judge him — is designed as a post-encounter argument prompt, per the teacher instructions.)

## 3. South Carolina — Social Studies College- and Career-Ready Standards (2019)

Grade band: **Grade 6** for the classical-civilizations anchors. (No SC high-school standard names Marcus Aurelius or the Antonine age; the 9–12 weight of this encounter is carried by the skills standards above.)

- **6.1.P** (Grade 6) — Analyze the shift from early to classical civilizations and the enduring contributions of classical civilizations. (Rome is within the Grade 6 course's scope ("world civilizations to 550"); the *Meditations* — Stoic philosophy from the imperial throne — is among Rome's enduring contributions. Grade-band caveat: this is SC's only classical-Rome content anchor, and it sits in Grade 6.)
- **6.1.E** (Grade 6) — Analyze multiple perspectives on the political, intellectual, and social achievements of classical societies through a variety of primary and secondary sources. (The *Meditations* vs. Eusebius's Lyon letter — a primary philosophical source and a secondary ecclesiastical source offering directly opposed perspectives on the same Roman achievement, imperial order.)

*Verified against: IXL's South Carolina standards pages (grade-6 social studies); commonplanner.com SC Grade 6 compilation. (The 2019 standards are sometimes dated "adopted 2020" in compilations — same document.)*

## 4. Other states (quick hits)

### New York (K–12 Social Studies Framework, Grades 9–10)

- **9.3b** — Empires used belief systems, systems of law, forms of government, military forces, and social hierarchies to consolidate and expand power. (The strongest NY fit in the whole set: Rome is named in the indicator's performance guidance, and the Lyon beat *is* the law in action — Trajan's rescript, unrevoked, applied to the Christians of Lugdunum in 177 — while the Marcomannic wars are the military force holding the frontier.)
- **9.3c** — A period of peace, prosperity, and cultural achievements can be designated as a Golden Age. (Students "examine the achievements of... Rome" to determine whether the civilization experienced a Golden Age — the Antonine age, the philosopher-emperor, and the *Meditations* belong to the evidence set.)

*Verified against: the NYS K–12 Social Studies Framework via nysacourse.weebly.com (official framework PDF mirror, revised February 2017).*

### Texas (TEKS, World History Studies, adopted 2022)

- **WH.1.B** (§113.42) — Identify major causes and describe the major effects of the following events from 500 BC to AD 600: the development of the classical civilizations of Greece, Rome, Persia, India (Maurya and Gupta), China (Zhou, Qin, and Han), and the development of major world religions. (Marcus's Rome (AD 161–180) sits inside the band; the encounter's cause-and-effect terrain includes the development of Christianity under imperial Rome — the Lyon martyrs of 177.)

*Verified against: commonplanner.com compilation of the 2022-adopted World History Studies TEKS — not the TEA-hosted PDF. Citation of the code is exact; the indicator text should be rechecked against tea.texas.gov before publication use.*

### Florida (NGSSS / State Academic Standards)

- **SS.912.W.2.13** — Explain how Western civilization arose from a synthesis of classical Greco-Roman civilization, Judeo-Christian influence, and the cultures of northern European peoples promoting a cultural unity in Europe. (Marcus embodies the synthesis: a Roman emperor writing philosophy in Greek while the Judeo-Christian movement grew under his rule — and was persecuted in his name.)
- **SS.6.W.3.8** (Grade 6) — Determine the impact of significant figures associated with ancient Rome. (The tighter content anchor for the man himself; Marcus is not among the named examples (Julius Caesar, Augustus, Constantine the Great) — he sits in the "significant figures" category. Grade-band caveat.)

*Verified against: fldoe.org instructional-materials documents (official, for SS.912.W.2.13); commonplanner.com and socialstudiesonline.com Grade 6 Florida compilations (for SS.6.W.3.8). Codes are stable across NGSSS and the 2023 State Academic Standards adoption.*

### California (History–Social Science Content Standards, Grade 6)

- **6.7.8** — Discuss the legacies of Roman art and architecture, technology and science, literature, language, and law. (The *Meditations* as a Roman legacy in literature — with the honest complication that he wrote it in Greek; the Lyon episode as the Roman legacy in law, applied.)

*Grade-band caveat: CA's Rome anchors sit in Grade 6; CA high-school HSS has no imperial-Rome anchor. Note 6.7.8 names legacy categories, not figures — the fit is via "literature" and "law," not a named-emperor slot.*
