## What is his own words vs. what is the record around him

- **His own words:** the Correspondance (letters and orders, mostly dictated — that dictated-at-speed character IS the voice); the bulletins and proclamations; the Josephine letters. The voice's vocabulary, facts, and silences are bounded by these.
- **Around him, not his:** the famous St. Helena lines. "Ma vraie gloire… c'est mon Code civil" and the "Spanish ulcer" image come down through the Mémorial tradition — reported speech, not written text. "Je suis la Révolution" is reported in Mme de Rémusat's memoirs. The "quarante siècles" of the Pyramids proclamation is reported by Bourrienne. The prompt never lets the voice present any of these as documents; where the figure states the underlying belief, sources.md says which are secondhand.
- **Notably thin or absent in his own corpus:** he never counts the dead of any campaign; the bulletins give strength returns and victories, not losses. That silence is scripted as a boundary, not filled.

## The honest caveat (for the teacher — read before running)

The Correspondance is the largest self-record any conqueror ever left — forty thousand letters, mostly dictated at speed, orders and decisions rather than reflections. It is also, like the bulletins, an instrument of rule: what he wrote was what he wanted known, and the speed is part of the seduction. The prompt treats the dictation voice as **his case** — inexhaustible, certain, and always moving before the question lands — and the debrief supplies what it omits: the reimposition of slavery (May 1802), the quarter-million French dead in Spain (Esdaile), the 612,000-to-112,000 arithmetic of Russia, and the 29th bulletin's lie of omission. The encounter's whole pedagogy is the student slowing down a voice no one ever slowed down. Do not let the student leave with only his version. That is what the debrief is for.

## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — say which.

- **The meeting itself (a single sitting, one conversation covering his life)** — pure dramatic device. No such interview occurred.
- **The setting: Longwood House, St. Helena, April 1821, dictating with the student as "new secretary"** — the dictation circle at Longwood is documented (Las Cases, Montholon, Gourgaud); placing the student in it is pure dramatic device. The April 1821 date is chosen so that he knows his whole life through his death and nothing after.
- **Compression of a lifetime into an hour** — the encounter has him speak on events twenty-five years apart as present-tense dictation. Dramatic compression.
- **The scripted phrasings** — the boundaries they mark (what the corpus supports vs. what is secondhand) are documented; the sentences themselves are authored, including the English renderings.
- **"He answers before the question is finished"** — the dictated-at-speed character of the Correspondance is documented; turning it into a live interview mechanic (answering the first half of a question, snapping "The other?") is authored.
- **The "truth is a munition" bulletins answer** — the 29th bulletin's line is documented; the sentences confessing the bulletins' function are authored. The device is honest: the propagandist explains the propaganda in his own register.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Written before the figure ships, even the ones that don't matter.

- **Death: when and of what?** 5 May 1821, Longwood House, St. Helena. The autopsy reported stomach cancer; later arsenic-poisoning theories are disputed and unresolved. The figure knows he is dying and does not know the day.
- **The Code civil: when?** Promulgated 21 March 1804 (30 Ventôse Year XII); renamed the Code Napoléon in 1807. The figure says "March 1804" without the day.
- **The coronation: 2 December 1804, Notre-Dame de Paris.** He took the crown from the altar and placed it on his own head; Pope Pius VII was present; he then crowned Joséphine. The figure's phrasing ("No one gives me what is mine — I take it") is authored; the act is documented.
- **The 1804 plebiscite.** The official result was overwhelmingly yes — commonly given as 3,572,329 to 2,569 — and widely considered a managed vote. The figure says "three and a half million"; he never mentions the management.
- **His role in drafting the Code.** Sources commonly say he presided over 57 of the 84 Council of State sessions devoted to the Code; the figure gives no number and says only that he "argued it article by article."
- **Russia 1812: the figures.** ~612,000 entered Russia (24 June 1812), ~112,000 returned; estimates vary by historian and by whether non-combatants are counted. The figure says "six hundred thousand" and "the road back ate the army" — no precise loss figure, which is honest to the corpus: he never gave one.
- **Borodino, Moscow, the retreat.** Borodino: 7 September 1812; Moscow entered 14 September, largely empty, then the fire; the 29th bulletin dated 3 December 1812 (Molodechno); he left the army at Smorgoni on 5 December and reached Paris on 18 December. Casualty figures for Borodino alone vary wildly and are not used.
- **"Ma vraie gloire… c'est mon Code civil."** Reported from the St. Helena conversations via the Mémorial tradition — secondhand. It is not in the Correspondance. The figure states it as his conviction, never as a written text.
- **"The Spanish ulcer."** Attributed to him in the St. Helena tradition; the exact sentence ("the Spanish ulcer ruined me") is reported speech, not a document. The prompt lets him use "ulcer" as his word and flags the transmission here.
- **"Je suis la Révolution."** Reported in Mme de Rémusat's memoirs — secondhand. Never quoted as his words; the prompt uses the authored "ended the Revolution by completing it" phrasing.
- **"Du haut de ces pyramides, quarante siècles vous contemplent."** Reported by Bourrienne — secondhand. Not used as a canonical quote.
- **Slavery: the legal mechanics.** The law of 20 May 1802 (30 Floréal Year X) reimposed slavery in the French colonies, reversing the 16 Pluviôse Year II (4 February 1794) abolition — the legal application across colonies (Martinique, Guadeloupe, Saint-Domingue) is complex; the debrief states the plain version and this caveat carries the nuance. Toussaint Louverture was arrested June 1802 and died at the Fort de Joux, April 1803; Haiti declared independence 1 January 1804.
- **Spain: the dates.** The Dos de Mayo uprising: 2 May 1808; the Bayonne abdications: May 1808; Joseph Bonaparte placed on the throne June 1808; Bailén (Dupont's surrender): July 1808 — the first major open-field defeat of a Napoleonic army in Europe. The war ran 1808–1814. The figure gives "six years" and names no battles.
- **Waterloo: 18 June 1815;** Ligny 16 June; Grouchy's failure to march to the guns is the traditional account (historians debate the orders). The figure gives the traditional account — it is what he believed.
- **The Hundred Days.** Landed at Golfe-Juan 1 March 1815; second abdication 22 June 1815; surrendered to the British (HMS Bellerophon) 15 July 1815; arrived St. Helena October 1815.
- **The "forty thousand letters."** The Fondation Napoléon edition publishes 40,497 letters; his total output was larger (estimates run higher; much is lost). His "forty thousand" is his round of the published count.
- **Josephine.** The divorce was pronounced December 1809 (civil) / January 1810 (religious annulment); he married Marie-Louise by proxy March 1810, in person April 1810; the King of Rome (Napoleon II) was born 20 March 1811.
- **Was he short?** He was about 5'6"–5'7" (1.68–1.70 m) — average for the period; the "short Napoleon" is British propaganda. Non-load-bearing; belongs to classroom Q&A.
