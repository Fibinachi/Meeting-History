# Meet Napoleon — Standards Alignment (verified 2026-10-15)

Encounter: Napoleon Bonaparte, dictating at Longwood House in April 1821, gives his own account at dictation speed — the Code, the crown, Spain, Russia — and the student must slow him down, notice what he counts (men, battles, marches) and what he never counts (the dead), and hold his case against the debrief's plain record. He recuses himself from the verdict absolutely. Closing question: *"He gave Europe the Code and gave himself the crown. Liberator or tyrant? Judge him."*

Key beats: the Code as "the Revolution written so it cannot be unwritten" — and what it buried (the married woman's legal incapacity, the father's absolute authority); the 1802 reimposition of slavery and Saint-Domingue; the self-crowning with the Pope watching; Spain as "the ulcer"; Russia 1812 in his telling vs. the debrief's arithmetic (612,000 crossed, ~112,000 returned); the 29th bulletin's lie of omission ("the Emperor's health has never been better"); the Mémorial lines as secondhand belief, never written text.

**Grade band fit for every framework below: 9–12** (high school World History / AP European History). All content anchors below sit in the high-school band; there are no grade-band caveats for this figure.

## 1. C3 Framework (National Council for the Social Studies, 2013)

Grade band: **9–12**. (Indicator texts verified against curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim; the official NCSS PDF was not directly retrievable in this session — see verification note at end.)

- **D2.His.3.9-12** — "Use questions generated about individuals and groups to assess how the significance of their actions changes over time and is shaped by the historical context." (His own St. Helena assessment — "what nothing will erase is my Code" — against the two-hundred-year liberator-or-tyrant argument the debrief describes; the student tracks how his significance was contested across each era.)
- **D2.His.4.9-12** — "Analyze complex and interacting factors that influenced the perspectives of people during different historical eras." (The Revolution's chaos, the coalitions ringed against France, the plebiscite — the factors behind his "the Revolution needed an emperor.")
- **D2.His.6.9-12** — "Analyze the ways in which the perspectives of those writing history shaped the history that they produced." (The bulletins were "orders to the nation" — instruments of rule; the St. Helena dictations were legend-building in the room, via Las Cases. The encounter names both as *his* case, never the settled account.)
- **D2.His.8.9-12** — "Analyze how current interpretations of the past are limited by the extent to which available historical sources represent perspectives of people at the time." (He never counted the dead of any campaign; the bulletins give strength returns and victories, not losses — the silence is in the sources, and the student must read it.)
- **D2.His.9.9-12** — "Analyze the relationship between historical sources and the secondary interpretations made from them." (The Correspondance — dictated orders, his pen — against the Mémorial — reported speech through Las Cases; the prompt keeps them explicitly separate, and the student learns why.)
- **D2.His.10.9-12** — "Detect possible limitations in various kinds of historical evidence and differing secondary interpretations." (The "Code" statement and the "ulcer" image are secondhand by definition — he may state them as beliefs, never as written text; the student detects the transmission gap.)
- **D2.His.11.9-12** — "Critique the usefulness of historical sources for a specific historical inquiry based on their maker, date, place of origin, intended audience, and purpose." (The 29th bulletin: maker the Emperor, audience France, purpose the campaign — "truth is a munition"; the Correspondance: dictated orders, not reflections.)
- **D2.His.14.9-12** — "Analyze multiple and complex causes and effects of events in the past." (Why the Empire fell: Spain, Russia, the coalitions, the Continental System — in his telling and in the record, held together.)
- **D2.His.15.9-12** — "Distinguish between long-term causes and triggering events in developing a historical argument." (Long-term: the Revolution's instability and the wars of the coalitions; triggering event: Brumaire, November 1799.)
- **D2.His.16.9-12** — "Integrate evidence from multiple relevant historical sources and interpretations into a reasoned argument about the past." (The closing judgment: the Correspondance, the bulletins, the Mémorial tradition, and the debrief's arithmetic — integrated, with his recusal honored.)

### Dimension 4 — Communicating Conclusions and Taking Informed Action

- **D4.1.9-12** — "Construct arguments using precise and knowledgeable claims, with evidence from multiple sources, while acknowledging counterclaims and evidentiary weaknesses." (The closing question, with the Code as the counterclaim to the crown — the verdict must carry both.)
- **D4.2.9-12** — "Construct explanations using sound reasoning, correct sequence (linear or nonlinear), examples, and details with significant and pertinent information and data, while acknowledging the strengths and weaknesses of the explanation given its purpose." (Explaining the 1812 campaign from his bulletins — then explaining what the bulletins omitted, with the omission named as the explanation's weakness.)
- **D4.4.9-12** — "Critique the use of claims and evidence in arguments for credibility." (The 29th bulletin as the practice case: "my health was excellent" — true, and saying nothing about the army dying around him.)

## 2. Common Core ELA — Literacy in History/Social Studies & Speaking/Listening

Grade band: **9–12**. (Verified against the official Common Core State Standards Initiative site, thecorestandards.org, 2026-10-15.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 9–10

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.1** — Cite specific textual evidence to support analysis of primary and secondary sources, attending to such features as the date and origin of the information. (The 29th bulletin, 3 December 1812, Molodechno — date, origin, and what it says vs. what it doesn't.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.2** — Determine the central ideas or information of a primary or secondary source; provide an accurate summary of how key events or ideas develop over the course of the text. (His account of the 1812 campaign, summarized back at dictation speed — then checked.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.6** — Compare the point of view of two or more authors for how they treat the same or similar topics, including which details they include and emphasize in their respective accounts. (His Russia telling — "the road back ate the army" — vs. the debrief's arithmetic: which details each includes.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.8** — Assess the extent to which the reasoning and evidence in a text support the author's claims. (Does the 29th bulletin's reasoning support its implied claim that all is well? The encounter's second load-bearing nudge.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.9** — Compare and contrast treatments of the same topic in several primary and secondary sources. (The Code in his telling vs. the Code's actual provisions on women and paternal authority.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.1** — Cite specific textual evidence to support analysis of primary and secondary sources, connecting insights gained from specific details to an understanding of the text as a whole. ("La santé de l'Empereur n'a jamais été meilleure" — one sentence that unlocks the whole bulletin system.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.2** — Determine the central ideas or information of a primary or secondary source; provide an accurate summary that makes clear the relationships among the key details and ideas. (His case: the Code kept, the crown necessary, the Empire the Revolution's heir.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.6** — Evaluate authors' differing points of view on the same historical event or issue by assessing the authors' claims, reasoning, and evidence. (His 1812 account vs. the campaign record — claims, reasoning, and evidence on both sides.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.8** — Evaluate an author's premises, claims, and evidence by corroborating or challenging them with other information. (His Code claims — "one law for all Frenchmen" — corroborated and challenged against the Code's actual text: equality before the law kept, women's legal capacity buried.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.9** — Integrate information from diverse sources, both primary and secondary, into a coherent understanding of an idea or event, noting discrepancies among sources. (Correspondance + bulletins + Mémorial + debrief — the discrepancies, especially the secondhand lines, are flagged rather than smoothed.)

### Speaking & Listening

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.3** — Evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric, assessing the stance, premises, links among ideas, word choice, points of emphasis, and tone used. (The dictation speed *is* rhetoric to evaluate: he answers before the question is finished, counts everything except the dead — the student must slow him down, which is the lesson.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.9-10.3** — Evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric, identifying any fallacious reasoning or exaggerated or distorted evidence. (The bulletins' lie of omission, named in his own register: "Truth is a munition.")
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.1** / **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.9-10.1** — Initiate and participate effectively in a range of collaborative discussions (one-on-one, in groups, and teacher-led) with diverse partners on grades 9–10 / 11–12 topics, texts, and issues, building on others' ideas and expressing their own clearly and persuasively. (The encounter is a sustained one-on-one dictation-session; the verdict discussion is the deliberation.)

### Writing, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.W.11-12.1** — Write arguments to support claims in an analysis of substantive topics or texts, using valid reasoning and relevant and sufficient evidence. (The closing question — liberator or tyrant — is designed as a post-encounter argument prompt, per the teacher instructions; his recusal means the argument is entirely the student's.)

## 3. South Carolina — Social Studies College- and Career-Ready Standards (2019), Modern World History

Grade band: **9–12** (high school Modern World History).

- **MWH.2.P** — Analyze significant developments resulting from colonists in the Western Hemisphere applying the principles of the Enlightenment in the creation of the new political institutions during the period 1765–1815. (SC's official inquiry guidance for this indicator: it "was developed to encourage inquiry into the European Enlightenment, and how the ideas of the Enlightenment led to the British, American, French, Haitian, and Latin American Revolutions." ## Verification caveats

1. **C3 Framework**: indicator texts were verified against multiple independent curriculum documents that quote the Framework verbatim (they agree with each other); the official NCSS PDF itself was not directly retrievable this session. Treat the official publisher as NCSS (2013), socialstudies.org/c3.
2. **South Carolina**: MWH.2.P's code and inquiry guidance were verified via the IMS Global cartridge metadata quoting the official 2019 indicator support language, cross-checked against the HMH correlation's MWH numbering structure. The ed.sc.gov-hosted PDF was not directly retrievable — recheck against ed.sc.gov before print citation.
3. **Texas**: WH.9.B verified against the 2022 TEKS compilation (mkt.commonplanner.com), not the TEA-hosted document. The 2022 revision (implemented 2024–25) renumbered strands; confirm the code against tea.texas.gov before classroom use.
4. **The secondhand boundary as standard**: the prompt's rule — Mémorial lines stated as belief, never as written text — is a design rule, not a standard, but it is what makes D2.His.9.9-12, D2.His.10.9-12, and D2.His.11.9-12 genuinely exercisable rather than notional. The alignment claims the rule as served by those standards, not as cited authority.
5. **His recusal and D4.1**: because the figure never renders the verdict, the D4.1 argument-construction work falls entirely on the student — which is why the closing question is built as the D4.1 prompt rather than as a figure-delivered judgment.
