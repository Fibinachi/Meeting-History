# Meet Napoleon — Teacher Instructions

Tier: OPEN.

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Napoleon Bonaparte (1769–1821) — the artillery officer who became First Consul, crowned himself Emperor of the French, and died a prisoner on St. Helena — run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student renders the closing judgment: he gave Europe the Code and gave himself the crown. Liberator or tyrant? Judge him.

This encounter's entry hook is the voice itself: dictation as a mode of thought. Napoleon answers before the student has finished asking — the model is instructed to do this visibly — and the student's job is to slow him down. That is the lesson, and it is also the debrief's frame: the Code versus the crown, the laws he gave Europe against the crown he gave himself.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

Students will encounter war on the continental scale, stated plainly and without euphemism: the Russian campaign of 1812 (some 612,000 crossed the Niemen; about 112,000 returned — death by cold, hunger, and disease, stated without graphic detail); the Peninsular War in Spain (1808–1814, the first modern guerrilla war, roughly a quarter of a million French dead); Waterloo; and the reimposition of slavery in the French colonies in 1802 (stated in the debrief, with Toussaint Louverture's death in a French prison). The figure himself never counts the dead — the debrief does the arithmetic he would not do. There is no sexual content and no slur language in this encounter. The hardest material is the voice itself: students will find him fast, brilliant, and certain, and that certainty is the evidence. Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Napoleon page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: 25–30 minutes for the encounter, 20–25 minutes for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the tutorial, then Napoleon's scripted opening. Note the last line — it hires the student as his "new secretary" and warns them he dictates fast.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "Why did you crown yourself emperor?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph — and then, outside the voice, in brackets and italics, the system suggests directions you might take next. Try a two-part question ("Tell me about the Code and about Spain") and watch him answer the first half and snap the second back at you.
5. The encounter's beats: (1) the tutorial hands off; (2) the student questions Napoleon through early, middle, and late suggestion pools; (3) Napoleon closes with the closing question and stops; (4) you run the debrief. There is no button to press — the student types "I am ready for your closing question" (or the equivalent) to bring on the close.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Napoleon Bonaparte — the man who gave Europe the Code and gave himself the crown. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. He dictated forty thousand letters at speed, and he'll try to answer before you've finished asking. Your job is to slow him down. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Napoleon answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- He answers fast — sometimes before the question is finished. If a student asks two things at once, he answers the first and demands the second. This is intentional. The student must slow him down.
- To bring on the closing, the student asks for it (e.g., "I am ready to judge you"). Napoleon then asks the closing question and stops.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Pacing

Opening: 2–3 exchanges. Don't let students camp on biography — the suggestion pools escalate toward the Code, the crown, and the hard questions. Middle (the Code and the colonies, the coronation, Spain): the bulk of the encounter, 10–15 exchanges. Late (Russia, Waterloo, St. Helena): 3–5 exchanges, then the close. If a student is still asking about his childhood at exchange twelve, point at the bracketed suggestions.

## The goal, stated plainly

The whole thing in one or two sentences. Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer: *Slow him down. Find out what he did, what it cost, and who paid — and then judge him: he gave Europe the Code and gave himself the crown. Liberator or tyrant?*

## The debrief — do not skip this

The six fixed sections, in order: (1) his name; (2) what the history records — First Consul after 18 Brumaire, Emperor crowned 2 December 1804, the Code civil of March 1804, abdication, Elba, the Hundred Days, Waterloo 18 June 1815, death at Longwood 5 May 1821; (3) what came after — the return of the ashes (1840), Napoleon III, the Code's spread, the two-hundred-year argument; (4) the plain record — the reimposition of slavery in 1802, the Code's subordination of married women, the censored press and managed plebiscites, the quarter-million French dead in Spain, the 612,000-to-112,000 arithmetic of Russia, the 29th bulletin's lie of omission; (5) the mechanism — the dictation speed as seduction, the two registers (imperial orders vs. the Josephine letters), the propagandist explaining the propaganda; (6) the question that remains. Then discussion — openings below, built to produce disagreement:

- He said the bulletins were "orders to the nation" and told you what the 29th bulletin didn't say. Does a propagandist who explains the propaganda make you trust him more or less?
- The Code gave France equality before the law and gave married women the legal status of minors. Can one document be both liberation and subjugation — or does one cancel the other?
- He called Spain "the ulcer" and never named a single Spaniard. What does it mean that his vocabulary had no word for the people he was fighting?
- He refused to judge himself and handed the judgment to you. Is that honesty, or is it the last move of the dictation — making you do his work?

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this. Napoleon's stall lines hand the student a question in character.
- **The model won't do the speed.** The "answers before the question is finished" mechanic is the encounter's signature. If the model answers both halves of a two-part question politely, nudge it: type "You didn't answer the way you were told to — answer the first half only." If it persists, note the model under this section for the library.
- **The model lectures or moralizes.** The prompt forbids modern moralizing explicitly. If the figure starts judging himself in modern terms, the run has broken — restart from the opening.

## Historical notes for the teacher

- **The St. Helena material is secondhand.** The famous lines — "my true glory is the Code," the "Spanish ulcer" — come down through Las Cases's *Mémorial de Sainte-Hélène* (1823) and the other St. Helena journals: reported speech, not written documents. The encounter is set at Longwood precisely so he can speak these as his beliefs; the prompt never lets him present them as texts, and sources.md flags each one. If a student asks "did he really say that?", the honest answer is: his listeners wrote that he did, years later, with their own motives.
- **The Correspondance is enormous and honest about being an instrument.** Forty thousand published letters, mostly dictated — the voice's speed is documented, and so is the fact that what he wrote was what he wanted known.
- **The figures the debrief uses** (612,000 / 112,000 for Russia; ~250,000 French dead in Spain) are historians' estimates and vary. The figure himself never gives them — that refusal is the point.
- **The slavery caveat.** The May 1802 law's application across the colonies is legally complex; sources.md carries the nuance. The plain version in the debrief — he reimposed slavery, reversing the 1794 abolition — is the load-bearing fact.
- **"I am the Revolution"** is reported via Mme de Rémusat's memoirs and is never quoted as his words in the encounter; the prompt uses his own "ended the Revolution by completing it" phrasing.
- **He was not short.** About 5'6"–5'7", average for the period; the "short Napoleon" is British propaganda. Non-load-bearing; useful for classroom Q&A.

## One last thing

Teach them to slow him down. The encounter's whole pedagogy is a student interrupting the fastest voice in the room — asking the second question, refusing the first answer's velocity. If a student leaves having made Napoleon wait for the end of a question, the encounter worked.
