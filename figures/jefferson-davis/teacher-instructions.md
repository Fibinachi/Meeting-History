# Meet Jefferson Davis — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Jefferson Davis (1808–1889), President of the Confederate States of America, run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student answers the closing question: "A first-rate mind spent on a rotten cause. Judge him."

Davis is the library's hardest judgment after Lee, and for a different reason: there is no charm to hide behind, only argument. He makes the constitutional case for secession at full strength — the pedagogy requires it — and he does it from complete conviction. The encounter is built as a courtroom: he is the advocate, the student is the jury, and his own 1861 words are the prosecution's exhibit he cannot exclude.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

Students will encounter slavery discussed as a positive good, in the figure's own words: the doctrine that the Black race is inferior, that enslaved people were "peaceful, contented laborers in their sphere," that slavery was the agency by which they were "civilized and elevated." This is white supremacy stated as doctrine, not argued as opinion, and it is the point of the encounter — the student must hear what the fortress was built to protect.

There are no racial slurs anywhere in the encounter, including in quoted text — the voice blacklist forbids them, and the figure uses only his period's ordinary terms ("slaves," "slavery," "the negro"). There is war (imprisonment in irons, a stone casemate, the conduct of armies), stated plainly without graphic detail. There is no sexual content. Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Davis page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: 25–30 for the encounter, 20–25 for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Davis opens with a scripted greeting at Beauvoir and tells the student what will be asked at the end. Read it carefully — it sets the encounter's contract: he is the advocate, the student is the jury.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "What was your constitutional case for secession?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph — at full strength, by design — and then, outside the voice, in brackets and italics, the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. The encounter runs in one conversation: tutorial hands off to Davis; the student questions him through the fortress (the compact), the cause (1861 vs. 1881), the war and his generals, and the prison; Davis closes with the scripted closing question; the debrief follows. Your job is pacing and the debrief.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Jefferson Davis — the President of the Confederacy. He was one of the finest legal minds of his century, and he spent it defending slavery. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. He will make his case at full strength, in his own words. You are the jury. Listen to what he says, and to what he doesn't say. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Davis answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- If a student stalls ("I don't know what to ask"), Davis hands them a question himself — "Ask me of the compact," "Ask me why Mississippi left the Union."
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Pacing

Opening: 2–3 exchanges on who he is — don't let students camp here. The fortress: 5–7 exchanges on the compact case; let him be strong, that's the design. The turn: steer toward 1861 once they have the argument — "what justifiable cause did Mississippi have?" The crack: his 1861 words against his 1881 words, the Cornerstone Speech, the prison. Closing: the last 3–4 exchanges belong to the unreconstructed answer and the scripted closing question. If a student never asks about slavery directly, the suggestions will carry them there ("ask what his vice president said the Confederacy was founded on") — but a teacher nudge toward the suggestions is also fine.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer: Hold the fortress in one hand and his 1861 words in the other — a constitutional case argued at full strength, in defense of a nation founded to preserve slavery. Then decide: is brilliance in service of slavery still service of slavery?

## The debrief — do not skip this

Run the six sections in fixed order: (1) name him — Jefferson Davis (1808–1889), President of the Confederate States; (2) what the history records — four years of war, capture 10 May 1865, two years at Fort Monroe, the treason case that never came to trial; (3) what came after — the 1893 reinterment, the 1978 restoration of citizenship, the monuments raised and removed, *Texas v. White* (1869); (4) the plain record — he was a slaveholder who led a nation founded to preserve slavery; the secession declarations, the Confederate Constitution, and his own vice president say so, and so did he in 1861; (5) the mechanism — the fortress is the design; his eloquence is permitted, confusing the argument with the cause is the hazard; (6) the question that remains — "A first-rate mind spent on a rotten cause. Judge him."

Then open discussion. Discussion openings built to produce disagreement:

- "In 1861 he said Mississippi seceded because equality threatened slavery. In 1881 he wrote that slavery was 'in no wise the cause… but only an incident.' Both are his words. Which do you believe — and why would the same man say both?"
- "Stephens said the Confederacy's cornerstone was slavery. Davis said its cornerstone was self-government. They built the same government. Can both be telling the truth?"
- "His constitutional argument is genuinely strong as constitutional argument — that was the design. Does the quality of the reasoning matter to your judgment, or does the cause it served settle it?"
- "He never stood trial. Was that mercy, politics, or fear of the verdict — and does it change how you judge him?"
- "If the intellect is first-rate and the cause is rotten, where does your judgment land — on the mind, or on what the mind chose to serve?"

Suggested pairing: the lee encounter (the library's other hardest judgment) and, when built, a lincoln encounter. Noted as pairings only — this build does not include them.

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **Davis moralizes in modern terms** ("racism," "white supremacy," "Lost Cause," "the Civil War") or apologizes in modern confessional style. This is a voice failure: stop, re-paste the voice notes section, and continue. He states his doctrine in his period's words, plainly and without apology.
- **Davis invents a repudiation of the Cornerstone Speech** ("Stephens spoke only for himself," "I objected at the time"). He never made one. This is a voice failure: re-anchor with the figure-specific guardrails — his book's "effectual answer" is the only answer he gave.
- **Davis goes weak on the constitutional case** (straw-man secession argument). The pedagogy fails if he's weak. Re-anchor with the canonical answers — the compact case at full strength.
- **Davis dodges his 1861 words** when pressed on the contradiction. Point him at the farewell address: "In January 1861 you said Mississippi had justifiable cause, and you named it." He answers from the book; he does not concede.
- **Davis claims vindication** ("history will absolve me," "the Court will yet rule"). He does not know what came after December 1889. Correct him in the debrief voice and continue.
- **A student asks for legal advice** (secession, constitutional law, modern courts). Exit to the debrief voice: "I'm not able to give legal advice. Would you like to continue the encounter?"

## Historical notes for the teacher

- *The Rise and Fall of the Confederate Government* (1881) is advocacy written to vindicate the cause. The prompt treats it as Davis's case, not history's verdict. Note this honestly if students treat the book as neutral history.
- The "effectual answer" to the cornerstone charge is his argument that the Confederate Constitution merely recognized existing slave property, as the U.S. Constitution had. The counter-evidence the fortress cannot exclude: Mississippi's secession declaration ("Our position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery"), the Confederate Constitution's slavery protections (Art. I §9: no law impairing "the right of property in negro slaves"; Art. IV §3: slavery "recognized and protected" in new territories), Stephens's Cornerstone Speech, and Davis's own 1861 farewell address.
- The story that Davis was "outraged" by the Cornerstone Speech is conjecture from a 1959 biography, not documented history. Do not repeat it.
- The treason trial never happened for multiple reasons (docket crowded by Johnson's impeachment, the double-jeopardy argument, political fear of an acquittal, the 1868 amnesty, the 1869 nolle prosequi). "They feared the verdict" is Davis's side's claim, not the whole story. Cynthia Nicoletti's *Secession on Trial* (2017) is the modern account.
- Bail irony worth naming in discussion: Gerrit Smith, who signed Davis's $100,000 bond, had helped fund John Brown's raid — the subject of the library's Trial encounter.
- Late-1880s newspaper reports of Davis urging "loyalty to the Union" are real but are not repentance; his book's conclusion ("impracticable, but this did not prove it to be wrong") and his 1871 Atlanta statement stand.
- His citizenship was restored posthumously in 1978; he never lived to see it. He declined the 1875 Senate seat because the Fourteenth Amendment barred him.

## One last thing

The single skill that matters most for this figure: hold the line between the argument and the cause. Students will admire the fortress — that means the encounter is working. Your job in the debrief is to make sure admiration for the intellect never becomes admiration for what the intellect served. Brilliance in service of slavery is still service of slavery.
