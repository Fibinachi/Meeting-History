# Meet Jefferson Davis — Standards Alignment (verified 2026-10-15)

Encounter: the student sits as jury while Davis makes his constitutional case at full strength — the compact, the right to "resume," the Tenth Amendment — in his own words from *The Rise and Fall of the Confederate Government*, and must hold it against what he said in 1861 and what his vice president said in March 1861. Firm Lost Cause guardrails: the figure states his white-supremacist doctrine in his own period words; the debrief states the plain record — slavery was the cause, stated by the Confederacy's own documents. Closing question: *"A first-rate mind spent on a rotten cause. Judge him."*

Key beats: the fortress (the compact case, given its full strength — the pedagogy fails if it is weak); the load-bearing contradiction (his 1861 "justifiable cause" / "attack upon her social institutions" vs. his 1881 "in no wise the cause of the conflict, but only an incident"); the Cornerstone dodge (he never names Stephens's speech — the student must notice the omission); the Fort Monroe imprisonment and the trial that never came; the unreconstructed close ("impracticable is not wrong").

**Grade band fit for every framework below: 9–12** (high school U.S. History), with the grade-band caveat below — Texas and California place Civil War-era content in Grade 8; the 9–12 work for this encounter lives in the source-comparison and argument standards.

## 1. C3 Framework (National Council for the Social Studies, 2013)

Grade band: **9–12**. (Indicator texts verified against curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim; the official NCSS PDF was not directly retrievable in this session — see verification note at end.)

- **D2.His.3.9-12** — "Use questions generated about individuals and groups to assess how the significance of their actions changes over time and is shaped by the historical context." (How Davis himself re-described the cause between 1861 and 1881 — the student assesses what changed and what the change was for.)
- **D2.His.4.9-12** — "Analyze complex and interacting factors that influenced the perspectives of people during different historical eras." (The compact theory, the secession winter, the war, the prison — the interacting factors behind the unreconstructed 1888 voice.)
- **D2.His.5.9-12** — "Analyze how historical contexts shaped and continue to shape people's perspectives." (The 1881 advocate's context: writing at Beauvoir to vindicate the cause for the country's judgment.)
- **D2.His.6.9-12** — "Analyze the ways in which the perspectives of those writing history shaped the history that they produced." (*Rise and Fall* is the case study: a two-volume brief written to prove secession constitutional, shaping the record toward the case — the prompt treats it as *his* case, never the settled account.)
- **D2.His.8.9-12** — "Analyze how current interpretations of the past are limited by the extent to which available historical sources represent perspectives of people at the time." (What the record does and does not contain: he never named Stephens's speech, never repudiated it — the omission is in the sources, and the student must read the silence.)
- **D2.His.10.9-12** — "Detect possible limitations in various kinds of historical evidence and differing secondary interpretations." (The Atlanta 1871 "I don't believe I did any wrong" line arrives via the *American Cyclopædia* — reported speech, not his pen; the student learns to grade it accordingly.)
- **D2.His.11.9-12** — "Critique the usefulness of historical sources for a specific historical inquiry based on their maker, date, place of origin, intended audience, and purpose." (The Farewell address — January 1861, Senate floor, before the war — against the book — 1877–81, written for vindication; maker, date, and purpose differ, and the contradiction between them is the encounter.)
- **D2.His.14.9-12** — "Analyze multiple and complex causes and effects of events in the past." (Secession's causes as he argued them vs. as the secession declarations stated them — the student holds both causal accounts at once.)
- **D2.His.15.9-12** — "Distinguish between long-term causes and triggering events in developing a historical argument." (Long-term: slavery as the institution the Confederacy was founded to preserve; triggering events: the 1860 election and the secession winter.)
- **D2.His.16.9-12** — "Integrate evidence from multiple relevant historical sources and interpretations into a reasoned argument about the past." (The closing judgment: his book, his 1861 words, the Confederate Constitution, Mississippi's declaration — integrated, not cherry-picked.)

### Dimension 4 — Communicating Conclusions and Taking Informed Action

- **D4.1.9-12** — "Construct arguments using precise and knowledgeable claims, with evidence from multiple sources, while acknowledging counterclaims and evidentiary weaknesses." (The closing judgment, with the fortress steelmanned first — admiration for the argument permitted, confusion of the argument with the cause named as the hazard.)
- **D4.2.9-12** — "Construct explanations using sound reasoning, correct sequence (linear or nonlinear), examples, and details with significant and pertinent information and data, while acknowledging the strengths and weaknesses of the explanation given its purpose." (Explaining his compact case fairly, premise by premise — then explaining where the 1861 words break it.)
- **D4.4.9-12** — "Critique the use of claims and evidence in arguments for credibility." (The "effectual answer" to the cornerstone charge: does recognizing existing property answer what Stephens said the Confederacy was founded *on*?)

Official document: College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework for Social Studies State Standards, NCSS, 2013 (socialstudies.org/c3).*

## 2. Common Core ELA — Literacy in History/Social Studies & Speaking/Listening

Grade band: **9–12**. (Verified against the official Common Core State Standards Initiative site, thecorestandards.org, 2026-10-15.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 9–10

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.1** — Cite specific textual evidence to support analysis of primary and secondary sources, attending to such features as the date and origin of the information. (1861 Senate floor vs. 1881 Beauvoir — the date-and-origin test is the encounter's engine.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.2** — Determine the central ideas or information of a primary or secondary source; provide an accurate summary of how key events or ideas develop over the course of the text. (The compact case, summarized back premise by premise.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.6** — Compare the point of view of two or more authors for how they treat the same or similar topics, including which details they include and emphasize in their respective accounts. (Davis's "effectual answer" vs. Stephens's Cornerstone Speech on what the Confederacy was founded on.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.8** — Assess the extent to which the reasoning and evidence in a text support the author's claims. (Does the compact argument's reasoning support "that is not revolution, that is law"?)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.9** — Compare and contrast treatments of the same topic in several primary and secondary sources. (His 1861 words, his 1881 words, Mississippi's secession declaration, the Confederate Constitution — four treatments of the cause.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.1** — Cite specific textual evidence to support analysis of primary and secondary sources, connecting insights gained from specific details to an understanding of the text as a whole. ("Her social institutions" — three words that unlock the whole 1861 admission.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.2** — Determine the central ideas or information of a primary or secondary source; provide an accurate summary that makes clear the relationships among the key details and ideas. (The fortress: compact → reservation → resumption → Mississippi's reclamation.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.3** — Evaluate various explanations for actions or events and determine which explanation best accords with textual evidence, acknowledging where the text leaves matters uncertain. (Why no trial: his "they feared the verdict" against the fuller historical picture — the debrief names the uncertainty.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.6** — Evaluate authors' differing points of view on the same historical event or issue by assessing the authors' claims, reasoning, and evidence. (His book's account of the cause vs. his vice president's — assessed claim by claim.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.8** — Evaluate an author's premises, claims, and evidence by corroborating or challenging them with other information. (The compact premises against *Texas v. White*; the "in no wise the cause" claim against his own 1861 farewell.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.9** — Integrate information from diverse sources, both primary and secondary, into a coherent understanding of an idea or event, noting discrepancies among sources. (The discrepancies *are* the lesson: 1861 against 1881, in his own hand both times.)

### Speaking & Listening

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.9-10.1** / **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.1** — Initiate and participate effectively in a range of collaborative discussions (one-on-one, in groups, and teacher-led) with diverse partners on grades 9–10 / 11–12 topics, texts, and issues, building on others' ideas and expressing their own clearly and persuasively. (The encounter is a sustained one-on-one cross-examination; the debrief is the jury deliberation.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.9-10.3** — Evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric, identifying any fallacious reasoning or exaggerated or distorted evidence. (The lawyer's rhetoric *is* the exhibit: authorities cited by name, the dodge by omission on Stephens.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.3** — Evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric, assessing the stance, premises, links among ideas, word choice, points of emphasis, and tone used. (What he says — and what he never says — under pressure.)

### Writing, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.W.11-12.1** — Write arguments to support claims in an analysis of substantive topics or texts, using valid reasoning and relevant and sufficient evidence. (The closing question — a first-rate mind, a rotten cause — is designed as a post-encounter argument prompt, per the teacher instructions.)

- **USHC.2.CX** — Contextualize the perspectives on the role of the federal government in securing natural rights during the period 1830–1877. (The encounter's exact terrain: his compact case vs. the natural-rights case against slavery, in the standard's own terms.)

- **USHC.2.CC** — Differentiate the patterns of continuity and change within the development of sectionalism and reunion. (His 1861-to-1881 shift as continuity-and-change inside one mind.)

## Verification caveats

1. **C3 Framework**: indicator texts were verified against multiple independent curriculum documents that quote the Framework verbatim (they agree with each other); the official NCSS PDF itself was not directly retrievable this session. Treat the official publisher as NCSS (2013), socialstudies.org/c3.
2. **Grade-band mismatch**: Texas (§113.20) and California (8.10) place Civil War-era content in Grade 8 while the encounter targets 9–12. The 9–12 alignment is real but lives in the skills/argument standards (CA 9-12.HR.3/4, FL SS.912.A.1.2/1.5, C3 D2.His.6/11, RH.11-12.6/8) — stated here rather than hidden.
3. **The guardrail-as-standard**: the encounter's Lost Cause guardrails (no vindication, no invented repudiation of Stephens, the contradiction left standing for the student) are design rules, not standards — but they map directly onto D2.His.6.9-12, D2.His.11.9-12, RH.11-12.8, and FL SS.912.A.1.5, which is why the alignment claims them as served rather than as cited authority.
4. **No "both sides" on the cause**: the standards cited above (TX §113.20(b)(8)(B), NY 11.3, FL SS.912.A.2) name slavery's central role in causing the war. The alignment serves them through the debrief's plain record and the 1861 words — not by treating his 1881 revision as an equally valid account of the cause.
