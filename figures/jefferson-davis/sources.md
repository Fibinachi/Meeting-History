## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — say which.

- **The setting: autumn 1888, the cottage at Beauvoir.** No direct evidence of such a conversation; historically plausible, not documented. The date is chosen deliberately: *Rise and Fall* is seven years in print, the war and the prison are decades behind him, the *Short History* is nearly finished, and he does not yet know his death (December 1889) — which gives the encounter its date discipline.
- **The conversation itself.** A student interviewing the retired Davis never happened — the standing dramatic device of the library.
- **Accessible 19th-century diction.** The figure's speech is simplified from Davis's dense legalistic prose into something students can follow. The substance, the authorities cited, and the vocabulary are his; the fluency is an authored concession.
- **Abridgment and compression of the canonical answers.** The compact case, the slavery doctrine, and the generals are compressed into 60–100-word answers assembled from his sentences across the corpus. Compression is authored; nothing in the substance is invented.
- **The *Texas v. White* dismissal.** He never wrote of the 1869 ruling in *Rise and Fall*. The canonical answer paraphrases his position (force does not settle right; the Conclusion) — marked as authored paraphrase.
- **"They feared the verdict."** This is his conviction and his counsel's position (O'Conor); historians debate the reasons the trial never happened (see Nicoletti). The figure states it as his belief; the debrief gives the fuller picture.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Write these before the figure ships, even the ones you decided don't matter.

- ***Rise and Fall* is advocacy, not history's verdict.** Written 1877–81 to vindicate the Confederate cause; Davis corresponded with former Confederates for fact-checking and shaped the record toward his case. The prompt treats it as *his* case, never as the settled account.
- **The 1861 vs. 1881 contradiction on slavery is real and is the encounter's engine.** Farewell address (1861): Mississippi secedes because equality threatens "her social institutions." *Rise and Fall* (1881): slavery was "in no wise the cause of the conflict, but only an incident." Both are his words. The encounter does not resolve which he believed; the student judges.
- **The Cornerstone Speech and Davis.** There is no evidence Davis reacted to Stephens's 21 March 1861 speech — no objection in his papers, his wife's memoir, or Stephens's writings. The story that Davis was "outraged" by it originates as conjecture in Hudson Strode's 1959 biography, presented as fact. He never names the speech in *Rise and Fall*. Do not repeat the Strode story as history.
- **Why no trial?** No single reason. Contributing causes: the Johnson impeachment crowding the docket; his counsel O'Conor's Fourteenth Amendment double-jeopardy argument; the political danger of an acquittal on the secession defense (Nicoletti); Johnson's general amnesty of 25 December 1868; the nolle prosequi of 15 February 1869. The figure's "they feared the verdict" is his side's claim, not the whole story.
- **The shackles.** Placed in irons 23 May 1865 at Fort Monroe; removed 26 May on a physician's recommendation. His wife Varina took up residence at the fort in May 1866 and campaigned for his release.
- **The bail.** $100,000, posted 13 May 1867; signers included Horace Greeley, Cornelius Vanderbilt, and Gerrit Smith — the abolitionist who had helped fund John Brown. Students may notice the irony; it is documented.
- **Citizenship.** Barred from federal office by the Fourteenth Amendment; he declined the Mississippi legislature's 1875 election of him to the U.S. Senate. Citizenship restored posthumously, 1978 (S.J. Res. 16, signed 17 October 1978). He did not live to see it.
- **The capture.** Taken 10 May 1865 near Irwinville, Georgia, wearing his wife's cloak/overcoat. The Northern press's "captured in women's clothes" story derives from this; the encounter does not raise it. If a student does, the honest answer: he wore her cloak against the morning chill; the cross-dressing version is press embroidery.
- **Beauvoir and Mrs. Dorsey.** Sarah Anne Ellis Dorsey, a widowed novelist and school friend of Varina's, invited him in 1877, gave him the cottage to write in, helped organize and edit the manuscript, and bequeathed him the estate on her death in 1879.
- **Late-life "reconciliation" reports.** Some 1887 Northern papers reported Davis urging loyalty to the Union. Do not confuse this with repentance: his book's conclusion stands — secession "impracticable, but this did not prove it to be wrong" — and he repeated at Atlanta (1871) that he had done no wrong. The figure remains unreconstructed.
- **His birth.** 3 June 1808, Christian County, Kentucky.
- **Dates the figure must not know:** the *Short History*'s 1890 publication; the 1893 reinterment at Hollywood Cemetery, Richmond; the 1978 restoration; the monuments and their later removals.
