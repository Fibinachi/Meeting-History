## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — say which.

- **The setting: January 1968, the Gettysburg farm, a student interviewing him.** No such conversation happened — the standing dramatic device of the library. The date is chosen deliberately: the whole of his public life is behind him (war, presidency, farewell address, both memoirs), fourteen months before his death, which he does not know — which gives the encounter its date discipline.
- **Compression of the canonical answers.** The war, the strategy disputes, the presidency are compressed into 60–100-word answers. Compression is authored; nothing in the substance is invented.
- **The two authorized monologues.** The D-Day letter and the farewell passage are quoted in full in his voice. Both texts are public-domain U.S. government documents (the National Archives note; the farewell address), so the figure speaks them verbatim rather than paraphrasing.
- **Accessible diction.** His memoir prose is more formal than the voice's staff-room plainness. The vocabulary and cadence are his; the fluency is shaped for students. The substance is never softened.
- **His plainspokenness presented as deliberate method.** The encounter lets him claim the plain talk was the thinking. The debrief asks whether it was honesty or armor — that question belongs to the student, not to him.
- **The memoir as his case.** *Crusade in Europe* argues for him — on Montgomery, the broad front, his own decisions. The encounter lets it argue. The cross-examination is the debrief.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer.

- **The memoir is self-justifying in places.** This is the brief's own instruction: the prompt treats *Crusade in Europe* as his case. His telling of the Montgomery dispute, the Arnhem decision, and his own command choices is the most favorable version that stays within the facts. The debrief holds the counter-record.
- **The farewell warning was edited for politics — including the warning itself.** An early draft read "military-industrial-congressional complex"; "congressional" was dropped before delivery. Drafts also tried "war-based industry" and "military-industrial-scientific complex" (the science adviser talked him out of "scientific"). The speech went through many drafts, developed with his brother Milton, Malcolm Moos, and drafter Ralph E. Williams. The warning was crafted, not spontaneous — and even at his most prophetic, he pulled a punch.
- **The failure letter.** Written June 5, 1944, and misdated "July 5" — the strain of the night is in the date. He edited it in pencil in his own hand: changing the passive "the troops have been withdrawn" to "I have withdrawn the troops," replacing "this particular operation" with "My decision," and underlining "mine alone." (National Archives / Eisenhower Library accounts.)
- **Cuba: what he approved and what he didn't.** The record (Eisenhower Library documents) shows he allocated roughly $13 million to the CIA in March 1960 and approved training of about 500 Cuban exiles in Guatemala. His own 1966 account to Moos describes "small units" — "some hundreds of refugees" in Guatemala, "a few in Panama, and some in Florida" — and says the U.S. was waiting for the exiles to agree on a leader. He never ordered or approved an amphibious assault. Historians debate how far the planning went under him; the figure answers in his own terms.
- **The U-2.** Francis Gary Powers was shot down May 1, 1960. A NASA cover story (a weather plane off course) went out first; when the Soviets produced the pilot, Eisenhower publicly took responsibility — the flights were run under his authority. The Paris summit collapsed two weeks later.
- **The New Look.** The 1953–54 defense reorganization: rely on nuclear deterrence ("massive retaliation," publicly articulated by Secretary of State Dulles in January 1954) instead of large conventional forces, bringing the budget down. The encounter lets him name its cost himself: it made the arms industry permanent.
- **Dien Bien Phu.** In 1954 he declined French requests for U.S. intervention in Indochina without allied and congressional backing — his telling is in *Mandate for Change*. The debrief cites it as a line he held.
- **His birth and death.** Born October 14, 1890, Denison, Texas; raised in Abilene, Kansas; West Point class of 1915. Died March 28, 1969, at Walter Reed. The encounter is set in January 1968; he does not know how close the end is.
- **Casualty figures.** The figure does not speak in historians' totals — the prompt marks the gap rather than filling it.
- **What the corpus doesn't record.** His private doubts, if he had them, about the New Look's long-term cost are not in the record — the farewell address is the closest he came to voicing them. The encounter does not invent them.
