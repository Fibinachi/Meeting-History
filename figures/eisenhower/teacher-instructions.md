# Meet Dwight D. Eisenhower — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Dwight D. Eisenhower (1890–1969), run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student renders the closing judgment: he built the machine, then warned us about it. Judge him.

Eisenhower is the general who ran the largest war machine ever assembled and the president who left office warning America about the military-industrial complex. The voice is plain, organized, warm — the chairman who ran the war like a meeting and the meeting like a war. He defends every decision in his own plain terms. He never judges himself. The student does.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

Students will encounter: war and combat death (D-Day, the Normandy landings, discussed plainly and without gratuitous detail); Cold War covert operations (the CIA overthrows in Iran and Guatemala, the U-2 program, the training of Cuban exiles — discussed as policy, in the debrief and, in his own terms, in the encounter); nuclear weapons as a defense policy (the New Look — discussed as strategy, never technically). Tier: OPEN. Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Eisenhower page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: 25–30 minutes for the encounter, 20–25 minutes for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the opening carefully. Note the two moments the figure is allowed to speak at length: the D-Day letter and the farewell warning, both quoted verbatim from the historical documents.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "Why did you warn us about the military-industrial complex?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. The encounter runs in three beats: early (the war and the command), middle (the strategy disputes and the warning), late (the presidency's contradictions). When the student is ready, the figure asks the closing question and stops. The debrief follows, in your voice or the system's — never in his.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Dwight Eisenhower — the general who ran D-Day and the president who warned America about the military-industrial complex. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. He built the machine, then he warned us about it. At the end, you judge him. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The entry hook: read the farewell address first

The farewell address is short enough to read whole — do it in class before the encounter, or assign it the night before (the full text is linked in sources.md). When students hear him quote the passage from memory, they will recognize it, and the recognition is the encounter's engine: the man saying these words is the man who built what the words describe. The warning-vs-record frame only works if they have the warning in their ears first.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Eisenhower answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief — with two exceptions: the D-Day letter and the farewell passage, which he quotes in full.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- Three times in the whole encounter, a bracketed line points at something in the answer just given — a "did you notice" nudge. These are the assignment talking. Make sure students know to read them.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Pacing

- Opening and early beat (the war, D-Day, the command): 6–8 exchanges. Don't let students camp here — the war is the setup, not the subject.
- Middle beat (the broad front, Montgomery, Patton, the warning): 6–8 exchanges. This is where the nudges fire. Slow down here.
- Late beat (the U-2, Cuba, the New Look, the plainspokenness): 5–7 exchanges. Then the closing question. Don't let the encounter drift past 25 exchanges — the debrief is where the judgment happens.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer: hold the builder and the warner in the same mind at the same time. A man took absolute responsibility for the largest invasion in history, built a permanent war machine as a system, and warned against it as a citizen. He never once judged himself. You do.

## The debrief — do not skip this

The debrief has six fixed sections, in order: (1) his name, (2) what the history records, (3) what came after, (4) the plain record, (5) the mechanism — what the voice was doing, (6) the question that remains. Run it as a discussion, not a read-aloud. Discussion openings:

- He said the New Look made the arms industry permanent — and he ordered the New Look. Can both halves be true of the same decision?
- He took all the blame for D-Day in advance, in writing. Does that change how you judge the decisions where he took none — the U-2 cover story, the Cuba training?
- An early draft of the farewell address said "military-industrial-congressional complex." He cut "congressional." What does that cut tell you about the warning?
- His plain talk: is it honesty, or is it armor? Point at a specific answer and argue it.
- The closing question, restated: he built the machine, then warned us about it. Judge him. Where did you land — and what moved you there?

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **The figure quotes the farewell passage at the wrong moment.** The passage is scripted for the warning question. If the model drops it early, steer the student to the middle-beat topics and let it land where it belongs.
- **The figure moralizes in modern terms.** This is a voice failure. The prompt forbids it explicitly; if it happens, note it, and treat the answer as unreliable — the debrief corrects it.
- No model-specific quirks recorded yet. Log what you find here.

## Historical notes for the teacher

The honest answers to "did it really happen like that?" — one bullet per caveat from sources.md.

- **The memoir argues for him.** *Crusade in Europe* is his case — the broad front, Montgomery, his own command choices, all in the most favorable version that stays within the facts. The encounter lets him argue it. The cross-examination is the debrief.
- **Even the warning was edited for politics.** The "military-industrial-congressional complex" draft, the dropped "scientific," the 21+ drafts with his brother Milton, Malcolm Moos, and Ralph Williams — the most prophetic paragraph of his life was workshopped, and he pulled a punch in it.
- **The D-Day letter is real and verbatim.** Written June 5, 1944, misdated "July 5," edited in pencil in his own hand — the passive voice crossed out, "mine alone" underlined. The National Archives holds it.
- **Cuba: he started it, Kennedy finished it.** Roughly $13 million and ~500 exiles trained in Guatemala approved March 1960; he never approved an amphibious assault. The Bay of Pigs decision was Kennedy's.
- **The U-2.** Powers was shot down May 1, 1960; the cover story collapsed; Eisenhower took public responsibility. The Paris summit died two weeks later.
- **What he wouldn't do.** No U.S. intervention at Dien Bien Phu (1954), no preventive war against the Soviets, eight years without a new American war. The restraint is as much the record as the buildup.

## One last thing

The single skill that matters most for this figure: teach them to hear the system. Eisenhower turns everything into a staff problem — situation, plan, execution — and the plainness is what makes it hard to see. The bracketed nudges point at the moments when people become numbers. Make sure students know the nudges are the assignment talking. The facts of his record are in every textbook. The way he organized the world — and what the organizing did to everyone inside it — is only in the voice.
