# Meet Eisenhower — Standards Alignment (verified 2026-10-15)

Encounter: the student interviews Dwight D. Eisenhower at the Gettysburg farm in January 1968 — seven years out of the presidency, the whole record behind him — pressing him on D-Day, the broad front, the New Look, the U-2, Cuba, Iran and Guatemala, and the farewell warning about the war machine he helped build. It closes with the judgment question: *he built the machine, then warned us about it. Judge him.*

**Grade band fit for every framework below: 9–12** (high school US History / ELA literacy).

---

## 1. C3 Framework (National Council for the Social Studies, 2013)

Grade band: **9–12**. (Indicator texts verified against curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim; the official NCSS PDF was not directly retrievable in this session — see verification note at end.)

### Dimension 2 — History

- **D2.His.3.9-12** — Use questions generated about individuals and groups to assess how the significance of their actions changes over time and is shaped by the historical context. (The builder-versus-warner judgment: he knows the farewell warning in 1968 but nothing of how it "became his most-remembered act" — the debrief asks the student to assess how the warning's significance changed after his death.)
- **D2.His.5.9-12** — Analyze how historical contexts shaped and continue to shape people's perspectives. (The WWII coalition commander who kept the broad front because "it fit the logistics, and it kept the alliance together" becomes the Cold War president who chose covert action because "covert, quiet, and over quickly beats a war" — the same staff-system mind in two different historical contexts.)
- **D2.His.14.9-12** — Analyze multiple and complex causes and effects of events in the past. (The New Look: budget down, no new American war — and the cost he names himself, "it made the arms industry permanent," the exact industry the farewell address warns about. Cause, intended effect, and the unintended effect that became the warning.)
- **D2.His.16.9-12** — Integrate evidence from multiple relevant historical sources and interpretations into a reasoned argument about the past. (The closing judgment requires integrating *Crusade in Europe* (his case), the farewell address, the "In Case of Failure" note, the diaries and wartime letters, and the presidential record into one verdict on the builder and the warner.)

### Dimension 4 — Communicating Conclusions and Taking Informed Action

- **D4.1.9-12** — Construct arguments using precise and knowledgeable claims, with evidence from multiple sources, while acknowledging counterclaims and evidentiary weaknesses. (The closing question — judge him — is a designed argument prompt with the counterclaims built in: he would not send Americans to die at Dien Bien Phu, he left office after eight years without a new American war — held against Iran, Guatemala, the U-2, and the Cuba training.)
- **D4.4.9-12** — Critique the use of claims and evidence in arguments for credibility. (The debrief is the cross-examination: *Crusade in Europe* is allowed to argue his case in the encounter — the broad front, Montgomery, his own decisions — and the student critiques its credibility, including the farewell drafts that show the warning itself was edited for politics, "congressional" dropped.)

*Verified against: multiple independent curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim (agreement among them). Official document: College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework for Social Studies State Standards, NCSS, 2013 (socialstudies.org/c3).*

## 2. Common Core ELA — Literacy in History/Social Studies & Speaking/Listening

Grade band: **9–12**. (Verified against the official Common Core State Standards Initiative site, thecorestandards.org.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 9–10

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.2** — Determine the central ideas or information of a primary or secondary source; provide an accurate summary of how key events or ideas develop over the course of the text. (The farewell passage — "we must guard against the acquisition of unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought, by the military-industrial complex" — quoted in full in the encounter; the student traces how the warning develops through the speech's acknowledgment of "the imperative need for this development.")
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.8** — Assess the extent to which the reasoning and evidence in a text support the author's claims. (His Iran and Guatemala answer — "covert action where it could work, so we didn't have to fight openly where we couldn't afford to... I'd make it again" — the student assesses the reasoning against the record the debrief supplies.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.2** — Determine the central ideas or information of a primary or secondary source; provide an accurate summary that makes clear the relationships among the key details and ideas. (The "In Case of Failure" note — the pencil edits from "the troops have been withdrawn" to "I have withdrawn the troops" — and how that revision relates to his claim that plain talk was the thinking.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.6** — Evaluate authors' differing points of view on the same historical event or issue by assessing the authors' claims, reasoning, and evidence. (*Crusade in Europe*'s case for the broad front and for his own command decisions, evaluated against the declassified record on Cuba, the CIA programs, and the historians' picture of the "active, deliberate operator.")
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.8** — Evaluate an author's premises, claims, and evidence by corroborating or challenging them with other information. (His Cuba answer — "I never ordered or approved an amphibious assault" — challenged or corroborated by the Eisenhower Library documents showing the ~$13 million CIA allocation and ~500 exiles trained in Guatemala, plus his own 1966 oral-history account.)

### Speaking & Listening, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.1** — Initiate and participate effectively in a range of collaborative discussions (one-on-one, in groups, and teacher-led) with diverse partners on grades 11–12 topics, texts, and issues, building on others' ideas and expressing their own clearly and persuasively. (The encounter *is* a sustained one-on-one discussion; the tutorial instructs the student to "question him until you understand what he believed and why he did what he did.")
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.3** — Evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric, assessing the stance, premises, links among ideas, word choice, points of emphasis, and tone used. (His plainness is the evidence: "if you can't say a thing plainly, you don't understand it" — the debrief asks whether the plain talk was honesty or armor, and the student evaluates word choice and stance across the D-Day letter, the broad-front defense, and the farewell warning.)

### Writing, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.W.11-12.1** — Write arguments to support claims in an analysis of substantive topics or texts, using valid reasoning and relevant and sufficient evidence. (The closing question — he built the machine, then warned us about it — is designed as a post-encounter argument prompt, per the teacher instructions.)

## 3. South Carolina — Social Studies College- and Career-Ready Standards (2019), US History and the Constitution

Grade band: **9–12** (high school US History, Grade 11). (Verified against the official SC Department of Education 2019 standards document, ed.sc.gov, opened and read live 2026-10-15.)

- **USHC.4.E** (Standard 4: Modernism and Interventionism) — "Utilize primary and secondary sources to analyze the impact of changes in American foreign policy, worldwide conflicts, and business cycles in capitalism." (The D-Day command and the broad-front strategy — the largest invasion in history, run through the "In Case of Failure" note, *Crusade in Europe*, and the wartime letters as primary sources.)
- **USHC.5.CO** (Standard 5: Legacy of the Cold War, 1945–present) — "Explain the technological developments and economic changes in the U.S. during the Cold War and post-Cold War eras using a comparative analysis." (The New Look: nuclear deterrence instead of large conventional forces, the budget brought down — and the "permanent armaments industry" it left behind, per his own farewell passage.)
- **USHC.5.CE** (Standard 5) — "Assess the immediate and long-term causes and effects through significant turning points of the Cold War." (The U-2 shootdown and the collapsed Paris summit, the 1960 CIA training of Cuban exiles, Iran (1953) and Guatemala (1954) — the encounter's Cold War beats are the indicator's turning points.)
- **USHC.5.E** (Standard 5) — "Utilize primary and secondary sources to judge the impact of evolving American foreign policy on American identity and capitalism." (The farewell address itself: "the very structure of our society" — the student judges what the permanent military establishment did to American identity and capitalism, using his own warning as the primary source.)
