# Sources — Plato

The corpus this figure's voice is bounded by. The voice may claim only what these texts support. Plato never speaks in his own voice anywhere in the dialogues — "Plato" the figure is therefore constructed from his writings, and the prompt is transparent about that in its internal logic: he answers as the author of the dialogues, expounding the positions the dialogues defend, and admits the dialogues are his art rather than transcripts.

## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — say which.

- **"Plato" as a speaking figure.** Plato never speaks in his own voice in the dialogues (he appears only twice, named in passing: *Apology* 34a/38b, *Phaedo* 59b). A first-person Plato is therefore a construction, not a quotation. The prompt handles this honestly: the figure presents himself as the *author* of the dialogues, expounding the positions they defend, and volunteers that the dialogues are his art, not transcripts. Pure dramatic device, disclosed in-voice.
- **The setting (Academy, c. 360 BC).** No source records a conversation like this one. The date is chosen so that the Republic is written, all three Sicilian voyages are past, and Aristotle is a student at the Academy — all documented facts arranged into one plausible evening. Historically plausible, not documented.
- **The dialogues-are-not-transcripts admission.** No dialogue claims to be verbatim reportage, and the scholarly consensus treats them as literary drama — but Plato himself never says so in the corpus. Having the figure say it is an honest interpretive device: it states the consensus position in his voice rather than inventing a false claim of transcript accuracy. Interpretive, disclosed here.
- **Modesty about the afterlife arguments.** The *Phaedo* presents the immortality arguments with full Socratic confidence. The encounter's Plato distinguishes his certainty about the forms from the reach of the soul arguments — a softening authored for pedagogy, to model intellectual honesty and to keep the closing question live. Authored; the corpus is more confident than the figure.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer.

- **Are the Letters really by Plato?** Disputed. All thirteen were generally accepted as genuine until the 19th century; modern philology rejects most of them outright. The Seventh is the most defended — much contemporary opinion favors authenticity — but prominent scholars (Malcolm Schofield, Myles Burnyeat, Julia Annas, Terence Irwin, George Boas) argue it is spurious. The encounter uses it only for Syracuse, and the figure names the dispute aloud every time. If a student asks, the honest answer is: "scholars disagree, and your teacher should tell you so."
- **Did Plato really get sold into slavery in Syracuse?** The story comes from Diogenes Laertius (3rd c. AD), citing earlier sources, and is *not* in the Seventh Letter — the two accounts differ in details. Treat it as tradition, not settled fact. The encounter's wording ("a friend ransomed me") follows Diogenes Laertius.
- **Did Socrates really say these things?** Unknown and unknowable. No transcript exists. The dialogues are Plato's literary-philosophical works with Socrates as chief speaker; Xenophon and Aristophanes give different, partial pictures. The encounter's position — the words are Plato's art, the man was real — is the mainstream scholarly consensus.
- **Dates are approximate.** Birth 428/427 BC, death 348/347 BC, Academy c. 387 BC, Sicilian voyages c. 388 / 367 / 361–360 BC. The prompt marks them with "c." and "around" throughout.
- **The Academy's closure (529 AD).** Traditionally dated to the emperor Justinian's decree of 529 AD; some scholars argue for a later or more gradual end. The debrief says "traditionally dated to."
- **Whitehead's "footnotes to Plato."** The line is from *Process and Reality* (1929): "The safest general characterization of the European philosophical tradition is that it consists of a series of footnotes to Plato." Quoted in paraphrase in the debrief.
