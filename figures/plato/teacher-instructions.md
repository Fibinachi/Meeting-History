# Meet Plato — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Plato (c. 428–c. 347 BC), run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student answers the closing question: **is there a Justice itself — beyond what any city happens to call just? Or is there only the city's word, and nothing beneath it?**

This is the **second** encounter in the library's opening sequence, designed to follow *Meet Socrates*. The one-two punch is the pedagogy: Socrates interrogated the student; Plato answers the student. Where Socrates asked what justice is, Plato claims to know — and the student must decide whether his certainty is warranted. The figure speaks as the *author* of the dialogues, openly admitting the words are his art rather than transcripts, and expounds the positions the dialogues defend: the forms, the cave, the tripartite soul, the philosopher-kings, and the failed attempt to put philosophy into power at Syracuse.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

- **Anti-democratic argument, stated with total confidence.** Plato argues that most people are unfit to rule and that philosophers should be kings. He means it. Students who take democracy as a given will find this confronting — that confrontation is part of the encounter's design, and the debrief is where the modern frame belongs.
- **Slavery, unexamined.** Plato takes slavery for granted and never questions it. A slave boy appears in the *Meno* as a prop in a geometry argument. The figure will be genuinely puzzled if pressed on it — the silence is load-bearing and is named in the debrief, never softened in the encounter.
- **Ancient Greek norms around love.** If a student asks about love, the figure answers from the *Symposium*'s ladder, which begins with the love of beautiful bodies — in Athens, that meant beautiful young men. He states it as Athenian custom, plainly and without prurience. Be aware this may come up; the debrief does not dwell on it, but you should be ready if students raise it.
- No graphic violence, no slurs, no sexual explicitness. The disturbing material here is intellectual, not visceral.

Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Plato page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: ~30 minutes for the encounter, ~20 minutes for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read Plato's opening carefully. Note what he promises: you ask, he answers, and at the end he asks you one question.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "What are the forms?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. Then try the hard ones: "Did Socrates really say all that?" and "What happened in Syracuse?" Watch how he handles the authorship admission and the letter caveat — that carefulness is the encounter modeling honest sourcing in real time.
6. End by typing something like "I think I'm done" and watch the closing question land.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Plato — the philosopher who wrote the dialogues, founded the Academy, and claimed that justice is real whether anyone believes in it or not. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. Last time, Socrates questioned you. This time, you get to do the questioning — and he is certain he has the answers. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Plato answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.
- Three times in the encounter, a bracketed nudge may point the student at something telling in the answer they just read — the dialogues admission, the two justices, the careful Syracuse story. These fire once each, at most.

### Pacing

- **Opening:** 2–3 exchanges. Don't let students camp here — push them toward the forms.
- **The forms, the cave, the soul:** 8–12 exchanges. This is the heart of it.
- **Syracuse and the dialogues-as-art:** 4–6 exchanges. The encounter turns personal here; let it.
- **The closing:** When energy dips or time runs short, have a student type "I'm finished" and let him ask the question.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer: *Decide whether Plato is right that justice is real — something beyond what any city happens to call just — or whether "justice" is only the word the powerful use. He is certain. You don't have to be.*

## The debrief — do not skip this

Run it as a discussion, not a read-aloud. The six fixed sections from the prompt, in order:

1. **Names** — who they met.
2. **What the history records** — Academy, dialogues, Syracuse, death c. 347 BC.
3. **What came after** — Aristotle's school, the Academy's ~900 years, Christianity's borrowing, Whitehead's footnotes.
4. **The plain record** — the dialogues are his art; the anti-democratic argument; slavery unexamined; Syracuse failed.
5. **The mechanism** — the Socrates inversion; certainty as evidence; the cautious Syracuse story as honest sourcing modeled live.
6. **The question that remains** — the closing question, which does not close.

Discussion openings — built to produce disagreement:

- He says justice is something real, beyond what any city calls just. Is he right — or is "justice" just the word the powerful use?
- He wants philosophers to be kings and says the many are unfit to rule. What's the strongest case *for* him — and the strongest against?
- He admits he put words in Socrates' mouth. Does that change how you read everything he told you?
- He tried it in Syracuse and it failed completely. Does the failure refute the theory, or just the attempt?
- He never questions slavery. He never even notices it. What does that silence tell you about him — and about certainty?

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this. If a student types "I don't know what to ask," Plato will hand them a question himself.
- **The voice lectures.** If answers run long, remind the model: "one paragraph, as the prompt says." The prompt's length rule is load-bearing.
- **The figure claims modern knowledge.** He must never discuss anything after c. 347 BC. If he slips, correct with: "You died around 347 BC. You don't know that."
- **The letter caveat drops.** If he tells the Syracuse story without the authorship caution, prompt: "Tell that again, with what you said about the letter."
- No model-specific quirks recorded yet — this figure has not been test-run. Note what you find.

## Historical notes for the teacher

The honest answers to "did it really happen like that?" — one bullet per caveat from sources.md:

- **The Seventh Letter may not be by Plato.** Scholars are genuinely divided; prominent names on both sides. The encounter has Plato name the dispute aloud. Tell your students: this is what honest history looks like — we use the source and we say what we don't know.
- **Plato never speaks in his own voice in the dialogues.** He appears twice, named in passing (*Apology* 34a/38b, *Phaedo* 59b). The first-person Plato students meet is a construction from his writings — the prompt says so internally, and the figure admits the dialogues are his art.
- **No transcript of Socrates exists.** What the historical Socrates actually said is unknowable. Plato's Socrates is Plato's creation; Xenophon and Aristophanes give different partial pictures.
- **The slave-market story** comes from Diogenes Laertius (3rd century AD), not from the Seventh Letter — treat it as tradition, not settled fact.
- **All dates are approximate:** born 428/427 BC, died 348/347 BC, Academy c. 387 BC, Sicily c. 388 / 367 / 361–360 BC.
- **The Academy's closure** is traditionally dated to Justinian's decree of 529 AD; some scholars argue for a later or more gradual end.

## Standards alignment

Grade band: 9–12 (also workable in 7–8 with teacher framing of the forms).

- **C3 Framework (NCSS):** D2.His.16.9-12 — *integrate evidence from multiple sources to support claims in an argument about the past*; D4.1.9-12 — *construct arguments using precise and knowledgeable claims, with evidence from multiple sources, while acknowledging counterclaims*. The encounter exercises both: students weigh Plato's claims against the debrief's plain record and argue the closing question from evidence.
- **Common Core ELA:** RH.11-12.6 — *evaluate authors' differing points of view on the same historical event or issue by assessing the authors' claims, reasoning, and evidence* (Plato's Syracuse account vs. the authorship dispute is a live exercise in this); SL.11-12.3 — *evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric*.

## One last thing

His answers arrive certain. Teach your students to ask the question he can't answer — not to trip him, but to find the edge of his certainty. The most important moment in this encounter isn't when he explains the forms. It's when a student asks him something he never thought to ask himself, and the whole room hears the silence.
