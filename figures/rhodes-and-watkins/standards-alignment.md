# Meet Rhodes and Watkins — Standards Alignment (verified 2026-10-15)

Encounter: a two-voice dialogue. The student questions two Civil War soldiers — Union Colonel Elisha Hunt Rhodes and Confederate private Sam Watkins — and the two men also answer *each other*: paired answers, a scripted response round, and a challenge rule that stops Watkins from drifting into Lost Cause abstraction. The encounter closes on the judgment: *Two ordinary men, opposite causes, both did their duty. Judge them.*

**Grade band fit for every framework below: 9–12** (high school US History / ELA literacy).

---

## 1. C3 Framework (National Council for the Social Studies, 2013)

Grade band: **9–12**. (Indicator texts verified against curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim — Eastern Illinois University "C3 at a Glance" compilation and Teacher's Discovery C3 correlation; the official NCSS PDF was not directly retrievable — see verification note.)

### Dimension 2 — History

- **D2.His.1.9-12** — Evaluate how historical events and developments were shaped by unique circumstances of time and place as well as broader historical contexts. (Two soldiers, two causes, one war — the tutorial states the war's cause as settled record before the conversation begins.)
- **D2.His.4.9-12** — Analyze complex and interacting factors that influenced the perspectives of people during different historical eras. (Slavery as the war's cause vs. what each man believed he was fighting for.)
- **D2.His.6.9-12** — Analyze the ways in which the perspectives of those writing history shaped the history that they produced. (Rhodes's terse diary vs. Watkins's elegiac memoir — the two corpora the encounter is built from.)
- **D2.His.8.9-12** — Analyze how current interpretations of the past are limited by the extent to which available historical sources represent perspectives of people at the time. (Rhodes's marked silence on emancipation; the encounter points at it rather than filling it.)
- **D2.His.11.9-12** — Critique the usefulness of historical sources for a specific historical inquiry based on their maker, date, place of origin, intended audience, and purpose. (The Lost Cause guardrail: Watkins wrote twenty years after the war, for his children; the challenge rule exists to keep the elegy from becoming evidence.)
- **D2.His.14.9-12** — Analyze multiple and complex causes and effects of events in the past. (The war's cause as settled scholarship vs. each man's stated reason for enlisting.)
- **D2.His.16.9-12** — Integrate evidence from multiple relevant historical sources and interpretations into a reasoned argument about the past. (The closing judgment.)

### Dimension 2 — Civics

- **D2.Civ.9.9-12** — Use appropriate deliberative processes in multiple settings. (The dialogue form itself: the student moderates two witnesses who answer each other.)

### Dimension 4 — Communicating Conclusions and Taking Informed Action

- **D4.1.9-12** — Construct arguments using precise and knowledgeable claims, with evidence from multiple sources, while acknowledging counterclaims and evidentiary weaknesses. (The verdict, in plain words, must name the cause in the sentence — per the teacher instructions.)
- **D4.4.9-12** — Critique the use of claims and evidence in arguments for credibility. (Testing Watkins's cause-statements against his own book.)

*D4 indicator texts verified 2026-10-15 against Greenwich Public Schools curriculum documents, the Kentucky Academic Standards social studies module, and the Teacher's Discovery C3 correlation (same verification pass as the Trial of John Brown alignment). Official document: College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework for Social Studies State Standards, NCSS, 2013 (socialstudies.org/c3).*

## 2. Common Core ELA — Literacy in History/Social Studies & Speaking/Listening

Grade band: **9–12**. (Verified against the official Common Core State Standards Initiative site, thecorestandards.org, 2026-10-15, in the Trial of John Brown verification pass.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.6** — Compare the point of view of two or more authors for how they treat the same or similar topics, including which details they include and emphasize in their respective accounts. (The paired answers on enlistment, killing, desertion, the war's end.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.2** — Determine the central ideas or information of a primary or secondary source; provide an accurate summary that makes clear the relationships among the key details and ideas. (The teacher instructions name this anchor; the canonical answers are paraphrase-close to the two corpora.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.6** — Evaluate authors' differing points of view on the same historical event or issue by assessing the authors' claims, reasoning, and evidence. (Two memoirs, one war; the challenge exchange is a worked example.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.8** — Evaluate an author's premises, claims, and evidence by corroborating or challenging them with other information. (Rhodes holding Watkins to his own book: "Your own book names it.")

### Speaking & Listening

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.1** — Initiate and participate effectively in a range of collaborative discussions with diverse partners, building on others' ideas and expressing their own clearly and persuasively. (The dialogue form: the student moderates; the response round is collaborative argument between two voices.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.3** — Evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric, assessing the stance, premises, links among ideas, word choice, points of emphasis, and tone used. (The core skill the encounter trains: noticing Watkins's elegy as rhetoric, noticing Rhodes's silence.)

## Verification caveats

1. **C3 Framework**: indicator texts verified against independent curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim (they agree); the official NCSS PDF was not directly retrievable this session. Official publisher: NCSS (2013), socialstudies.org/c3.
2. **Code correction vs. teacher instructions**: the teacher-instructions condensed alignment for this figure attributes the "critique the usefulness of historical sources for a specific inquiry (maker, date, audience, purpose)" text to **D2.His.13.9-12**. That text is actually **D2.His.11.9-12**; D2.His.13.9-12 reads "Critique the appropriateness of the historical sources used in a secondary interpretation." This document uses the correct code. The condensed line in teacher-instructions.md should be corrected.
3. **Texas**: high-school US History TEKS begin at 1877 — no adopted high-school anchor for the Civil War era; the anchor is Grade 8. A 2026 SBOE TEKS revision is in process but not adopted.
4. **C3 indicators are grade-band endpoints** ("by the end of Grade 12"), not grade-specific standards.
