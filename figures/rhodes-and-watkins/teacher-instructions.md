# Meet Rhodes and Watkins — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with two soldiers of the American Civil War — Elisha Hunt Rhodes (1842–1917), Union, 2nd Rhode Island Volunteers, and Samuel Rush Watkins (1839–1901), Confederate, 1st Tennessee Infantry — run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with them — by typing questions — and the two men also talk with *each other*: answering the same questions side by side, correcting and challenging each other where their own words honestly allow it.

At the end each student renders the closing judgment, in plain words: *Two ordinary men, opposite causes, both did their duty. Judge them.*

This is the library's first two-voice encounter. The form is the pedagogy: the student cannot hear Watkins's elegy without hearing Rhodes's answer to it, and cannot hear Rhodes's certainty without noticing what he never says.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, one or both men answer. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. Neither figure will break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

Students will encounter: graphic descriptions of Civil War battle — wounds, corpses, killing, in plain period language (Watkins is unsparing; this is the stronger of the two voices on violence). Death on a large scale, including the execution of deserters. Slavery as the war's cause, stated plainly and repeatedly. Period racial language: Watkins's memoir contains racial slurs; the encounter is built under the library's absolute blacklist — no slur is ever reproduced, and where the memoir uses one the figure paraphrases around it. The debrief (modern voice) names what was elided so the classroom does not mistake the omission for the history. Grief, loss, and the wreckage of defeat.

Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Rhodes-and-Watkins page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: ~30 minutes for the encounter, ~20 minutes for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the tutorial and the paired opening carefully — Rhodes first, then Watkins. Note the contrast they establish in under 200 words.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try a paired one first, e.g.: `Both of you: why did you enlist?` Watch what happens: each man answers in one short paragraph, then each gets one short reply to the other's answer — and then, outside both voices, in brackets and italics, the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. Then try addressing one man — `Watkins, tell me about Shiloh` — and then a topic with no name — `Tell me about desertion` — and watch both answer. Then try the drift test: ask Watkins what "states' rights" meant, and watch Rhodes answer him.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with two soldiers from the Civil War — one who fought for the Union, one who fought for the Confederacy. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, they answer. You can talk to one of them, or both at once, and they will argue with each other. At the end, you judge them. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, they answer." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them. The second-most important sentence is "you can talk to one of them, or both at once."

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Each man answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- Address one man by name (`Rhodes, …` / `Watkins, …`), both (`Both of you: …`), or just name a topic (`Tell me about desertion`) and hear from both.
- On paired questions the men answer in turn, then each gets one short reply to the other's answer. Then they stop and wait. The student always drives.
- After every student turn, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.
- To close: the student says they're ready, or asks for the closing. Watkins and Rhodes close it together, and Rhodes asks the question.

### Pacing

Two voices means twice the reading. Budget for fewer, better questions — this is a feature, not a bug.

- Opening and first paired question: ~8 minutes. Don't let students camp on enlistment.
- Middle (killing, desertion, the cause, a battle): ~12 minutes. This is where the challenge rule usually fires — let it.
- Late (the end, emancipation, the dead): ~8 minutes.
- Closing question + transition to debrief: ~2 minutes.
- Debrief discussion: ~20 minutes. Do not compress this.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer: *By the end, you have to judge two men who both did their duty for opposite causes — and you have to do it knowing exactly what each cause was.*

## The debrief — do not skip this

The six fixed sections, in order: their names; what the history records; what came after; the plain record; the mechanism; the question that remains. The full text is in the encounter prompt. Run it as a discussion, not a read-aloud.

Discussion openings — built to produce disagreement:

- Watkins owned no slaves and says he fought for his home. Does that change what he fought *for*? (Press: the home he fought for rested on slavery. His own book says the war was about slavery.)
- Rhodes never talks about slavery — only "the Union." Is his silence a kind of honesty, or a kind of evasion?
- Both men killed. Both did their duty. Is "I did my duty" a defense? For whom, and of what?
- Watkins is the more likeable voice — funnier, warmer, sadder. Did you notice yourself liking him more? What did that liking tempt you to excuse?
- The closing question, restated: two ordinary men, opposite causes, both did their duty. Judge them. (Take answers in full sentences. Make them name the cause in the sentence.)

### The Lost Cause guardrail — the firmer hand

This is the encounter's known failure mode, and you are its backstop. Watkins's elegiac tone is seductive: grief, humor, and humanity can make a student feel that the two causes were morally equivalent — two brave boys, two honest duties. They were not. Slavery was the cause of the war; the Confederacy existed to preserve it; Watkins's own book says so.

The encounter is built with three defenses, in depth:

1. **The prompt's framing** states slavery as the war's cause plainly, before the conversation starts.
2. **The challenge rule**: if Watkins drifts toward Lost Cause abstraction, Rhodes answers him from Watkins's own book. Watch for it in your solo run — it is scripted and it works.
3. **The debrief's plain record** states it again, in the modern voice, without euphemism.

If a student leaves the encounter saying "both sides were equally right" or "it wasn't really about slavery," the encounter did not fail — the debrief hasn't happened yet. That sentence is your opening. Put Watkins's own cause statement next to it and ask the student to reconcile them. Do not let the elegy launder the cause; do not let the debrief become a second elegy. The question that remains is about judging the *men*, and it only has weight if the *cause* is named first.

## If the model misbehaves

- **One voice dominates.** If the model lets Watkins (the more quotable voice) swallow Rhodes, address Rhodes by name: `Rhodes, what do you say to that?` The prompt alternates the lead on paired answers, but a nudge doesn't hurt.
- **The response round runs long.** If the men start debating past their one-reply each, type: `Thank you both — my turn.` and ask your next question.
- **Lost Cause drift that Rhodes doesn't catch.** Rare, but possible on long runs. Quote the challenge back at the model: `Rhodes, Sam just said the war wasn't about slavery. Your response?` If it persists, note it and correct it yourself in the debrief — that is what the debrief is for.
- **Rhodes invents views on emancipation.** The prompt scripts him to mark the gap. If the model fills it anyway, treat it as a break: `Rhodes, stay inside what you wrote.` and note it under Historical notes.
- **Modern vocabulary.** If either man says "PTSD," "war crimes," "the Lost Cause," or similar, it's a break. Redirect in character-neutral terms and move on.
- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.

## Historical notes for the teacher

- Rhodes mustered in as a *corporal*, not a private (June 5, 1861) — the encounter says "rose from the ranks," which is accurate; the brief's "private" is corrected here.
- Rhodes's final rank: lieutenant colonel commanding the 2nd Rhode Island (Feb. 6, 1865), brevet colonel (Apr. 2, 1865). Publisher copy sometimes shortens this to "colonel."
- Watkins ended the war a corporal but calls himself a private throughout *Co. Aytch*; the encounter follows his usage.
- "Seven men left at the surrender" is Watkins's figure (North Carolina, April 1865), attributed to him.
- Watkins on the war's cause (ch. I, "Enlisted"): he names slavery directly, in period racial language the encounter paraphrases around per the library blacklist. The substance is the memoir's, not the encounter's invention.
- Rhodes's diary is genuinely terse on emancipation and Black soldiers; the figure's refusal to elaborate is fidelity, not evasion. Name the silence in the debrief.
- The two men never met. The dialogue is the encounter's one authored device, disclosed in the tutorial's setting note and in sources.md.

## One last thing

Teach them to ask the *paired* question. A student who asks only Watkins gets an elegy; a student who asks only Rhodes gets a monument. The encounter only works when the same question lands on both men — "Both of you: why did you enlist?" is the single skill that unlocks everything else. Say it out loud in the framing, and model it in your solo run.
