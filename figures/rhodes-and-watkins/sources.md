## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — say which.

- **The meeting itself.** Rhodes and Watkins never met; no record places them in the same place at any time. The entire dialogue is a pure dramatic device — the library's first two-voice encounter. Both men speak with full knowledge of their own lives through their own deaths and nothing after.
- **The response round.** The turn-taking machinery (paired answers, one reply each, alternating lead) is an encounter convention, not a historical reconstruction.
- **In-voice renderings, not quotations.** The canonical answers are paraphrase-close renderings in each man's voice, traced to chapters or diary periods — not verbatim recitation. This is deliberate: it keeps the voice bounded by the corpus without pretending to a textual precision the encounter cannot guarantee, and it keeps the Rhodes volume (edited 1985) within fair-use handling. A few short famous phrases ("all for the Union," "I came home with a broken heart") are used as the men's own signature words.
- **Rhodes's emancipation answer.** The published diary is terse on emancipation and Black soldiers; the figure is scripted to mark that gap honestly rather than fill it. The silence is the corpus's, not an invention.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer.

- **Rhodes's enlistment rank.** The figure brief says "private"; Rhodes actually mustered in as a corporal (June 5, 1861, Co. D, 2nd Rhode Island). The encounter says "rose from the ranks," which is accurate; "private" is corrected here.
- **Rhodes's final rank.** Sources vary: lieutenant colonel commanding the regiment (substantive, Feb. 6, 1865) with a brevet (honorary) colonelcy (Apr. 2, 1865). Publisher copy sometimes shortens this to "colonel." The debrief uses the precise form.
- **Watkins's rank.** He served in the ranks and ended the war a corporal; throughout *Co. Aytch* he calls himself a private, and the encounter follows his own usage.
- **"Seven survivors."** Watkins writes that seven men of his company remained at the surrender in North Carolina (April 1865). This is his memoir's figure; regimental records are fragmentary. The encounter attributes it to him, not to the archive.
- **Watkins on the war's cause.** In chapter I ("Enlisted"), Watkins addresses the cause directly and names slavery — in the racial language of his day. The encounter paraphrases around the period slur per the library blacklist; the substance (slavery named as the cause, owned by none of the men in the ranks, fought for home regardless) is the memoir's.
- **Watkins on Black soldiers.** The memoir touches on Black Federal troops late in the war but briefly; the figure is scripted not to elaborate beyond that. Do not let the model invent Watkins opinions on emancipation beyond what is scripted.
- **The "fun" arc.** Watkins's famous framing — enlisted for fun, came home with a broken heart — is the memoir's own arc, from chapter I to the final chapter ("The End").
- **Both men are DEAD.** Watkins died July 20, 1901 (Maury County, Tennessee); Rhodes died January 14, 1917 (Providence, Rhode Island). The DEAD RULE is satisfied.
- **Ken Burns.** Both men's words were popularized by *The Civil War* (1990). The figures do not know this (date discipline); it belongs in the debrief.
