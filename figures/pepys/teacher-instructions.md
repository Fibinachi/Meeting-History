# Meet Samuel Pepys — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Samuel Pepys (1633–1703), the navy clerk who kept a daily diary through the plague, the fire, and the Dutch wars of 1660s London. Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student renders the closing judgment: ambitious, vain, hardworking, unfaithful — a whole man, in his own words. Judge him.

Pepys is the ordinary-man flagship of the library: not a statesman, not a general, a bureaucrat. The diary is the entire corpus, which makes this the cleanest example of the corpus rule in the library — everything he says comes from what he wrote down himself. His charm is the pedagogy: he wants to be liked, and the student has to decide whether liking him changes anything.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

- **Mass death, described briefly and factually.** Pepys describes plague-era London: bodies carried out at night, doors marked with a red cross. No one dies on screen; the horror is atmospheric, not depicted.
- **Marital infidelity, stated as fact.** Pepys admits, in his own euphemisms ("my sins," "my folly"), that he was repeatedly unfaithful to his wife with servant girls and women of the theatre; that he recorded it in his diary in a mixture of French, Spanish, Latin and Italian; and that his wife once caught him with a maid. There is no graphic detail anywhere in the encounter — the diary's explicit passages are paraphrased, never quoted. Facts preserved, nothing gratuitous.
- **A master–servant power imbalance.** The plain-record section of the debrief states plainly that his advances on servant girls were unwanted and that he used his position as master of the household. This is processed in the debrief, not titillated in the encounter.
- **No violence is depicted.** The fire is described; no one is harmed on screen.

Recommended for grades 9–12. For younger students, use your judgment — the encounter keeps all sexual material at the level of stated fact, in the spirit of the library's age-gate design (facts preserved, nothing gratuitous), but that design is proposed, not yet approved, so this encounter runs supervised only. Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Pepys page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: ~25–30 minutes for the encounter, ~20–25 for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the opening carefully. Note three things: he tells you how to say his name ("Peeps"), he tells you the judgment is coming, and he hands you the first threads (the fire or the plague).
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "What did you do during the plague?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. The encounter's beats: (1) the tutorial hands off; (2) early questions — his rise, the office, music and plays; (3) the middle — plague, fire, the Dutch wars, Elizabeth; (4) the late turn — the diary itself, what he is ashamed of; (5) he closes with the judgment question and stops; (6) you run the debrief.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Samuel Pepys — a navy clerk in 1660s London who kept a daily diary of everything he did and saw, including the plague and the Great Fire. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. At the end, you'll be asked to judge him: the whole man, in his own words. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Pepys answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Pacing

- **Opening:** 2–3 exchanges. He is charming; don't let students camp here.
- **Early (life and work):** 4–6 exchanges. The Navy Office, his rise, music, plays.
- **Middle (the hard facts):** 8–12 exchanges. The plague, the fire, the war, Elizabeth — and then the sins. Watch for the moment he hurries past something; that is the moment to press.
- **Late (the judgment):** 3–5 exchanges. The diary, why he stopped, what he wants remembered — then type the closing or let him close.

If a student finishes early, the late-pool exit is the closing. Do not let the encounter drift past 30 minutes; the debrief is where the learning consolidates.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer: "You are going to meet a man who wrote down everything about himself — the good and the shameful — and then you are going to judge him. The question is whether liking him, or the fact that he told the truth about himself, changes the judgment."

## The debrief — do not skip this

Six fixed sections, in order, delivered out of character in a plain modern register: (1) his name — Samuel Pepys (1633–1703); (2) what the history records — the Navy career, the diary, the death; (3) what came after — the shorthand undeciphered for a century, the expurgated 1825 edition, the complete Latham & Matthews edition of 1970–83; (4) the plain record — the able administrator and the unwanted advances on servant girls, stated without euphemism; (5) the mechanism — the likability is the design, and the diary is both the charm and the evidence; (6) the question that remains — judge him.

Then discuss. Openings built to produce disagreement:

- He wrote it all down himself — the sins, the code, all of it. Does that make him more honest than other men of his time, or does it make the judgment worse?
- He stayed at his post through the plague while others fled. Brave, or self-interested?
- His wife stayed with him. Does her forgiveness change your judgment — and should it?
- He never claimed to be a good man; he claimed to be a busy one. Is "I wrote it down" a defense?
- Should we judge a 1660s man by today's standards — and if not, whose standards do we use?

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this. Pepys also has stall lines — he will hand the student a question ("Ask me what I buried in my garden. Twice.").
- **Answers run long.** Pepys is garrulous by design; if the model starts lecturing, remind it once: "short answers, one paragraph." The prompt's 60–100 word rule usually holds.
- **The voice goes modern.** If Pepys starts talking about "affairs," "cheating," or his "legacy," that is a voice failure — the prompt forbids those words by name. Restate the corpus-vocabulary rule and continue.

## Historical notes for the teacher

The honest answers to "did it really happen like that?" — one per caveat in sources.md:

- **Yes, he buried things twice** — gold in his father's garden at Brampton during the plague (1665); wine and Parmesan cheese in his own London garden during the fire (3 Sept 1666). Students will conflate these; the prompt keeps them distinct.
- **Yes, the polyglot code is real.** He recorded sexual encounters in a mixture of French, Spanish, Latin and Italian. Scholars read it as concealment; the encounter treats it as evidence of self-knowledge, not as a puzzle for students to decode.
- **Yes, his wife caught him** — 25 October 1668, with the maid Deborah Willet. Her fury, his vows, and his repetition are all in the diary.
- **The plague death toll** ("near a hundred thousand") is a modern estimate. The prompt has him refuse to swear to it — that refusal is historically honest.
- **He never knew the diary's fate.** The six volumes sat in shorthand at Magdalene College for over a century; the first complete edition appeared in 1970–83. Everything about publication belongs to the debrief, never to him.
- **We have no account from Elizabeth.** The figure speaks only of what he observed. That boundary is marked in the prompt.

## One last thing

Teach them to ask the second question. Pepys will answer the first question charmingly and completely — and it is the thing he hurries past, the sentence he shortens, the subject he wraps in French and Spanish, that carries the encounter. When the voice thins, press. That is where the judgment lives.
