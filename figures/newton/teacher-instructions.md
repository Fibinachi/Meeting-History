# Meet Isaac Newton — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Isaac Newton (1643–1727), run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student renders the closing judgment: *the genius who remade the world and ruined a rival to do it. Judge him.*

The voice is towering, suspicious, and solitary: certain of his genius and certain he is surrounded by thieves. He explains gravity and the prism experiments plainly, in his own period's words — and he describes, without remorse, how he used the presidency of the Royal Society to write the verdict in his own priority dispute. The encounter is the library's de-mythologizing entry: the legend says the apple; the record says the committee.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design. In particular: Newton will not concede that Leibnitz invented the calculus independently, will not apologize for the *Commercium Epistolicum*, and will not discuss his alchemy beyond guardedness. The debrief is where the modern view is stated.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

- Students will hear a man describe, calmly and without remorse, how he staged the verdict that destroyed a rival's reputation — and present it as justice.
- The Mint years include executions stated as fact: William Chaloner was hanged at Tyburn in 1699 after begging Newton personally for mercy. No graphic detail; the severity is in the plainness.
- Nothing here is sexual or slur-laden. The hard material is moral, not visual: a great man describing ruthless acts as righteous ones.

Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Newton page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: 25 for the encounter, 20 for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the opening carefully: Newton states the format ("You may ask what you will"), names his three domains (the heavens, the light, the Mint), and tells you men tried to take the first from him. That last sentence is the thread.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: *"How does the moon stay up?"* Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. The beats: (a) his work — gravity, light, the Mint; (b) the turn — ask about Leibnitz, or Hooke, or the committee; (c) the admission — he tells you who wrote the verdict; (d) the closing question. Type *"I have no more questions"* or follow the late-pool exit to reach it.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Isaac Newton — the man who explained gravity and light. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. At the end you'll be asked to judge him: the genius who remade the world and ruined a rival to do it. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Newton answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.
- If a student stalls, Newton hands them a question ("Ask me of the German, if you dare."). The stall lines are in the prompt.

### Pacing

- **Opening (2–3 exchanges):** his work — gravity, light. Don't let students camp on the apple; he shuts it down himself, and the suggestions move them on.
- **Middle (6–10 exchanges):** the hard facts — Leibnitz, the committee, Hooke, the Mint, the laboratory. This is where the encounter lives. Make sure every student reaches the Commercium Epistolicum admission.
- **Late (2–3 exchanges):** the judgment builds, then the closing question. End it there.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer: *Find out who Newton was — what he did, and what kind of man does those things — and at the end, judge him: the genius who remade the world and ruined a rival to do it.*

## The debrief — do not skip this

Run the six fixed sections in order (names, what the history records, what came after, the plain record, the mechanism, the question that remains) — the full text is in the prompt. Then open discussion. These are built to produce disagreement:

- Newton believed the verdict was true — the letters were genuine. Does that change your judgment of how he got it?
- He had the method first in private; Leibnitz published first. Who "invented" the calculus — and does it matter that we still use Leibnitz's notation?
- Is there a difference between what he did to Leibnitz and what he did to Chaloner? Both men were destroyed by proceedings Newton controlled.
- He spent decades on alchemy and hid it all his life. Does that change what the *Principia* means?
- Can the work be separated from the man — or is that itself the wrong question?

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **The model softens him.** The likeliest failure: the model makes Newton warm, or turns the feud into a both-sides summary, or has him express remorse. None of that is in the record. Re-paste the voice notes if needed: *he pronounces; he never apologizes for his wars.*
- **The model explains the calculus in modern notation.** Newton speaks of fluxions, not derivatives. If a student needs the modern bridge, give it yourself in the debrief — don't let the figure break period to do it.
- **The model mocks the alchemy.** The guardrail forbids this explicitly. Newton was secretive about the laboratory, not foolish about it; the figure stays guarded, and the debrief states the scale of the manuscripts plainly.

## Historical notes for the teacher

The honest answers to "did it really happen like that?" — one bullet per caveat from sources.md.

- **The apple** comes only from Stukeley's 1752 memoir, recalling conversations from the 1720s. It is evidence Newton told the story late in life, not evidence of how gravitation was found. Newton says this himself in the encounter.
- **The Hooke portrait** is legend. A portrait existed (seen in 1710); it went missing when the Society moved premises under Newton's presidency. No evidence connects Newton to its loss, and Hooke's biographer Allan Chapman rejects deliberate destruction as myth. The figure's "I do not burn pictures" is our device for marking this honestly.
- **"Standing on the shoulders of giants"** is real (letter to Hooke, 5 February 1676). Whether it mocked Hooke's build is debated, not settled — the encounter doesn't use the line.
- **The verdict was staged; the evidence was real.** Newton appointed the committee, directed the report, and anonymously wrote its review — and the old letters it printed were genuine. Both halves of that sentence are true, and the judgment the student renders has to hold both.
- **Modern consensus is independent invention** of the calculus. The debrief states it; Newton never hears it.
- **The alchemy is the larger body of writing.** Roughly a million words of alchemy, theology, and prophecy survive — far more than the published science. Keynes's 1936 purchase made this undeniable. Students find this destabilizing; that is the point.
- **Dates are Old Style/New Style.** England used the Julian calendar: born 25 December 1642 (O.S.), died 20 March 1726 (O.S.) — rendered 1643–1727 New Style throughout the library.

## One last thing

Teach them to ask the second question. The first question gets the legend — the apple, the genius. The second question gets the record: "Who wrote the verdict?" "What happened to Hooke's name in the *Principia*?" "What did you do at the Mint?" Newton's voice is built so that the polite questions receive pronouncements and the pressing questions receive the evidence. The students who press are the ones who meet him.
