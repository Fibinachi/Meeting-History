# Sources — Isaac Newton

The corpus this figure's voice is bounded by. The voice may claim only what these texts support. Newton died 20 March 1726 (Old Style) / 31 March 1727 (New Style) in London — DEAD RULE satisfied; the library uses 1643–1727 throughout.

A note on the shape of the corpus, stated honestly: the published science is the smaller part of what Newton wrote. The surviving manuscripts on alchemy, theology, and prophecy run to roughly a million words — far more than the *Principia* and the *Opticks* combined — and almost none of it was published in his lifetime. The encounter does not hide this; the figure is guarded about it, as Newton was.

## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — said plainly here.

- **The 1726 study visit.** Newton receiving a student-examiner at No. 35 St Martin's Street in early 1726 and answering questions about his life is a pure dramatic device. The house and dates are documented; the conversation is not.
- **The free admission of the verdict's authorship.** Historically Newton published the *Commercium Epistolicum* report and the 1716 "Account" anonymously and never publicly acknowledged writing them. Having him state it plainly to an examiner is plausible — he believed the verdict true and saw nothing to hide — but not documented.
- **The in-character denial about the portrait** ("I do not burn pictures"). No record shows Newton addressing the portrait story; the denial is our device for marking the legend honestly inside the encounter rather than letting the student carry it away unexamined.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Written before the figure ships, even the ones that don't matter.

- **The apple is anecdote, not history.** The only source is Stukeley's memoir of 1752, recalling conversations from the 1720s — written twenty-five years after Newton's death. It is evidence that Newton told the story late in life, not evidence of how the theory of gravitation was found. The encounter has him say exactly this.
- **The Hooke portrait: legend, not fact.** A portrait of Hooke existed (a visitor saw "Boyle and Hoock" portraits at the Society in 1710); after the Society's 1710 move to Crane Court, Hooke's was the only portrait missing. There is no evidence Newton destroyed it, and Hooke's biographer Allan Chapman rejects deliberate destruction as a myth. Negligence under Newton's presidency is the most that is documented. The encounter marks this.
- **"Shoulders of Giants" may not be a dig.** The line is real (letter to Hooke, 5 February 1676). Whether it mocked Hooke's build is debated by scholars, not settled. The encounter does not use the line at all — it is too tempting and too uncertain.
- **The calculus: independent invention is the modern consensus.** Newton had the method first in his private papers (1669–1671); Leibnitz published first (1684). Both claims the figure makes about dates are true; his inference of theft is his own. The debrief states the consensus plainly; the figure never hears it.
- **Leibnitz died in 1716**, ten years before the encounter's setting, with the Royal Society's verdict standing against him. The priority dispute continued through surrogates (Bernoulli, Keill) into the 1720s.
- **Chaloner was hanged at Tyburn on 22 March 1699**, convicted of high treason on 3 March. Counterfeiting the coin was high treason; the sentence was death. His final letter to Newton begging for mercy ("your near murdered humble servant") is documented; Newton's refusal to intervene is documented.
- **Old Style / New Style dates.** England still used the Julian calendar in Newton's lifetime. He was born 25 December 1642 (O.S.) = 4 January 1643 (N.S.), and died 20 March 1726 (O.S.) = 31 March 1727 (N.S.). The library uses 1643–1727 throughout.
- **He never married and had no children.** His household in his last years was kept by his niece Catherine Barton Conduitt and her husband John Conduitt, who became his executor.
- **The alchemy was the larger body of writing.** This surprises students and some teachers. It is the scholarly consensus (Keynes's 1936 purchase made it undeniable) and the encounter treats it as fact, not speculation.
- **He was Lucasian Professor from 1669**, Fellow of the Royal Society from 1672, its President from 1703 (elected 30 November 1703, months after Hooke's death), Warden of the Mint from 1696, Master from 1699, knighted by Queen Anne in 1705, and sat twice in Parliament for Cambridge University.
- **DEAD RULE satisfied:** Isaac Newton died 31 March 1727 (N.S.) in London and was buried in Westminster Abbey.
