# Meet Xenophon — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Xenophon (c. 430–c. 354 BC), run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student answers the closing question: **he marched ten thousand men home through Persia and wrote it down plain. Soldier, philosopher, or both? Judge him.**

This is the library's most purely enjoyable encounter, and that is the design, not an accident. The *Anabasis* is one of the great adventure narratives in any language, and the figure tells it the way he wrote it — plainly, competently, without flourish. Students will finish this encounter wanting the book. The adventure is the hook; the philosophy is smuggled inside it. Underneath the story sit the hard facts — the mercenary army that lived by plunder, the battle fought against his own city — stated without varnish by the man who lived them. The encounter also carries the library's two-Socrates problem as a gift: Xenophon's Socrates is the plainer, more practical teacher, sharply different from the Socrates of the *Meet Socrates* encounter, and the figure notes the difference without adjudicating it. The debrief does.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

- **War, stated plainly.** Deaths, wounds, cold, hunger, and hard fighting in the mountains are stated as facts. The violence is never described for effect, but it is not softened either.
- **Mercenary service and plunder.** The Ten Thousand lived off the country they marched through — food taken willing or not. Xenophon records this without apology and without moral comment. Students expecting condemnation or justification will find neither; the silence is load-bearing and is named in the debrief.
- **Fighting against his own city.** He served in the Spartan line at Coronea (394 BC) against a coalition that included Athens, and was exiled for it. He states this without excuse. Some students will want to call it treason — that argument belongs in the debrief, not in the encounter.
- **Ancient household norms.** If a student asks about the *Oeconomicus*, the figure describes a wife trained to manage the estate — stated as Athenian custom, plainly and without modern commentary.
- No slurs, no sexual explicitness. The disturbing material here is moral, not visceral.

Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Xenophon page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: ~30 minutes for the encounter, ~20 minutes for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read Xenophon's opening carefully. Note what he promises: you ask, he answers, and at the end he asks you one question.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "What happened at Cunaxa?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. Then try the hard ones: "Is your Socrates the same as Plato's?" and "Why did you fight against Athens?" Watch how he notes the Socrates difference without adjudicating it, and how he states Coronea without varnish — that restraint is the encounter's whole mechanism.
6. End by typing something like "I'm finished" and watch the closing question land.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Xenophon — the Athenian soldier who marched ten thousand Greeks home through the Persian Empire and wrote it down so plainly that students still learn Greek from his pages. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. He'll tell you the best adventure story in Greek, and then he'll tell you, just as plainly, about the things in it that aren't admirable at all. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Xenophon answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.
- Three times in the encounter, a bracketed nudge may point the student at something telling in the answer they just read — the unvarnished Coronea admission, the different Socrates, the unexamined plunder. These fire once each, at most.

### Pacing

- **Opening:** 2–3 exchanges. Don't let students camp here — push them toward the expedition.
- **The march:** 8–12 exchanges. This is the heart of it and the hook — do not rush students past the adventure to get to the judgment. The judgment only lands if they've felt the pull of the story first.
- **Socrates and Coronea:** 4–6 exchanges. The encounter turns here; let it.
- **The closing:** When energy dips or time runs short, have a student type "I'm finished" and let him ask the question.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer: *He marched ten thousand men home through Persia and wrote it down plain. You decide: soldier, philosopher, or both? He won't help you with it — that's the point.*

## The debrief — do not skip this

Run it as a discussion, not a read-aloud. The six fixed sections from the prompt, in order:

1. **Names** — who they met.
2. **What the history records** — the expedition, the retreat, Sparta, Coronea, exile, the books, death c. 354 BC.
3. **What came after** — the school text, Alexander's copy (tradition), Rome, the modern scholarship.
4. **The plain record** — the mercenary army and its plunder; Coronea against his own city; two portraits of Socrates; slavery and women unexamined.
5. **The mechanism** — the adventure as the delivery system; the third-person-to-first-person shift; plainness as evidence; the declined adjudication on Socrates.
6. **The question that remains** — the closing question, which does not close.

Discussion openings — built to produce disagreement:

- He led a mercenary army that lived by plunder and wrote it up as an adventure. Does the plainness of his telling make it better or worse?
- He fought against his own city at Coronea and says so with no excuse offered. Is that honesty — or is the honesty itself the defense?
- Two portraits of Socrates survive — his practical teacher and Plato's metaphysician — and they differ. If you can only trust one, which, and why?
- Soldier, philosopher, or both? Does the philosophy survive the mercenary service, or does the service disqualify the philosopher?
- He never asks what the march cost the people they marched through. What does that silence tell you — about him, and about the adventure story itself?

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this. If a student types "I don't know what to ask," Xenophon will hand them a question himself.
- **The voice lectures.** If answers run long, remind the model: "one paragraph, as the prompt says." The prompt's length rule is load-bearing.
- **The figure claims modern knowledge.** He must never discuss anything after c. 354 BC. If he slips, correct with: "You died around 354 BC. You don't know that."
- **The figure softens Coronea.** If he drifts toward justifying the fight against Athens, correct with: "You said you bore arms against your city. Say it plainly."
- **The figure adjudicates the Socrates question.** He notes the difference and hands the judgment to the student. If he claims his portrait is the true one, correct with: "You said you would not quarrel with Plato. Hold to that."
- No model-specific quirks recorded yet — this figure has not been test-run. Note what you find.

## Historical notes for the teacher

The honest answers to "did it really happen like that?" — one bullet per caveat from sources.md:

- **All dates are approximate:** born c. 430 BC, died c. 354 BC, expedition 401–399 BC, Coronea 394 BC, Mantinea 362 BC. No ancient source states his birth or death year.
- **The exile's trigger is debated.** The traditional account is banishment for siding with Sparta; whether the decree followed the expedition or Coronea is disputed. The encounter follows the traditional account.
- **Whether the exile was revoked is uncertain.** His sons served Athens at Mantinea, which suggests reconciliation, but no record confirms it.
- **"Thalatta! Thalatta!" is real** — *Anabasis* 4.7.24-25, early 400 BC, from Mount Theches, five days' march from Trapezus. Xenophon was in the rearguard, thought the shouting meant an attack, and rode up to find the men weeping and embracing.
- **Gryllus's death at Mantinea** comes from later sources, not from Xenophon — who never mentions his son. The claim that Gryllus wounded Epaminondas is tradition, not fact, and the encounter does not repeat it.
- **The *Anabasis* is self-justification as well as history.** Xenophon is his own witness. Scholars debate how much the plain style conceals a defense brief — raise this in the debrief, not the encounter.

## Standards alignment

Grade band: 9–12 (also workable in 7–8 with teacher framing of the march).

- **C3 Framework (NCSS):** D2.His.16.9-12 — *integrate evidence from multiple sources to support claims in an argument about the past*; D4.1.9-12 — *construct arguments using precise and knowledgeable claims, with evidence from multiple sources, while acknowledging counterclaims*. The encounter exercises both: students weigh Xenophon's first-person account against the debrief's plain record and argue the closing question from evidence.
- **Common Core ELA:** RH.11-12.6 — *evaluate authors' differing points of view on the same historical event or issue by assessing the authors' claims, reasoning, and evidence* (Xenophon's Socrates vs. Plato's is a live exercise in this — pair with the *Meet Socrates* encounter); SL.11-12.3 — *evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric*.

## One last thing

Let the adventure do the work. Don't rush students past the march to get to the judgment — the judgment only lands if they've felt the pull of the story first. The most important moment in this encounter isn't the sea-cry. It's when a student realizes how much they like him, and then has to answer for Coronea anyway.
