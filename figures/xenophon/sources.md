## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — said plainly here.

- **The Corinth setting (c. 358 BC)** — no text documents Xenophon reviewing his life at Corinth. Diogenes Laertius places his last years at Corinth after the flight from Scillus. Historically plausible, not documented.
- **First-person voice** — the *Anabasis* narrates its author largely in the third person (Xenophon names himself only rarely). The encounter's "I" is a dramatic device; the debrief names the shift.
- **The compressed life-review** — no text shows him summarizing his whole life in one sitting. Pure dramatic device.
- **His stated modesty about Gryllus's absence from the *Hellenica*** ("a father may be silent where a historian must be plain") — the absence is the scholars' observation; the motive ascribed is the encounter's, not the text's.
- **The closing question** — authored for the library's closing-question invariant.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Written before the figure ships, even the ones that don't matter.

- **Birth and death dates are scholarly convention.** c. 430 BC (some give 431) to c. 354 BC. No ancient source states either. The figure never offers them.
- **The exile's trigger is debated.** The traditional account (Diogenes Laertius 2.51) is banishment for Laconism — siding with Sparta. Whether the decree followed the Persian expedition (c. 399) or Coronea (394) is disputed among scholars. The encounter follows the traditional account and does not adjudicate the trigger date.
- **Whether the exile was ever revoked is uncertain.** His sons served in the Athenian cavalry at Mantinea (362), which suggests reconciliation, but no record confirms a formal revocation. The figure's "I leave it to the city" is the honest position.
- **"Ten Thousand" is the conventional name.** At Cunaxa the Greek force numbered roughly 10,400 hoplites plus about 2,500 peltasts (*Anabasis* 1.7.10). The figure speaks loosely, as the tradition does.
- **Gryllus at Mantinea comes from later sources** (Diogenes Laertius, Pausanias). The claim that he struck Epaminondas's mortal wound is Athenian/Theban tradition, not Xenophon's — Xenophon never mentions his son at all. The encounter does not repeat the Epaminondas claim.
- **Alexander carrying the *Anabasis*** is later tradition, not in the corpus. The figure is never told it.
- **The Socratic problem.** Xenophon's Socrates and Plato's differ sharply — the practical moralist vs. the metaphysician. Scholars have argued for centuries about which is closer to the man. The encounter has Xenophon note the difference and decline to adjudicate it; the debrief states the problem plainly.
- **The *Anabasis* is self-justification as well as history.** Xenophon is his own witness, and hostile scholars read the plain style as a defense brief. The debrief names this; the figure is not asked to confess it.
- **DEAD RULE satisfied:** Xenophon died c. 354 BC at Corinth (or possibly Athens — the place is uncertain; the date is conventional).
