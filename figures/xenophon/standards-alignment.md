# Meet Xenophon — Standards Alignment (verified 2026-10-15)

Encounter: the student questions the Athenian soldier-writer about the march of the Ten Thousand — the adventure carries the philosophy. He states the plunder without apology, Coronea without varnish, and his Socrates without conceding it is the true one. The *Anabasis* was written by the man who commanded the march, largely in the third person, as if about someone else. It closes with the handoff question: *he marched ten thousand men home through Persia and wrote it down plain — soldier, philosopher, or both? Judge him.*

**Grade band fit for every framework below: 9–12** (high school ELA literacy / World History). Note: most states' *content* anchors for classical Greece sit in lower grades (see caveats); the 9–12 weight of this encounter comes from skills standards — source evaluation, memoir-as-evidence, argumentation — which are band-matched.

## 1. C3 Framework (National Council for the Social Studies, 2013)

Grade band: **9–12**. (Indicator texts verified against curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim; the official NCSS PDF was not directly retrievable in this session — see verification note at end.)

This encounter is the library's flagship **memoir-as-evidence** exercise: the primary source and its author are the same man.

### Dimension 2 — History

- **D2.His.11.9-12** — Critique the usefulness of historical sources for a specific historical inquiry based on their maker, date, place of origin, intended audience, and purpose. (The headline indicator for this encounter. The *Anabasis* is a memoir by the commander of the march, written at Scillus decades later, in the third person: the student interrogates maker (his own witness), date (retirement, after the fact), place of origin, intended audience, and purpose — and the debrief names the hostile reading that the plain style is a defense brief.)
- **D2.His.6.9-12** — Analyze the ways in which the perspectives of those writing history shaped the history that they produced. (Xenophon wrote the history of his own march — and his *Hellenica* never once names his son Gryllus, killed at Mantinea; the encounter stages the writer's perspective shaping the history, including what the writer leaves out.)
- **D2.His.9.9-12** — Analyze the relationship between historical sources and the secondary interpretations made from them. (The two-Socrates problem: Xenophon's *Memorabilia* and Plato's dialogues are two primary sources on the same teacher; centuries of secondary interpretation argue over which portrait is truer — the encounter has him note the difference and decline to adjudicate, so the interpretation work falls to the student.)
- **D2.His.16.9-12** — Integrate evidence from multiple relevant historical sources and interpretations into a reasoned argument about the past. (The closing question — soldier, philosopher, or both? — demands integrating the march narrative, Coronea, the household writings, and the Socratic problem into one reasoned judgment.)

### Dimension 4 — Communicating Conclusions and Taking Informed Action

- **D4.1.9-12** — Construct arguments using precise and knowledgeable claims, with evidence from multiple sources, while acknowledging counterclaims and evidentiary weaknesses. (The student's verdict must reckon with the counterclaims the encounter refuses to soften: the plundered villages, Coronea fought against his own city, the possibly-less-true Socrates — and the evidentiary weakness that he is his own only witness.)
- **D4.4.9-12** — Critique the use of claims and evidence in arguments for credibility. (His plainness is the evidence, not the conclusion: the debrief's design note — he states Coronea without varnish and the plunder without apology — is precisely what lets the student assess the credibility of the account.)

*Verified against: resources.finalsite.net (Greenwich Public Schools C3 curriculum); mnhs.org and historicfortsnelling.com (Minnesota Historical Society standards-connections documents); learninglab.si.edu; thinkport.org. Official document: College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework for Social Studies State Standards, NCSS, 2013 (socialstudies.org/c3).*

## 2. Common Core ELA — Literacy in History/Social Studies, Speaking/Listening & Writing

Grade band: **9–12**. (Verified against the official Common Core State Standards Initiative site, thecorestandards.org.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 9–10

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.6** — Compare the point of view of two or more authors for how they treat the same or similar topics, including which details they include and emphasize in their respective accounts. (Xenophon's *Memorabilia* vs. Plato's *Apology* on Socrates's trial and character — which details each includes and emphasizes: the practical moral teacher vs. the metaphysician.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.1** — Cite specific textual evidence to support analysis of primary and secondary sources, attending to such features as the date and origin of the information. (The *Anabasis* as primary source: the Carduchian mountains, the Armenian snow, the sea-cry at Theches — cited with attention to when and by whom the account was written.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.6** — Evaluate authors' differing points of view on the same historical event or issue by assessing the authors' claims, reasoning, and evidence. (The Socratic problem at full strength: assessing Xenophon's and Plato's competing claims about the same teacher on the strength of their reasoning and evidence.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.3** — Evaluate various explanations for actions or events and determine which explanation best accords with textual evidence, acknowledging where the text leaves matters uncertain. (The plunder and Coronea: the encounter gives the facts and withholds the moral frame — the student evaluates explanations (necessity, custom, treason) against his plain record, acknowledging where his silence leaves matters uncertain.)

### Speaking & Listening, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.1** — Initiate and participate effectively in a range of collaborative discussions (one-on-one, in groups, and teacher-led) with diverse partners on grades 11–12 topics, texts, and issues, building on others' ideas and expressing their own clearly and persuasively. (The encounter is a sustained one-on-one discussion — the student questions a man back from a long voyage, pressing where his plainness may be hiding something.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.3** — Evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric, assessing the stance, premises, links among ideas, word choice, points of emphasis, and tone used. (The nudge beats train this: what he does with the plunder, stated as fact — the student's ear for what plainness emphasizes and what it omits.)

### Writing, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.W.11-12.1** — Write arguments to support claims in an analysis of substantive topics or texts, using valid reasoning and relevant and sufficient evidence. (The closing question — soldier, philosopher, or both? — is designed as a post-encounter argument prompt, per the teacher instructions.)

## 3. South Carolina — Social Studies College- and Career-Ready Standards (2019)

Grade band: **Grade 6** for the classical-civilizations anchors. (No SC high-school standard names Xenophon or the Persian expedition; the 9–12 weight of this encounter is carried by the skills standards above.)

- **6.1.P** (Grade 6) — Analyze the shift from early to classical civilizations and the enduring contributions of classical civilizations. (The *Anabasis* — the founding adventure-history of the Western tradition, the text generations learned Greek from — is among classical Athens's enduring contributions. Grade-band caveat: this is SC's only classical-Greece content anchor, and it sits in Grade 6.)
- **6.1.E** (Grade 6) — Analyze multiple perspectives on the political, intellectual, and social achievements of classical societies through a variety of primary and secondary sources. (Xenophon's Socrates vs. Plato's — two primary perspectives on the same Athenian achievement, philosophy, in direct tension; the encounter's debrief names the Socratic problem for the student to adjudicate.)

*Verified against: IXL's South Carolina standards pages (grade-6 social studies); commonplanner.com SC Grade 6 compilation. (The 2019 standards are sometimes dated "adopted 2020" in compilations — same document.)*

## 4. Other states (quick hits)

### New York (K–12 Social Studies Framework, Grades 9–10)

- **9.3b** — Empires used belief systems, systems of law, forms of government, military forces, and social hierarchies to consolidate and expand power. (The encounter traverses the Persian Empire at its height — satraps, the King's army, Tissaphernes's treachery — the imperial military machinery the Ten Thousand survived.)
- **9.3c** — A period of peace, prosperity, and cultural achievements can be designated as a Golden Age. (Students "examine the achievements of Greece" — the *Anabasis* as a Greek prose achievement belongs to the evidence set for judging a Golden Age.)

*Caveat: 9.3b's performance guidance names Mauryan, Qin, and Roman civilizations as its exemplars — Persia fits the indicator's general principle but is not a named exemplar. 9.3c names Greece explicitly.*

*Verified against: the NYS K–12 Social Studies Framework via nysacourse.weebly.com (official framework PDF mirror, revised February 2017).*

### Texas (TEKS, World History Studies, adopted 2022)

- **WH.1.B** (§113.42) — Identify major causes and describe the major effects of the following events from 500 BC to AD 600: the development of the classical civilizations of Greece, Rome, Persia, India (Maurya and Gupta), China (Zhou, Qin, and Han), and the development of major world religions. (Xenophon's expedition and retreat sit inside the band on both sides: classical Greece (the Ten Thousand) and Persia (Cyrus, Artaxerxes) are both named civilizations in the indicator.)

*Verified against: commonplanner.com compilation of the 2022-adopted World History Studies TEKS — not the TEA-hosted PDF. Citation of the code is exact; the indicator text should be rechecked against tea.texas.gov before publication use.*

### Florida (NGSSS / State Academic Standards)

- **SS.912.W.2.13** — Explain how Western civilization arose from a synthesis of classical Greco-Roman civilization, Judeo-Christian influence, and the cultures of northern European peoples promoting a cultural unity in Europe. (The *Anabasis* as founding Western adventure-historiography — Alexander reportedly carried it; Rome admired him — feeds the Greco-Roman synthesis the benchmark covers.)
- **SS.6.W.3.6** (Grade 6) — Determine the impact of key figures from ancient Greece. (The tighter content anchor; Xenophon is not among the named examples (Aristotle, Hippocrates, Homer, Plato, Socrates) — he sits in the "key figures" category. Grade-band caveat.)

*Verified against: fldoe.org instructional-materials documents (official, for SS.912.W.2.13); commonplanner.com and socialstudiesonline.com Grade 6 Florida compilations (for SS.6.W.3.6). Codes are stable across NGSSS and the 2023 State Academic Standards adoption.*

### California (History–Social Science Content Standards, Grade 6)

- **6.4.8** — Describe the enduring contributions of important Greek figures in the arts and sciences (e.g., Hypatia, Socrates, Plato, Aristotle, Euclid, Thucydides). (Xenophon is not named in the parenthetical — the "e.g." list is not closed, but the fit is via the "important Greek figures" category; the historian-soldier is the enduring contribution.)
