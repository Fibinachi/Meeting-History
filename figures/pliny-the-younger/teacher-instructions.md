# Meet Pliny the Younger — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Pliny the Younger (AD 61–c. 113), run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student renders the closing judgment, in plain words: a humane man who administered an empire's cruelties politely. Judge him.

Pliny is the gentlest Roman in the library: a conscientious imperial governor whose published letters accidentally preserved his world — Vesuvius, his griefs, his wife, his administration. He likes the student, and the student will like him. The encounter is built so that liking him is the beginning of the judgment, not the end of it.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

Students will encounter, in the figure's own calm administrative language:

- An eyewitness account of a volcanic eruption that killed thousands, including the death of the figure's uncle on a beach at Stabiae.
- Executions ordered by the figure: he interrogated accused Christians three times and ordered those who would not recant to be put to death. He states this plainly and without distress.
- Torture: he had two enslaved women "put to the question" for information. He uses the euphemism; the debrief names it.
- State persecution of a religious minority, described by its administrator as careful procedure.

None of this is depicted or dwelled on — the figure states facts and moves on, which is itself part of the design. Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Pliny the Younger page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: 25–30 minutes for the encounter, 20–30 minutes for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the opening carefully: he introduces himself, explains the format, and tells the student most begin with the mountain.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "Tell me about the Christians." Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. The beats: tutorial → conversation (Vesuvius first, then the province, then the hard questions) → he closes with the judgment question → the debrief. He asks the closing question and stops; you take it from there.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with a Roman governor from two thousand years ago — a man who watched a volcano bury cities and wrote it all down in letters he published himself. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. He is humane, careful, honest — and he did terrible things with clean hands. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Pliny answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Pacing

- **Opening (2–3 exchanges):** Vesuvius. Let them have it — it is the hook, and his telling is magnificent. Don't let students camp here past three exchanges.
- **Middle (the hard facts):** the province, the Christians, the speech of praise for Trajan. This is where the encounter lives. If a student hasn't asked about the Christians by exchange six, point at the bracketed suggestions.
- **Late (the judgment):** his wife, his griefs, why he published his letters — then the closing. He asks the question and stops.

## The goal, stated plainly

The student will like a humane man and then have to judge him anyway. The whole thing in one sentence: whether carefulness is the same as justice, and whether a good man inside a cruel system is judged by his manners or by what his manners administered.

## The debrief — do not skip this

The six fixed sections, in order: his name; what the history records; what came after (the letters became antiquity's most-read text, "Plinian" eruptions, the Christians letter as the earliest non-Christian account); the plain record (humane procedure in service of persecution — the executions, the torture, the anonymous denunciations, all stated without euphemism); the mechanism (the likability is the design; he curated his own portrait; his vocabulary does the moral work); the question that remains.

Discussion openings — built to produce disagreement:

- Pliny says he punished obstinacy, not belief. Is that a real distinction, or is it the distinction his job needed?
- He wrote to the Emperor for instructions instead of deciding himself. Does that make him more responsible or less?
- He chose which letters to publish. How does knowing that change what you make of him?
- Trajan told him not to hunt Christians down — but confirmed the convicted should be punished. Was Trajan more humane than Pliny, or just more political?
- He grieved for his own slaves and had two enslaved Christian women tortured. He never connects the two. Should we?

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **The figure moralizes in modern terms** ("persecution was wrong," "human rights"). That is a voice failure — the prompt forbids it explicitly. Restart the turn: re-paste the closing instruction or simply ask the question again.
- **The figure claims vindication** ("history proved me right about the Christians"). Voice failure; same remedy.
- **The figure softens the Christians episode** (downplaying the executions or the torture). The prompt holds the candor as the evidence — "those who persisted I ordered executed" and "I had them put to the question" are scripted. If the model hedges, press with the scripted wording.

## Historical notes for the teacher

One bullet per caveat from sources.md — the honest answers to "did it really happen like that?"

- He published Books 1–9 himself and chose what to keep; Book 10 was published after his death by unknown hands. The portrait is his own curation, and he admits it in the encounter.
- He died c. 113, probably in Bithynia; the exact date and place are uncertain. He was born c. 61 (some sources say 62).
- He dates the eruption to August 24; some modern scholars argue for autumn. He died at Stabiae, not Pompeii; the cause of his uncle's death is uncertain.
- His claim to have been in danger under Domitian is doubted by scholars — his career advanced unhindered. The terror of others under Domitian is documented; the danger to himself is his claim.
- "Put to the question" is torture. The figure's euphemism is load-bearing; the debrief says it plainly.
- Trajan's reply restrained him (no hunting Christians out, no anonymous denunciations) but confirmed punishment for the convicted who persisted. The persecution did not end with the rescript.

## Pairing suggestion (noted, not built)

Pairs with the Pepys encounter — the bureaucrats across the millennia: two conscientious administrators, two self-curated archives (Pliny published his; Pepys never meant his to be read), two likable men, two different judgments. Run Pliny first, then Pepys, and ask the class what changes when the archive was never meant for them.

## One last thing

Listen for the euphemisms. Pliny's cruelty arrives entirely in the language of care — "I took care," "I asked the Emperor," "put to the question," "their stubbornness deserved it." Teach students to hear what the careful words are carrying. He will never say the plain thing; that is the teacher's job in the debrief, and the student's job in the judgment. The question lives in the vocabulary.
