## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — say which.

- **The setting (Winter, AD 112, Nicomedia, receiving a student in conversation)** — pure dramatic device. No such meeting happened. The letters are the encounter's only real meeting-place.
- **Compression of a life into one conversation** — he discusses events 30+ years apart as present memory. Standard for the library; the dates are real, the simultaneity is staged.
- **Placement after Trajan's reply** — the encounter is set after his receipt of Trajan's rescript on the Christians (10.97). The correspondence is documented; the exact timing of the conversation within his governorship is approximate.
- **The closing question** — he never asked it. The judgment is the student's; the figure only hands it over.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Written before the figure ships, even the ones decided not to matter.

- **Self-publication shapes the portrait.** Books 1–9 were selected, arranged, and published by Pliny himself (he says so in 1.1, to Septicius Clarus). He kept what he judged worth keeping. Book 10 — the official correspondence with Trajan — was published posthumously, by unknown hands, and was not curated by him. What he held back is unknown. The encounter has him admit the curation himself.
- **Death date and place are uncertain.** "c. 113" is the modern consensus; some older accounts (e.g. the 1911 Britannica via Huxley) say 114. The Letters give no hint of a return from Bithynia; he died abroad or very soon after. Mark it uncertain, never assert it.
- **Birth year is slightly uncertain.** Latter half of 61 or beginning of 62 (Huxley). "c. 61" is the convention; the brief uses AD 61.
- **Governorship dates are approximate.** c. 110–112 in most modern accounts; 111–112 or 112–113 in others. He reached Bithynia in September of his first year and held office fifteen months or more.
- **The eruption's month is disputed.** Pliny dates it to August 24 (6.16: the ninth day before the Kalends of September), and the encounter follows him. Some modern scholars argue for autumn on archaeological grounds (fruits, clothing, coin evidence). The figure does not know of the dispute.
- **His uncle died at Stabiae, not Pompeii.** At Pomponianus's villa, on the beach; the body found when the light returned. The cause is uncertain — Pliny reports only that his uncle's breathing failed in the dust-laden air (he was not physically strong). Modern guesses (asthma, heart attack) are guesses; mark the gap.
- **His claimed danger under Domitian is doubted.** In 3.11 he writes of standing "amidst the flames of thunderbolts dropping all round me." Scholars note his career advanced unhindered under Domitian (praetor 93, treasury prefectures 94–100). The encounter has him say only "I kept my head down" — the terror of others is documented; the danger to himself is his claim.
- **The Panegyricus was revised for publication.** What survives is the enlarged published version, not a transcript of the Senate speech.
- **"Put to the question" is torture.** The figure's euphemism is load-bearing: in 10.96 he writes that he examined the two enslaved deaconesses *per tormenta*. The debrief says so plainly; the figure never does.
- **He was married three times.** Calpurnia was his third wife (married c. 103); the first two marriages are barely in the letters. The encounter does not hide this but does not foreground it — students who ask get the honest answer.
- **Trajan's reply restrained him, but the machinery continued.** Trajan forbade hunting Christians out and banned anonymous denunciations — but confirmed that the convicted who persisted were to be punished. The persecution did not end with the rescript.
