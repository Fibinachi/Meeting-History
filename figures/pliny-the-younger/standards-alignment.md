# Meet Pliny the Younger — Standards Alignment (verified 2026-10-15)

Encounter: you meet Gaius Plinius Caecilius Secundus at Nicomedia in winter AD 112 — two years into his governorship of Bithynia-Pontus, fresh from receiving Trajan's rescript on the Christians — the humane, conscientious bureaucrat who interrogated Christians three times and executed the obstinate, acted on an unsigned pamphlet, and had two enslaved deaconesses "put to the question," all politely, all in careful letters to the Emperor. The encounter closes on the question he cannot answer himself: a humane man who administered an empire's cruelties politely — judge him.

**Grade band fit for every framework below: 9–12** (high school ELA literacy / World History). Note: most states' *content* anchors for classical Rome sit in Grade 6 (see caveats); the 9–12 weight of this encounter comes from skills standards — close reading of primary texts, discussion, argumentation — which are band-matched.

## 1. C3 Framework (National Council for the Social Studies, 2013)

Grade band: **9–12**. (Indicator texts verified against curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim; the official NCSS PDF was not directly retrievable in this session — see verification note at end.)

### Dimension 2 — History

- **D2.His.4.9-12** — Analyze complex and interacting factors that influenced the perspectives of people during different historical eras. (His perspective — the governor who "would rather ask a hundred questions than answer one unjustly" — shaped by the terror of Domitian's last years, the example of Trajan's "liberty with order," and the Roman bureaucrat's ethic: ask rather than presume.)
- **D2.His.8.9-12** — Analyze how current interpretations of the past are limited by the extent to which available historical sources represent perspectives of people at the time. (The record contains his procedure but never the Christians' own account — and never the two enslaved deaconesses' — and the accusers' unsigned pamphlet is nameless: the encounter's judgment is limited by exactly whose perspectives the sources represent.)
- **D2.His.10.9-12** — Detect possible limitations in various kinds of historical evidence and differing secondary interpretations. (His vocabulary is the evidence problem: "superstition" for their religion, "obstinacy" for their refusal to recant, "put to the question" for torture — the euphemisms the debrief translates plainly, which the figure never does.)
- **D2.His.11.9-12** — Critique the usefulness of historical sources for a specific historical inquiry based on their maker, date, place of origin, intended audience, and purpose. (The two corpora: Books 1–9 selected, arranged, and published by Pliny himself (maker, audience, purpose — "the truest picture he is willing to show") vs Book 10's official correspondence, published posthumously and never curated by him — the portrait vs the file.)
- **D2.His.16.9-12** — Integrate evidence from multiple relevant historical sources and interpretations into a reasoned argument about the past. (The closing judgment requires integrating 10.96, Trajan's 10.97, his grief for his own slaves (8.16) — which he never sets beside the tortured deaconesses — and the Panegyricus's public flattery into one verdict.)

### Dimension 4 — Communicating Conclusions and Taking Informed Action

- **D4.1.9-12** — Construct arguments using precise and knowledgeable claims, with evidence from multiple sources, while acknowledging counterclaims and evidentiary weaknesses. (The closing question is the argument prompt — and the debrief's model sentence is the counterclaim handled honestly: "The reluctance is real. It does not change what the procedure did.")
- **D4.4.9-12** — Critique the use of claims and evidence in arguments for credibility. (Students test his evidence against his design: the likability is the design — he chose every letter, every word — and judge whether carefulness is the same as justice.)

*Verified against: multiple independent curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim (2026-10-15; they agree with each other — womenshistory.org lesson plans, thinkport.org inquiry kits, curriculum.llilasbenson.utexas.edu units, electionlab.org alignment). Official document: College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework for Social Studies State Standards, NCSS, 2013 (socialstudies.org/c3).*

## 2. Common Core ELA — Literacy in History/Social Studies & Speaking/Listening

Grade band: **9–12**. (Verified against the official Common Core State Standards Initiative site, thecorestandards.org.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 9–10

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.1** — Cite specific textual evidence to support analysis of primary and secondary sources, attending to such features as the date and origin of the information. (The debrief's plain record is built from his own letter's steps — the threefold questioning, the executions, the citizens remitted to Rome, the two ministrae *per tormenta* — and students cite 10.96 by date and occasion against his self-portrait.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.6** — Compare the point of view of two or more authors for how they treat the same or similar topics, including which details they include and emphasize in their respective accounts. (Pliny on the Christians (10.96) vs Pliny on his own household's dead (8.16) — the grief he grants his slaves, the torture he orders for the deaconesses — compared for which details each account includes and emphasizes; the figure never sets them side by side, the student does.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.8** — Assess the extent to which the reasoning and evidence in a text support the author's claims. (His claim "I would rather ask a hundred questions than answer one unjustly" — tested against the procedure: did writing to the Emperor discharge him, or did asking become the alibi?)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.1** — Cite specific textual evidence to support analysis of primary and secondary sources, connecting insights gained from specific details to an understanding of the text as a whole. (Details-to-whole: the single phrase "superstitionem pravam et immodicam" connected to the whole letter's machinery of procedure.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.4** — Determine the meaning of words and phrases as they are used in a text, including analyzing how an author uses and refines the meaning of a key term over the course of a text. (His vocabulary doing moral work across the letter: "superstition" for their religion, "obstinacy" for their refusal to recant, "put to the question" for torture — the load-bearing nudge: "Did you notice the words he uses for what he did — and what those words leave out?")
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.6** — Evaluate authors' differing points of view on the same historical event or issue by assessing the authors' claims, reasoning, and evidence. (His procedure (10.96) vs Trajan's rescript (10.97) on the same cases: not to be sought out, punished if convicted and persistent, pardoned on denial and proof, no anonymous accusations — the Emperor restraining the governor, claim by claim.)

### Speaking & Listening, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.1** — Initiate and participate effectively in a range of collaborative discussions (one-on-one, in groups, and teacher-led) with diverse partners on grades 11–12 topics, texts, and issues, building on others' ideas and expressing their own clearly and persuasively. (The encounter *is* a sustained one-on-one examination: the tutorial's brief is to "question him the way an examiner presses a witness" — find out what he did, and what kind of world he governed.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.3** — Evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric, assessing the stance, premises, links among ideas, word choice, points of emphasis, and tone used. (Students evaluate the warm, careful, avuncular voice as rhetoric — the warmth that does not break when the subject turns to the Christians, the procedure described "the way he describes an aqueduct: step by step, as a problem solved well" — and weigh the tone against the facts.)

### Writing, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.W.11-12.1** — Write arguments to support claims in an analysis of substantive topics or texts, using valid reasoning and relevant and sufficient evidence. (The closing question — a humane man who administered an empire's cruelties politely: judge him — is designed as a post-encounter argument prompt, per the teacher instructions.)

## 3. South Carolina — Social Studies College- and Career-Ready Standards (2019)

Grade band: **9–12** encounter weight is skills-based; the content anchors are Grade 6.

- **6.1.P** (Grade 6) — Analyze the shift from early to classical civilizations and the enduring contributions of classical civilizations. (State guidance names Rome among the "four major classical civilizations" whose "cultural and technological advancements... continue to influence modern societies" — his Vesuvius letters (6.16/6.20) are the foundation of the record: volcanologists named that kind of eruption after him, a "Plinian" eruption, and the letters are how we know the eruption's date. Grade-band caveat below.)
- **6.1.CX** (Grade 6) — Contextualize the origins and spread of major world religions and their enduring influence. (State guidance: inquiry into the "development, basic tenets, and impact" of Christianity "in relation to place and time" — his 10.96 is the earliest detailed non-Christian account of Christianity in existence, and Trajan's reply was cited as imperial precedent for centuries.)

*Verified against: the official 2019 South Carolina Social Studies College- and Career-Ready Standards document on ed.sc.gov (Grade 6 standards; indicators and state guidance quoted verbatim 2026-10-15).*

## Verification caveats

1. **C3 Framework**: indicator texts were verified against multiple independent curriculum documents that quote the Framework verbatim (they agree with each other); the official NCSS PDF itself was not directly retrievable this session. Treat the official publisher as NCSS (2013), socialstudies.org/c3.
2. **Grade-band split**: the 9–12 alignments for Pliny are skills standards (RH, SL, W, D4) plus the perspective/source-analysis C3 indicators. State content anchors for classical Rome sit below the 9–12 band except NY 9.3 (Grades 9–10), TX §113.42 (World History), and FL SS.912.W.2.13 (9–12).
3. **RH fit is debrief-driven**: the encounter proper is conversational; the reading standards engage through the debrief's primary-text work (10.96/10.97 side by side, 8.16 set against the deaconesses, 6.16/6.20) and the teacher-supplied excerpts of the corpus.
4. **No adopted SC high-school anchor for ancient Rome**: checked the 2019 SC high-school courses — Modern World History begins at 1300; USHC and US Government cover American history. 6.1.P and 6.1.CX (Grade 6) are the only SC content anchors.
