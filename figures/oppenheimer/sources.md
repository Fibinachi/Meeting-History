## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — say which.

- **The conversation itself.** Oppenheimer never gave an interview in this form, to a student, in his study. Pure dramatic device — the encounter's premise.
- **The setting: Princeton, December 1966.** A chosen moment in the last winter of his life (he died February 18, 1967), selected for its reflective register. He gave no such end-of-life accounting; the posture is authored, the facts are not.
- **The closing question ("Was I a good man?").** He never posed this question. It is the library's design, not his words — he recuses himself from his own judgment absolutely, which the voice-construction guide requires be stated as design, not history.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Written before the figure ships, even the ones decided not to matter.

- **"I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds" — the real context.** The line is from the Bhagavad Gita (chapter 11), which Oppenheimer read in Sanskrit — he studied the language under Arthur Ryder at Berkeley in 1933. He did NOT say it at Trinity. It is his recollection, given in a 1965 NBC interview, twenty years after the test, of what came to his mind. His brother Frank, who was present at Trinity, recalled him saying only "It worked." The quote did not circulate as Los Alamos lore until the late 1940s–1950s. Scholar James Hijiya argues its meaning is about duty (Arjuna must do his duty as a warrior), not self-accusation — the encounter lets the figure present the line and marks the timing honestly rather than resolving the interpretation.
- **The hearing transcript is adversarial testimony, not reflection.** It is thousands of pages of a man answering a hostile counsel's questions under oath. The lectures (*The Open Mind*, the Reith Lectures) are where he speaks most freely. The encounter treats the transcript as the anchor for facts and the lectures as the anchor for voice.
- **"Blood on my hands."** Oppenheimer is widely reported to have told President Truman, at their October 1945 meeting, that he felt he had blood on his hands; Truman was furious and later called him a "cry-baby scientist." The details come from Truman's later accounts and are disputed in their specifics. The encounter does not put the line in Oppenheimer's mouth; the figure says only that the meeting went badly and his counsel for international control was not taken up.
- **The Chevalier incident.** In 1943 his friend Haakon Chevalier passed along a Soviet-linked approach (via George Eltenton) seeking information; Oppenheimer rejected it but reported it late and then told security officers a fabricated version to protect Chevalier. Under oath in 1954 he admitted the fabrication and could offer no better explanation than "I was an idiot." His motives remain his own account; no fuller explanation exists in the record.
- **The 1954 decision was vacated — after his death.** In December 2022 the Department of Energy formally vacated the AEC's 1954 revocation, finding the process flawed. Oppenheimer died in 1967 believing the record stood. The figure must never know this; it belongs in the debrief's "what came after."
- **Hiroshima/Nagasaki casualty figures.** Historians' estimates vary; roughly 70,000–140,000 dead at Hiroshima and 60,000–80,000 at Nagasaki by the end of 1945. The debrief gives ranges, not a single number.
- **The Fermi Prize (1963).** Kennedy selected him; Johnson presented it on December 2, 1963. A public rehabilitation he lived to see — within the figure's knowledge, mentioned in the debrief only.
- **Teller's testimony.** Edward Teller testified at the hearing that he would prefer the country's vital interests in hands he understood better and trusted more. The figure declines to characterize Teller's motives — that restraint is authored to match the record of Oppenheimer's own public silence on the matter.
