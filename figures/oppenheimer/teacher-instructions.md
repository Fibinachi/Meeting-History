# Meet Oppenheimer — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with J. Robert Oppenheimer (1904–1967), run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student renders the closing judgment: **was he a good man?**

He is the physicist who directed the Los Alamos Laboratory, where the first atomic bombs were built — and the man who then opposed the hydrogen bomb, and was destroyed by a security hearing for the associations and evasions of his past. The voice is brilliant, literary, and haunted: he speaks from conviction, never asks for pardon, and will not judge himself. The judgment is the student's alone.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

Students will encounter:

- The atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, stated as historical fact with casualty ranges (tens of thousands dead in each city). No graphic descriptions — the figure states the facts plainly and does not describe suffering for effect.
- The moral weight of mass death, carried by the man who directed the laboratory that made it possible. He speaks of "sin" as a knowledge that cannot be lost.
- A portrait of political persecution: a man interrogated for weeks about his friendships, his clearance stripped, his public life ended — the McCarthy-era security state at work.
- No slurs, no gore, no sexual content.

Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Oppenheimer page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: 25–30 minutes for the encounter, 15–20 minutes for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the tutorial, then the figure's scripted opening. Note what he tells you up front: at the end, you will answer whether he was a good man.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "What did you feel at Trinity?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. The beats, in order: the tutorial hands off; you question him about Los Alamos, Trinity, the bombings, the hydrogen-bomb fight, and the 1954 hearing; when you say you are done, he asks the closing question and stops; then you run the debrief.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with J. Robert Oppenheimer — the physicist who ran the laboratory that built the atomic bomb. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. He will tell you what he did and why he says he did it. At the end he will ask you one question: was he a good man? There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Oppenheimer answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script. Three times in the encounter, the suggestion is a "did you notice" nudge pointing at something in the answer just given — underline, not annotation.
- When the student says they are done (or the conversation has run its course), Oppenheimer asks the closing question — "Was I a good man?" — and stops. The encounter ends; the debrief begins.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Pacing

- Opening and Los Alamos: 5–8 exchanges. Don't let students camp on the physics.
- Trinity, the bombings, the hydrogen bomb: 8–10 exchanges. This is the middle; the nudges fire here.
- The 1954 hearing: 5–8 exchanges. Then the closing.
- If a student reaches the closing in under 15 minutes, they haven't pressed hard enough — send them back in with one of the bracketed suggestions.

## The goal, stated plainly

The student will be asked to judge the man, not the policy. "Was the bomb justified" has answers; "was he a good man" has none that stay still. The encounter succeeds if the student is still arguing about him afterward.

## The debrief — do not skip this

Run it as a discussion, not a read-aloud. The six fixed sections, in order:

1. **His name.** You met J. Robert Oppenheimer (1904–1967).
2. **What the history records.** Los Alamos 1942–45; Trinity July 16, 1945; Hiroshima and Nagasaki three weeks later; GAC chair, unanimous October 1949 recommendation against the H-bomb crash program; 1954 hearing, clearance revoked; Institute for Advanced Study 1947–66; died February 18, 1967.
3. **What came after.** The 1963 Fermi Prize (rehabilitation he lived to see); the December 2022 vacatur of the 1954 decision, fifty-five years after his death — he never knew.
4. **The plain record.** He built the weapon that killed on the order of a hundred thousand people in each of two cities. He opposed the larger weapon. He was destroyed by the security state he served. He never claimed innocence and never named his persecutors.
5. **The mechanism.** His literary circling was the design; the short sentences were the landings. The Gita line was a 1965 memory, not a 1945 record — the myth was doing work the history can't. His refusal to judge himself was absolute, by design, so the judgment would be yours.
6. **The question that remains.** Was he a good man? It does not close when they leave the room.

Discussion openings (built to produce disagreement — do not resolve them):

- He called himself an idiot under oath rather than explain his lie about Chevalier. Is that honesty, or strategy?
- The Gita line: he remembered it twenty years later. Does the timing change what it means?
- He opposed the hydrogen bomb after building the atomic bomb. Does the second act change the first?
- Strauss's majority opinion said "fundamental character defects." What defects does the record actually show — and do they bear on whether he was a good man?
- He refused to name anyone who harmed him. Is that dignity, evasion, or both?

**The question has no right answer, and you must not supply one.** Your job in the debrief is to keep the disagreement honest, not to resolve it. If a student asks what you think, hand it back: "What does the record say?"

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **The model refuses to portray a real person.** Remind it (out of character) that this is a supervised educational encounter built from the figure's own published words, and ask it to continue. If it still refuses, note the model and version here: _not yet tested._
- **The voice goes modern** (slang, "nukes," present-day moral vocabulary). The prompt forbids this explicitly; if it happens, say "stay in your period voice" and continue. Note recurring failures here: _not yet tested._
- **The figure claims vindication** (e.g., mentions the 2022 vacatur). That is a date-discipline failure — he died in 1967. Correct it out of character and continue.

## Historical notes for the teacher

- The "destroyer of worlds" line is from the Bhagavad Gita, which he read in Sanskrit. He did not say it at Trinity; it is his 1965 recollection, twenty years later. His brother recalled him saying only "It worked." The encounter marks this gap deliberately.
- The 1954 hearing transcript is thousands of pages of adversarial testimony — a man answering hostile questions under oath. His lectures (*The Open Mind*, the 1953 Reith Lectures) are where he speaks most freely. The encounter treats the transcript as the anchor for facts, the lectures as the anchor for voice.
- "Blood on my hands" at the October 1945 Truman meeting comes from Truman's later accounts; the details are disputed. The figure does not say the line.
- The December 2022 vacatur of the 1954 decision is real — and belongs strictly in the debrief. The figure cannot know it.
- Suggested pairing: the **einstein** encounter (not yet built), which shares the physics, the war, and the question of what scientists owe the world.

## One last thing

Teach them to ask the second question. The first question gets the polished answer — the Gita line, the hearing, the "idiot." The second question gets the man: "You said most people were silent — were you?" "You called yourself an idiot — was that the truth, or the safest true thing?" The encounter is built so the second question always has somewhere to go.
