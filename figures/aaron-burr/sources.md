## What is Burr's own words vs. what is the record around him

- **Burr's own words:** the letters in the Davis *Memoirs*, the *Private Journal*, and the Kline edition — his correspondence with Theodosia, Van Ness, political allies, and his western associates; his courtroom arguments at Richmond as reported in the trial proceedings. The voice's diction, cadence, aphoristic style, and courtesy come from these.
- **Around him, not his:** the duel record (Hamilton's pre-duel statement, the seconds' accounts, Dr. Cooper's letter as printed in the *Albany Register*), the prosecution's case at Richmond (Wilkinson's testimony, the doctored cipher letter), Jefferson's public condemnation, and the press of the period. The prompt uses these for facts Burr could state, but never lets the voice claim knowledge it doesn't have — e.g., it never quotes Hamilton's private letters, only their public effect.
- **Notably thin in Burr's own corpus:** he wrote almost nothing about the duel itself — that silence is scripted as a boundary, not filled. His western letters are guarded and often coded; the prompt does not decode them for him.

## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — say which.

- **The meeting itself (a single sitting, one conversation covering his whole life)** — pure dramatic device. No such interview occurred.
- **The setting: Port Richmond, Staten Island, spring 1836** — Burr did die at Port Richmond on September 14, 1836, paralyzed by an 1834 stroke. Placing the encounter there in the months before is historically plausible, not documented. No record exists of his conversations in that period.
- **Compression of a lifetime into an hour** — the encounter has him speak on events thirty years apart as present-tense memory. Dramatic compression.
- **The scripted duel answer's phrasing** — the boundaries it marks (what is certain vs. disputed) are documented; the sentences themselves are authored. The reply "Contemptible, if true" is Burr's recorded response to the throw-away-shot story.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Written before the figure ships, even the ones that don't matter.

- **Who fired first at Weehawken?** Genuinely unresolved. The only witnesses were the two seconds, who turned their backs to preserve deniability and later disagreed in their accounts. The prompt does not resolve it and forbids the figure from resolving it.
- **Did Hamilton "throw away his shot"?** Hamilton's own pre-duel "Statement on Impending Duel" says he resolved to "reserve and throw away my first fire." Whether he did so on the field — or whether his pistol discharged accidentally when he was hit — is disputed. Scholars also debate how much of the statement was sincere and how much was written to ruin Burr posthumously if Hamilton were killed. The encounter presents Hamilton's statement and Burr's recorded reply ("Contemptible, if true") and leaves the question open.
- **What was the western expedition for?** Never established. Burr's version: land settlement, and — if war with Spain came, as was widely expected — a strike at Spanish possessions. Jefferson's version: seizing New Orleans and severing the western country from the Union. Wilkinson, the government's star witness, doctored the cipher letter he carried to Jefferson, and Wilkinson himself was later shown to have been on Spain's payroll. The acquittal was evidentiary (no two witnesses to an overt act; Burr was ~100 miles from Blennerhassett Island on December 10, 1806), not a finding about his intentions.
- **Did Burr intrigue for the presidency during the 1800 tie?** Disputed. Burr maintained he made no efforts on his own behalf and would not campaign against the head of his own ticket; Jefferson and his partisans believed otherwise. The 1911 *Britannica* notes he "would make no efforts to obtain votes in his own favour." The voice states Burr's position; the teacher should know the dispute.
- **Was Burr's reputation as an unprincipled schemer fair?** That is the question the encounter exists to put to the student. Modern scholarship is divided: one major biography (Isenberg, *Fallen Founder*, 2007) argues he was the most maligned founder; others hold the traditional view. The prompt takes no position.
- **Hamilton's "Catiline of America" remark** (private letter to Oliver Wolcott, 1800–1801) is Hamilton's, not Burr's, and is not in Burr's corpus. The voice refers only to Hamilton's known, undisguised opposition — never quotes his private letters.
- **Aphorisms attributed to Burr** ("never do today what you can do tomorrow," "law is whatever is boldly asserted and plausibly maintained," etc.) come down through the Davis *Memoirs* and later collections. The prompt uses his aphoristic *cadence* but puts no long verbatim quotation in his mouth that isn't traceable.
- **The second marriage** (to the widow Eliza Jumel, in the 1830s; ended in divorce) is omitted from the encounter as non-load-bearing; it belongs to classroom Q&A if raised, not to the scripted material.
