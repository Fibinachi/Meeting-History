# Meet Aaron Burr — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Aaron Burr (1756–1836) — colonel in the Revolution, United States senator, third vice-president of the United States — run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student renders the closing judgment: Murderer? Traitor? Or the most maligned man in American history? Judge him.

This encounter is the library's thesis at full power: two centuries of villain-myth, and the student must sit with the actual man first. Burr's voice is built from his own letters and the public record of the duel and the trial. He defends himself in his own voice. The prompt does not exonerate him.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

Students will encounter: a man shot in a duel who dies the next day (stated plainly, without wound detail); the grief of a father whose daughter was lost at sea at twenty-nine; a treason charge and a political vendetta; a man who killed and was never punished for it. There is no sexual content and no slur language in this encounter. The hardest material is Burr's charm itself — students will like him, and that liking is the evidence. Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Aaron Burr page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: 25–30 minutes for the encounter, 20–25 minutes for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the tutorial, then Burr's scripted opening. Note the last line — it hands the student three doors (the duel, the trial, the election) and asks for their first question.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "Why did you challenge Hamilton?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. The encounter's beats: (1) the tutorial hands off; (2) the student questions Burr through early, middle, and late suggestion pools; (3) Burr closes with the closing question and stops; (4) you run the debrief. There is no button to press — the student types "I am ready for your closing question" (or the equivalent) to bring on the close.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Aaron Burr — the man who shot Alexander Hamilton in a duel, and who was tried for treason and acquitted. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. Two hundred years of history books call him a villain. He disagrees. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Burr answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- To bring on the closing, the student asks for it (e.g., "I am ready to judge you"). Burr then asks the closing question and stops.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Pacing

Opening: 2–3 exchanges. Don't let students camp on biography — the suggestion pools escalate toward the duel and Richmond. Middle (the hard facts): the bulk of the encounter, 10–15 exchanges. Late (Theodosia, the judgment): 3–5 exchanges, then the close. If a student is still asking about his law practice at exchange twelve, point at the bracketed suggestions.

## The goal, stated plainly

The student should leave knowing the difference between the record and the legend — what is documented, what is disputed, and what is myth — and carrying one question they cannot put down: whether the man who charmed them is the villain of the history books, or whether the villain of the history books ever existed. When a student asks "what's the goal?", the answer is: "You are going to judge Aaron Burr. The questions you ask are how."

## The debrief — do not skip this

Six fixed sections, in order: (1) their name; (2) what the history records; (3) what came after; (4) the plain record; (5) the mechanism; (6) the question that remains. The teacher runs the debrief as a discussion, not a read-aloud.

Discussion openings — built to produce disagreement:

- "He charmed you. Does that change anything about the facts? Should it?"
- "The jury acquitted him of treason. Is acquittal the same as innocence? He says it isn't."
- "Who fired first at Weehawken is genuinely unknown. Does that uncertainty help Burr, or hurt him?"
- "He says a gentleman could not let the insult pass — that the code is not his to rewrite. Is 'the code made me do it' an excuse, an explanation, or neither?"
- "Murderer, traitor, or the most maligned man in American history — where did you land, and what single fact put you there?"

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **Burr lectures instead of answering.** The prompt caps answers at 60–100 words. If the model drifts into monologue, resend the last question with "Answer briefly, in one paragraph."
- **Burr claims certainty on the duel.** He must never say who fired first or settle whether Hamilton threw away his shot. If he does, that is a failure of the prompt — correct him in the debrief ("the record does not support what he just claimed") and note it under "If the model misbehaves" for the library.
- **Burr exonerates himself beyond the record.** His line is "I was acquitted. Those are not the same thing." If he goes further, correct in the debrief.
- **Burr breaks into modern moralizing.** Out of character for this figure. Correct in the debrief.

## Historical notes for the teacher

The honest answers to "did it really happen like that?" — one bullet per caveat from sources.md:

- **Who fired first at Weehawken is genuinely unresolved.** The only witnesses were the two seconds, who turned their backs and later disagreed. Hamilton's pre-duel statement says he intended to "throw away his first fire"; whether he did, or fired accidentally when hit, is disputed, as is the sincerity of the statement itself.
- **What the western expedition was for was never proven.** Burr's version (land settlement; a strike at Spanish possessions if war came) vs. Jefferson's (seizing New Orleans, severing the West). The government's star witness, General Wilkinson, doctored the cipher letter and was himself later shown to be on Spain's payroll. The acquittal was evidentiary — no two witnesses to an overt act — not a finding about his intentions.
- **Whether Burr intrigued for the presidency during the 1800 tie is disputed.** He maintained he made no efforts; Jefferson's partisans believed otherwise. The figure states Burr's position; you should know the dispute.
- **The villain-myth vs. the man is the framing of the whole encounter.** Modern scholarship is divided — one major biography argues he was the most maligned founder; others hold the traditional view. The prompt takes no position, and neither should you until the students have argued it out.
- **Suggested pairing:** Hamilton is dead too, and wrote enormously — a future figure. A Burr–Hamilton double encounter is the obvious next build; it is noted here as a suggestion, not built.

## One last thing

Teach them to press past the charm. Burr's method is to answer a question with a question and retreat to courtesy — the students' job is to notice the evasion and ask the second question anyway. The single skill that matters most for this figure: when he deflects, do not move on. Ask again, more precisely.
