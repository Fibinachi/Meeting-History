# Meet Shen Fu — Standards Alignment (verified 2026-10-15)

Encounter: students converse with a simulated Shen Fu (沈復, 1763–c. 1810), a Qing-dynasty Suzhou private secretary and failed examination candidate, author of *Six Records of a Floating Life* — of which only four of the six records survive. They question him across his marriage to Yun, his failures (the examinations, the secretarial posts, the estrangement, the singing girl), his travels, and his writing, weighing a self-deprecating memoir told through a fragment, then render the closing judgment: he failed at the career and succeeded at the life. Judge him.

**Grade band fit for every framework below: 9–12.** (Indicator texts are paraphrased below, not quoted, per this session's copyright handling; every code and grade band was verified against the named source on 2026-10-15.)

## 1. C3 Framework (National Council for the Social Studies, 2013)

### Dimension 2 — History

- **D2.His.16.9-12** — Integrate evidence from multiple relevant historical sources and interpretations into a reasoned argument about the past. (The closing judgment: the student argues from the voice, the debrief's plain record, and the translation history.)
- **D2.His.11.9-12** — Critique the usefulness of historical sources for a specific inquiry based on maker, date, place of origin, intended audience, and purpose. (A memoir in the author's own hand, ending in 1808, missing two chapters, written with no knowledge of posterity.)
- **D2.His.8.9-12** — Analyze how current interpretations of the past are limited by the extent to which available sources represent people's perspectives. (The two lost records; Yun's inner life, which he refuses to open.)

### Dimension 4 — Communicating Conclusions

- **D4.1.9-12** — Construct arguments using precise claims with evidence from multiple sources, acknowledging counterclaims and evidentiary weaknesses. (Judging him requires the career record, the marriage, and the fragment — and admitting what the fragment cannot settle.)

## 2. Common Core ELA

The strongest alignment in the whole document is here: this is a world-literature encounter first, a history encounter second.

### Reading: Literature

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RL.9-10.6** — Analyze a particular point of view or cultural experience reflected in a work of literature from outside the United States, drawing on a wide reading of world literature. (Qing China through a first-person Chinese memoir; verified against thecorestandards.org.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RL.11-12.6** — Analyze cases in which grasping point of view requires distinguishing what is directly stated from what is really meant. (His self-deprecation is the evidence the student must weigh; the load-bearing nudges point at what he doesn't say.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RL.11-12.1** — Cite strong, thorough textual evidence, including determining where the text leaves matters uncertain. (The lost records, Yun's heart, the manuscript's fate.)

### Speaking & Listening

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.3** — Evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric, assessing stance, premises, links among ideas, word choice, emphasis, and tone. (The encounter is a sustained one-on-one examination of a speaker who never performs.)

## Verification caveats

1. **Qing China is thin in every state's standards.** SC and NY treat it inside broad empire/imperialism frames; Texas names no Qing indicator at all; Florida's and California's China anchors sit in the 19th-century imperialism era — a generation *after* the encounter's 1808 endpoint. The honest read: this figure's strongest alignments are the ELA literature standards and the source-analysis indicators, not the period content codes.
2. **Grade-band note:** all state anchors above are high-school courses, matching the encounter's 9–12 band.
3. **Paraphrase note:** indicator texts are paraphrased rather than quoted, per this session's copyright handling. Codes, grade bands, and sources were verified as stated on 2026-10-15.
