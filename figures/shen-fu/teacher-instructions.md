# Meet Shen Fu — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Shen Fu (沈復, 1763–c. 1810), a private secretary of Qing-dynasty Suzhou, China, run through DeepSeek. Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student renders the closing judgment, in plain words: **he failed at the career and succeeded at the life. Judge him.**

Shen Fu is the gentlest figure in the library, and the most ordinary — no office, no war, no cause, no empire. His memoir, *Six Records of a Floating Life*, is about a marriage, some flowers, some insects, and a string of honest failures. That ordinariness is map-correcting: most students' mental map of "history worth knowing" has no room for a man like this. The encounter asks them to make room — and then to judge what they find there.

One honesty to carry into the room with you: only four of the six records survive. The book's title promises six; the world kept four. Say so out loud before you begin. The students should know they are meeting a man through a fragment — and that the fragment is enough.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

Students will encounter: the death of a spouse from a wasting illness, and grief spoken of plainly; sustained poverty (a couple selling their possessions, a wife selling her hair); references to concubinage and a singing girl whose purchase falls through; a years-long estrangement between a father and son. There is no violence, no graphic content, and nothing sexual is described. The tone throughout is tender, not harrowing — but grief is grief, and a student who has lost someone may feel it. Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open DeepSeek in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the opening carefully — note that he introduces himself fully (no hidden identity here; the discovery is the quality of the life, not who he is) and points the student at Yun first.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "Tell me about Yun." Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. The beats: 2–3 exchanges on Yun and the marriage (the light); 6–8 on the failures — the money, the father, the singing girl, her death (the shadow); 3–4 on the travels and the writing; then tell him you are ready, and he asks the closing question. The debrief follows.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Shen Fu — a man from China, two hundred years ago, who failed at everything his world told him mattered, and wrote down the life he actually lived. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. At the end you'll be asked to judge him: he failed at the career and succeeded at the life. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

Then add, plainly: "Only four of his six chapters survive. You're meeting him through a fragment." The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Shen Fu answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- When the student is ready — or when you call time — they tell him so, and he asks the closing question. That ends the encounter; the debrief begins.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Pacing

Opening (Yun, the marriage): 2–3 exchanges. Don't let students camp here — the light is lovely, but the judgment needs the shadow. Middle (failures, the rift, the singing girl, her death): the bulk of the encounter, 6–8 exchanges. Late (travels, the writing, the missing records): 3–4 exchanges, then the closing. Total encounter time: ~25 minutes. If a student rushes to the closing, let them — the debrief will do the rest.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer: "You are going to meet a man who lost the world's game and kept the things that mattered. At the end you have to judge him: he failed at the career and succeeded at the life. Your judgment is the assignment."

## The debrief — do not skip this

Six sections, in fixed order: (1) his name — Shen Fu (沈復, 1763–c. 1810), courtesy name Sanbai, of Suzhou, Qing China; (2) what the history records — the failed examinations, the secretarial work, the 1780 marriage, twenty-three years, Yun's death in 1803, the four surviving records; (3) what came after — the bookstall discovery, the 1877 printing, the forged "complete" chapters of the 1930s, the four English translations, Lin Yutang's praise, the disputed death date; (4) the plain record — what he did, without euphemism and without his vocabulary: no office, no money, the estrangement and reconciliation, the singing girl, the wife treated as a friend; (5) the mechanism — the gentleness was the device, the silences were marked not solved, his ignorance of his fame is date discipline; (6) the question that remains — he failed at the career and succeeded at the life; judge him; it does not close when they leave the room.

Discussion openings — built to produce disagreement:

- He says he failed at everything the world counts. Is he right that the life succeeded — or is that the consolation of a man who had no choice?
- He calls Yun his friend, in a world where husbands didn't say that of their wives. Does that make him remarkable, or just honest?
- The singing girl: Yun wanted it, he accepted it, and the disappointment hastened Yun's decline. Who — if anyone — is at fault?
- Four chapters survive of six. Does it matter that we only have part of the story — could the lost chapters change your judgment?
- He never blames anyone — not the officials, not his father, not the girl's mother. Is that grace, or is it a refusal to see clearly?

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this. The figure also has in-character stall lines — "Ask me about the toad" will restart almost anyone.
- **The model turns him into a sage.** This is the most likely failure mode for this figure: the model wants to make him profound — to turn the flower arrangements into philosophy, the failures into lessons. If he starts dispensing wisdom or life advice, stop the conversation and re-paste the prompt; do not try to repair it mid-stream. He remembers; he does not teach.
- **The model invents the lost chapters.** If asked about the fifth or sixth record, he must say he never wrote them (or that they are lost) — never describe their contents. If he starts describing them, that is a corpus break. Stop, re-paste, continue.
- **The model moralizes in modern terms.** If he ever uses words like trauma, agency, or feminism about his own life, that is a voice break. Stop, re-paste, continue.
- **The model claims vindication.** If he ever suggests he knew the book would survive or be famous, that is a date-discipline break. Stop, re-paste, continue.

## Historical notes for the teacher

The honest answers to "did it really happen like that?" — one bullet per caveat from sources.md:

- **Only four of six chapters survive.** Say this to the class before you begin. The lost chapters ("A History of Life at Zhongshan," "The Way of Living") are known by title only, from the original table of contents. "Complete" six-chapter versions that surfaced in the 1930s were declared forgeries by scholars.
- **His death date is uncertain.** The traditional date is about 1810; some modern sources say "after 1825," but that rests on the forged chapters. The encounter is set in 1808 and his knowledge ends there, so the dispute never touches the voice.
- **First printed 1877.** Found at a secondhand bookstall by Yang Yin, passed to Wang Tao, printed in Shanghai — an instant classic. (One scholarly encyclopedia says 1870; the difference is minor and doesn't affect the encounter.)
- **The marriage:** 1780, twenty-three years, Yun died 1803. He was a private secretary (幕僚) to officials, never passed the examinations, never held office.
- **The Hanyuan episode** — Yun trying to buy a singing girl's freedom for his sake — is in "Sorrow," not invented. So is Yun cutting and selling her hair when they were destitute, and Yun dressing as a man for festival outings.
- **The children** — a son and a daughter — are mentioned in the chapters but barely; the encounter does not invent their fates, and neither should you.
- **The translation used** is Leonard Pratt and Chiang Su-hui's (Viking 1983; Penguin Classics). Lin Yutang's 1936 version, which made the book famous in English, renders some passages differently; if a student reads a different translation, small wording differences are expected.

## One last thing

Teach them to ask about the failures, not just the love story. Students will want to stay with Yun — she is the light of the encounter, and the figure himself points them at her first. But the judgment needs the whole man: the lost money, the estranged father, the singing girl, the coffin bought on charity. The gentleness is the evidence, and it only counts if they've seen what it cost. When a student asks "what does it all mean?" — hand them back to what happened. The figure remembers; the student judges.
