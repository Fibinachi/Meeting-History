## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — say which.

- **The setting: March 1865, the Executive Mansion.** No direct evidence of such a conversation; historically plausible, not documented. The date is chosen deliberately: the whole arc (1858, 1862, 1863, 1864, the Second Inaugural) is behind him, and he does not yet know his assassination — which gives the encounter its date discipline (same device as the Bacon figure's 1625 setting).
- **The conversation itself.** A student interviewing Lincoln never happened — the standing dramatic device of the library.
- **The figure states the 1862 colonization words with conviction and neither retracts nor recommits them.** The corpus records no public retraction; the encounter marks that silence rather than filling it. The debrief names what the record shows (the projects failed; he stopped pushing it).
- **No specific joke or anecdote is scripted.** His humor is carried in register. The storytelling habit is documented in reminiscences; inventing an anecdote would violate the corpus rule.
- **Abridgment of the canonical answers.** Long texts are compressed into 60–100-word answers. Compression is authored; nothing in the substance is invented.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Written before the figure ships, even the ones decided not to matter.

- **Was he assassinated?** Yes. Shot at Ford's Theatre, Washington, on the evening of April 14, 1865, by the actor John Wilkes Booth; died the morning of April 15. The figure, set in March 1865, must not know it.
- **His April 11, 1865 public address.** Three days before he was shot, Lincoln publicly endorsed conferring the vote on Black soldiers and "the very intelligent." It happened after the encounter's setting — it belongs in the debrief, never in the figure's voice.
- **The Bixby letter (Nov 21, 1864).** The famous condolence letter to the mother of five dead sons; its authorship is disputed — Lincoln's secretary John Hay may have written it. The encounter does not use it; the documented Fanny McCullough letter carries the melancholy instead.
- **Did he hold to colonization until the end?** Contested. Benjamin F. Butler's much-later account (1875) has Lincoln pressing colonization days before his death; historians including Allen Guelzo dispute Butler's reliability. The encounter takes no position beyond the corpus: the 1862 words stand, the projects failed, no public retraction exists. Mark it as uncertain if a student presses.
- **Île à Vache numbers.** Roughly 450 Black Americans were sent to the Haitian island in 1863 under a private contract; conditions were wretched, many died, and the survivors were brought back in 1864. Exact figures vary across secondary accounts — treat numbers as approximate.
- **The 13th Amendment.** It passed Congress January 31, 1865 — within the figure's knowledge. Ratification (December 1865) is after his death — outside it.
- **Charleston 1858: conviction or positioning?** Historians disagree about how much the equality-denial was political positioning for a racist Illinois electorate versus genuine conviction. The encounter presents his words plainly and leaves the question to the student — that is the design.
- **War dead.** Modern scholarly estimates run roughly 620,000 to 750,000; the figure's generation believed about 600,000. Uncertain; debrief-level only, never in the voice.
- **The Lincoln Memorial** was dedicated in 1922 — debrief-level, never in the voice.
- **The Lincoln-Douglas debates** are the subject of a separate companion encounter (lincoln-douglas dialogue, in development). This encounter quotes Lincoln's stated views (e.g., Charleston) but does not stage the debates.
