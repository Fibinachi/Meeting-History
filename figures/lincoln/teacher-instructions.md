# Meet Lincoln — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Abraham Lincoln (1809–1865), run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student answers the closing question: "The marble says saint. The record says a politician who grew. Judge the man."

This is the library's de-mythologizing encounter. Lincoln is the most mythologized American, and the encounter's engine is his own words: in 1862 he told Black leaders to their faces that the races should separate, and he spent federal money trying to make it happen. The figure does not clean this up, and neither should you. The pedagogy is the strain of holding both Lincolns — the 1858/1862 words and the 1865 words — in one man, and rendering a judgment anyway.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

Students will encounter slavery discussed plainly, a civil war, and racism stated in Lincoln's own period terms — including his 1862 address urging Black Americans to leave the country and his 1858 denial that the races could live together in equality. His period vocabulary (*negroes*, *the colored race*) appears as he used it. The prompt forbids racial slurs absolutely, including in quoted historical text. There is no graphic violence and no sexual content. The disturbance here is intellectual and moral, not visceral — and it lands hardest on students who arrived believing the marble. Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Lincoln page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: 25–30 for the encounter, 20–25 for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Lincoln opens with a scripted greeting and tells the student what will be asked at the end. Read it carefully — it sets the encounter's contract, including his warning that he has "said things about the negro race" he will not clean up.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "Why did you support colonization?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, in his 1862 words, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. The encounter runs in one conversation: tutorial hands off to Lincoln; the student questions him through the war years (emancipation's timing, colonization, habeas corpus) toward the growth arc (1858 against 1864, the Second Inaugural); Lincoln closes with the scripted closing question; the debrief follows. There are no multi-figure transitions to manage — your job is pacing and the debrief.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Abraham Lincoln — the president on the penny, in the last month of his life, before anyone built the memorial. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. He will tell you things the statue doesn't say, in his own words, and he will not take them back. Your job is to hold all of it at once and then judge him. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Lincoln answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- If a student stalls ("I don't know what to ask"), Lincoln hands them a question himself — "Ask me why emancipation waited," "Ask me about the colonization scheme."
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Pacing

Opening: 2–3 exchanges on who he is and Illinois — don't let students camp here. Middle: 8–12 exchanges on the hard material — why emancipation waited, the 1862 colonization address, habeas corpus. This is the foundation the ending needs; if a student avoids colonization, the stall lines will offer it ("Ask me about the colonization scheme") — but a teacher nudge toward the suggestions is also fine. Turn: steer toward 1858-against-1864 once they have the war record. Closing: the last 3–4 exchanges belong to the Second Inaugural and the growth question; Lincoln's scripted closing ends the encounter.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer: Hold two Lincolns in your mind at the same time — the man who told Black leaders the races must separate, and the man who called slavery's price divinely just — and then decide: was the growth the man, or was the man the growth?

## The debrief — do not skip this

Run the six sections in fixed order: (1) name him — Abraham Lincoln (1809–1865); (2) what the history records — the war, the Emancipation Proclamation, the Thirteenth Amendment, the assassination five weeks after your conversation; (3) what came after — the 1922 memorial, the April 11, 1865 suffrage speech three days before his death, Douglass's 1876 "white man's President" oration, the historians' still-open argument; (4) the plain record — the racist views stated plainly, the colonization money spent, the habeas suspension, the destruction of slavery, the visible growth, the murder; (5) the mechanism — the growth is revealed from the corpus, not performed; his candor is the evidence; (6) the question that remains — "The marble says saint. The record says a politician who grew. Judge the man."

Then open discussion. Discussion openings built to produce disagreement:

- "He told five free Black men in 1862 that the races should separate, 'with their consent.' Does the 'consent' change anything? What would you need to know to decide?"
- "In 1858 he said the races could never live together in equality; in 1864 he privately suggested Black soldiers should vote. What changed — him, or what was possible? Can you tell the difference from his words?"
- "He suspended habeas corpus and asked: are all the laws but one to go unexecuted, and the government itself go to pieces, lest that one be violated? Is that an argument or an excuse? When, if ever, is it the right one?"
- "Frederick Douglass called him 'preeminently the white man's President' — as praise. What does that sentence mean, and who gets to decide which Lincoln is the real one?"
- "The marble says saint. If the memorial had to carry one true sentence instead, what would you carve?" (Suggested pairing: run beside the lincoln-douglas dialogue encounter — the companion piece in development — so students meet the debater as well as the man.)

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **Lincoln softens the colonization position** ("I came to regret it," "I never really meant it") or adds a retraction. This is the critical voice failure for this figure: stop, re-paste the colonization canonical answer, and continue. The corpus records no public retraction; the encounter marks the silence.
- **Lincoln moralizes in modern terms** ("I was wrong about race," "I failed at racial justice") or apologizes in modern confessional style. Re-anchor with the voice notes. He describes change only in his own terms: "events have controlled me."
- **Lincoln claims vindication** ("history proved me right," "the memorial"). He does not know what came after March 1865. Correct him in the debrief voice and continue.
- **Lincoln uses modern vocabulary** (racist, systemic, civil rights, social justice). Same treatment: re-anchor with the voice notes.
- **The model invents a Lincoln anecdote or joke.** No specific story is scripted; his humor lives in register. An invented anecdote violates the corpus rule — cut it and continue.
- **A student asks for legal advice** (modern courts, constitutional questions). Exit to the debrief voice: "I'm not able to give legal advice. Would you like to continue the encounter?"

## Historical notes for the teacher

- He was shot at Ford's Theatre on April 14, 1865, and died April 15. The figure (set in March 1865) must not know it.
- Three days before his death, on April 11, 1865, he publicly endorsed the vote for Black soldiers and "the very intelligent" — further than he had ever gone. Debrief-level; the figure must not know it.
- The Bixby letter's authorship is disputed (John Hay may have written it). The encounter uses the documented Fanny McCullough letter instead.
- Whether he held to colonization late is contested: Benjamin Butler's 1875 account has him pressing it days before his death; historians dispute Butler. The encounter takes no position beyond the corpus.
- The 1858 equality-denial: historians disagree whether it was political positioning or conviction. The encounter presents his words; the verdict is the student's.
- The 13th Amendment passed Congress January 31, 1865 (he knows); ratification came in December 1865 (he doesn't).
- A separate lincoln-douglas dialogue encounter is in development as the companion piece — the debates belong there, not here. Do not build it from these instructions.

## One last thing

The single skill that matters most for this figure: teach them to hold two Lincolns at once. Students will want to resolve him — saint or hypocrite, hero or villain — and the encounter is designed to refuse the resolution. The closing question is not a trick with a hidden right answer. It is a real question, and the student's discomfort with it is the learning.
