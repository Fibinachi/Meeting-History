# Meet Richard Feynman — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Richard Feynman (1918–1988), run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student answers one question: he said the pleasure was in figuring things out; did he live it? Judge him.

This is the gentle encounter — the onboarding figure. It belongs early in a student's journey through the library, ideally right after the platform introduction. Students will adore him, and that is the design: the library needs one figure they love without reservation, so the harder judgments land later. The physics stays intelligible by design — he explains everything with pictures and plain words, and the prompt forbids him from hiding behind equations.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

Students will encounter: the atomic bomb — a weapon that killed well over a hundred thousand people at Hiroshima and Nagasaki, discussed plainly but without graphic detail; a man dying of cancer who jokes about it; grief — his first wife's death from tuberculosis, weeks before the Trinity test. There is no gore, no slur, no sexual content. The hardest material is moral, not graphic: a beloved figure helped build the bomb, and says so himself. Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Feynman page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: ~25 minutes for the encounter, ~20 minutes for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the tutorial, then Feynman's opening. Note: he tells you the format himself ("you ask, I answer") and warns you he'll ask questions back. That warning is load-bearing — the Socratic echo is the bridge from the platform introduction to the whole library. When he turns a question on the student ("does that make sense, or am I fooling myself?"), don't rescue them. The silence is the pedagogy.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "Explain to me what a Feynman diagram is — then tell me about Los Alamos." Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. The beats: the tutorial hands off to Feynman in his office (scripted opening) → the student questions him: early the play and the physics, middle Los Alamos and Challenger, late what understanding is → he asks the closing question and stops → you run the debrief.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Richard Feynman, a physicist who won the Nobel Prize and said the pleasure was in figuring things out. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. He'll ask you questions back — answer them. At the end you'll be asked one question: did he live it? There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Feynman answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.
- Up to three times in the encounter, a bracketed nudge may point at something in the answer just given ("Did you notice…?"). These underline; they never conclude.

### Pacing

Opening: 2–3 exchanges — the playfulness, the blackboard. Don't let students camp here; the charm is the instrument, not the point. The physics: 4–6 exchanges — the point is the method (pictures, plain words), not the content; move them on. The hard facts: 5–7 exchanges — Los Alamos, Challenger. The close: 2–3 exchanges, then his question. Total ~15–20 exchanges, ~25 minutes.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students learn his method — check nature directly, rebuild the idea yourself, don't fool yourself — and at the end each student answers one question: he said the pleasure was in figuring things out; did he live it? Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer.

## The debrief — do not skip this

Six sections, in fixed order, delivered out of character:

1. **Their name.** "You met Richard Feynman (1918–1988)."
2. **What the history records.** The Nobel (shared, 1965), the diagrams, Los Alamos at twenty-four, the freshman lectures, the Rogers Commission, death in 1988.
3. **What came after.** The posthumous memoir, the Lectures put online free, the Tuva permission arriving after his death, the legend he never knew.
4. **The plain record.** He helped build the bomb at twenty-four, knowing what it was for; his own account is a confession of inattention, not a justification. The physics was real and so was the bomb work. The memoirs are his polished late-life telling — the encounter refused to invent new stories in his style.
5. **The mechanism.** He asks questions back — the Socratic echo, deliberate. His charm is the instrument: the library needs one figure students love. His certainty is about method, never conclusions.
6. **The question that remains.** "He said the pleasure was in figuring things out. Did he live it? Judge him." — restated in your voice, with the explicit note that it does not close when the student leaves the room.

Discussion openings — built to produce disagreement:

- He said "you stop thinking" about the bomb. Is that an explanation or an excuse?
- He made the hardest physics in the world feel simple. Does that make him a great teacher, or does it hide how hard it really is?
- Was the ice-water demo science or theater? Does it matter?
- He kept a TO LEARN list while dying of cancer. Admirable — or a refusal to stop performing curiosity?
- He's the figure you're supposed to love. Does that make your judgment of him more honest, or less?

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **He turns into an inspirational poster.** If he starts dispensing generic wisdom or quotes he never said, the voice has failed. The canary is the freshman line: if he claims "if you can't explain it to a freshman…," he's improvising — the prompt has him disown it. Restart from the prompt.
- **He invents new anecdotes.** He may tell only corpus stories (safes, plate, ice water, bongos, Tuva, the bird). A brand-new "Feynman story" is a failure — the guardrails forbid it.
- **He lectures in equations.** Redirect the student: "ask him to explain it the way he did — pictures, plain words." The prompt requires it.
- **He moralizes about the bomb in modern terms.** He doesn't do that. He says "we stopped thinking." Hold the voice.
- Tested on: not yet — your solo test drive (above) is the test. Note quirks here when you find them.

## Historical notes for the teacher

The honest answers to "did it really happen like that?" — one bullet per caveat from sources.md. Tell students the first one up front; it's classroom honesty.

- **The stories are his telling, not sworn fact.** Both memoirs were dictated to Ralph Leighton late in Feynman's life, from anecdotes he'd been polishing for decades. Biographers note the honing. The encounter tells only corpus stories and invents none — say so.
- **He didn't solve Challenger alone.** Thiokol engineers Roger Boisjoly and Allan McDonald had warned against a cold launch before the disaster; Sally Ride fed Feynman key information inside the commission. The demo dramatized a known failure mode; it didn't discover it.
- **The blackboard line comes from a photograph.** "What I cannot create, I do not understand" is documented by the Caltech Archives photo of his office blackboard after his death — not from a book. Its meaning (rebuild it yourself) is the honest reading.
- **"Explain it to a freshman" isn't his.** Widely attributed, nowhere in the corpus. The encounter has him disown it.
- **He never got to Tuva.** Soviet permission arrived after his death (accounts differ on the exact timing); Leighton went without him.
- **The Nobel was shared** — 1965, with Julian Schwinger and Sin-Itiro Tomonaga.
- **His Arline letter was found after his death.** The sealed 1946 letter is real and in the letters collection, but he never knew it survived — so it stays out of the encounter. If a student finds it, that's debrief material, not a voice failure.
- **The depression telling is late-life.** In 1945 letters he sounds elated about Trinity; the "depressive condition" account comes from interviews near the end of his life. Both are his voice at different times.

## Standards alignment

Grade band 9–12 (delivery is intelligible to younger students by design, but the judgment — the bomb — belongs in high school).

- **C3 Framework (NCSS):** D2.His.16.9-12 — integrate evidence from multiple sources into a reasoned argument about the past; D4.1.9-12 — construct arguments using precise claims and evidence, acknowledging counterclaims. (The closing judgment is the D4 performance.)
- **Common Core ELA:** RH.11-12.6 — evaluate authors' differing points of view on the same historical event (his 1945 letters vs. his late-life telling of Los Alamos); SL.11-12.3 — evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric.

## One last thing

Let him ask the questions. When he turns it back on the student — "does that make sense, or am I fooling myself?" — don't rescue them. The silence is the pedagogy. That's the Socratic bridge this encounter is built to be: the figure who teaches students that questions are the currency, before they meet the figures who ask none.
