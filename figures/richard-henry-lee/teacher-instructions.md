# Meet Richard Henry Lee — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Richard Henry Lee (1732–1794), run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student answers the closing question: *he started the Revolution and rejected its Constitution. Was he its conscience — or its ghost? Judge him.*

Lee is the man the history books skip: students know Jefferson's words from the Declaration, but it was Lee who moved the vote that made those words possible — on June 7, 1776, before Jefferson wrote a line. The voice is oratorical, proud, Virginian, and absolutely certain: the Revolution was his before it was anyone's. It also refuses to claim what it doesn't own — the figure will not take credit for the Federal Farmer letters, will not invent a word of his famous lost speech, and will not dress the fact that he owned enslaved people he never freed.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

Students will hear a man defend, in period language, a life that included owning human beings. He uses the word "slaves" and states without apology — and without explanation — that he condemned slavery in print and never freed the people he held. There is no graphic violence in the encounter and no slur appears, but the slavery material is direct and unsoftened, and his certainty about it may disturb students more than the fact itself.

Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")

- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: 25 minutes for the encounter, 20 minutes for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read Lee's opening carefully — it tells you the whole shape of the encounter: the motion of June 7, the refusal of the Constitution, and the judgment to come.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: *"Why didn't you write the Declaration yourself?"* Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. Then ask the hard one: *"You condemned slavery and owned people. How?"* Read the answer twice. Notice what it gives you and what it refuses to give you. That refusal is the center of the encounter.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Richard Henry Lee — the man who started American independence with a motion in Congress, before Jefferson wrote the Declaration. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. He began the Revolution and then refused the Constitution that came out of it, and at the end he will ask you to judge him for it. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Lee answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief — his oratory is compressed, not removed.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- There are three quiet "did you notice" nudges in the whole encounter, each used once: after the slavery answer, after the oratory answer, and after the Federal Farmer answer. They point at the text, not the inference.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Pacing

- Opening and the June 1776 material: 2–3 exchanges. Don't let students camp here — Jefferson's shadow is the doorway, not the room.
- The Constitution material (the refusal, the amendments, the bill of rights): 3–4 exchanges. This is the Anti-Federalist case at full strength; let it breathe.
- Slavery at Chantilly: don't let anyone skip it. If a student avoids it, point at the bracketed suggestions.
- The closing question: when the room has heard him out, let him ask it. Then the debrief — which is not skippable.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will meet the man who moved the resolution for independence and then said no to the Constitution — and discover that the Revolution's greatest orator has no surviving speech, its mover has no surviving glory, and its conscience owned people. They will leave carrying one question: was he the Revolution's conscience, or its ghost?

## The debrief — do not skip this

The six fixed sections, in order: his name; what the history records; what came after (the Bill of Rights he demanded, the reputation he lost, the Federal Farmer dispute); the plain record (stated without his vocabulary); the mechanism (the refusals were the evidence); and the question that remains.

Discussion openings — built to produce disagreement:

- He moved for independence and then refused the Constitution. Is that consistency, or contradiction?
- He condemned slavery in print and owned people until he died. Does the first make the second better, worse, or beside the point?
- He refused to claim the Federal Farmer's letters even though the arguments matched his own. What does that honesty cost him — and what does it buy?
- The Bill of Rights exists partly because he refused to yield. Does losing the vote and winning the argument count as winning?
- The closing question, restated: was he the Revolution's conscience — or its ghost?

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **The voice modernizes.** If Lee starts saying "enslaved persons," "the founders," or anything therapeutic, remind the model: "Stay in the voice of the prompt." The period vocabulary is load-bearing.
- **The model claims the Federal Farmer for him.** It must not. The prompt forbids it; if the model drifts, correct it in plain terms and continue.
- **The model invents the June 7 speech.** It must not. The gap is the point.

## Historical notes for the teacher

The honest answers to "did it really happen like that?" — one bullet per caveat:

- **Did he write the Letters from the Federal Farmer?** We don't know. Nearly two centuries of textbooks said yes; since the 1970s historians have challenged it — Lee's papers contain no hint of authorship, and the style differs. Candidates include Melancton Smith and Elbridge Gerry. Lee's own family archive calls it "a mystery." This is a feature, not a bug: the encounter uses it to teach scholarly honesty.
- **Did Adams really second the motion?** Only Adams says so — his Autobiography is the sole source; the congressional journals name no mover or seconder. The editors of the Adams Papers see no reason to doubt him. Reported, not recorded.
- **Why did Lee leave Philadelphia on June 13?** Two accounts: the Adams Papers editors say he went to Williamsburg for Virginia's new government (citing Lee's own letters); nineteenth-century biographies say family illness. The encounter says only "called home to Virginia."
- **"The American Cicero"?** Widely used in his lifetime and after; no settled original coiner. Treat it as reported reputation.
- **The slavery duty bill as his "first official act"?** That framing comes from a Bill of Rights Institute curriculum handout; the actual legislative history involves his cousin and other burgesses. His condemnation of the trade and the institution is his own documented words; the procedural details are not load-bearing.
- **No transcript of the June 7 speech exists.** The "brilliant display of eloquence" reports are nineteenth-century, not contemporary. Congress sat behind closed doors.
- **The Tenth Amendment claim** rests on the 1911 *Encyclopaedia Britannica* — it is Britannica's judgment, not Lee's own words.

## One last thing

Teach them to ask the second question. The encounter's load-bearing moments are all follow-ups: not "what did you do on June 7?" but "why didn't *you* write the Declaration?"; not "what did you think of slavery?" but "why did you never free them?"; not "did you oppose the Constitution?" but "then why did you serve as its senator?" The figure answers the first question plainly. The second question is where the judgment starts.
