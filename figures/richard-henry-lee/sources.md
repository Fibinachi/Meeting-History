## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — say which.

- The encounter's setting — Chantilly in the spring of 1794, weeks before Lee's death — is an authored frame. Lee lived at Chantilly until he died there June 19, 1794 (documented); the conversation itself is invention.
- "I lost the vote. I won the argument" is an authored distillation of Lee's conviction, not a quotation from his letters. It states his position; it does not invent a fact.
- "The speeches are smoke. The motion is stone." is authored phrasing for the honest device of marking the lost June 7 speech — the *gap* is documented, the *words* are the prompt's.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Write these before the figure ships, even the ones you decided don't matter.

- **The Federal Farmer authorship is DISPUTED.** For nearly two centuries the Letters from the Federal Farmer (1787–88) were attributed to Lee. Since the 1970s, historians (notably Gordon Wood) have challenged the attribution: Lee's papers contain no hint of authorship and the style differs from his. Alternatives proposed include Melancton Smith and (by John P. Kaminski) Elbridge Gerry. The Lee Family Digital Archive itself concludes "the identity of the Federal Farmer remains a mystery." The figure must never claim them. If a student presses, the teacher's honest answer: *we don't know who wrote them.*
- **Adams's seconding of the resolution is attested only through Adams's Autobiography.** The Journals of the Continental Congress name neither the mover nor the seconder; the Avalon/JCC note records "The motion was seconded by John Adams" on the strength of Adams's statement. The Adams Papers editors write there is "no reason to doubt his word." It is reported, not recorded — say so if asked.
- **Why Lee left Philadelphia on June 13, 1776 is told two ways.** The Adams Papers editors (citing Ballagh's *Letters*, 1:201, 203) say he went to Williamsburg for the formation of Virginia's new government; nineteenth-century biographies say sickness in his family. The prompt says only that he "was called home to Virginia" — the honest common ground.
- **No transcript of the June 7, 1776 speech exists.** Congress sat with closed doors. The "brilliant display of eloquence" reports come from nineteenth-century biographers, not contemporary record. The encounter's central honesty device is having Lee himself say so.
- **The "American Cicero" nickname is widely used; its original coiner is unsettled.** Treat it as reported reputation, which is how the figure treats it.
- **The "first official act" duty-bill framing comes from the Bill of Rights Institute curriculum.** The legislative history is messier: the 1767 duty bill's passage also involved Lee's cousin Henry Lee and Richard Bland's amendments. The figure does not adjudicate which Lee moved what.
- **The letters record no stated reason for Lee never freeing the people he held.** The figure offers none. The curriculum handout's "he could not afford to" is a secondary explanation the corpus does not confirm — it is not voiced.
- **The Tenth Amendment claim rests on the 1911 Britannica** ("in substantially the form in which it was adopted"). It is Britannica's judgment, cited as such in the debrief's sources, not as Lee's own words.
