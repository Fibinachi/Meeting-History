# Meet Richard Henry Lee — Standards Alignment (verified 2026-10-15)

Encounter: students cross-examine a simulated Richard Henry Lee (1732–1794) at Chantilly in the spring of 1794 — the Virginian who moved the June 7, 1776 resolution for independence (adopted July 2, announced by Jefferson's Declaration) and then refused the Constitution that independence made possible. They press him on the resolution, on why Jefferson wrote the Declaration instead of him, on his Anti-Federalist objections and the bill of rights he demanded as the price, on the slaves at Chantilly he condemned the trade for and never freed, and on the Letters from the Federal Farmer — which he refuses to claim. It closes with his question: *he started the Revolution and rejected its Constitution — was he its conscience, or its ghost? Judge him.*

**Grade band fit for every framework below: 9–12** (high school U.S. History / Government / ELA literacy). Note: most states' *content* anchors for the founding era sit in grades 7–8 (see caveats); the 9–12 weight comes from South Carolina's USHC placement and from skills standards — argument, source critique, deliberation — which are band-matched.

## 1. C3 Framework (National Council for the Social Studies, 2013)

Grade band: **9–12**. (Indicator texts verified against curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim; the official NCSS PDF was not directly retrievable in this session — see verification note at end.)

### Dimension 2 — Civics

- **D2.Civ.4.9-12** — Explain how the U.S. Constitution establishes a system of government that has powers, responsibilities, and limits that have changed over time and that are still contested. (The encounter's civic core: his objection that the Philadelphia plan traded "thirteen free governments for one consolidated one" with no bill of rights — the Constitution as a still-contested instrument, argued by the man who refused it.)

### Dimension 2 — History

- **D2.His.8.9-12** — Analyze how current interpretations of the past are limited by the extent to which available historical sources represent perspectives of people at the time. (No transcript of his famous June 7, 1776 speech survives — Congress sat with closed doors; his oratorical reputation rests on reports, not texts. The encounter's honesty device is having Lee himself say: "The speeches are smoke, sir. The motion is stone.")
- **D2.His.11.9-12** — Critique the usefulness of historical sources for a specific historical inquiry based on their maker, date, place of origin, intended audience, and purpose. (The Letters from the Federal Farmer — attributed to him for nearly two centuries, authorship now genuinely disputed; the figure refuses to claim them, and the student must weigh unattributed essays as evidence. Adams's retrospective Autobiography as a hostile secondary source.)

### Dimension 4 — Communicating Conclusions and Taking Informed Action

- **D4.1.9-12** — Construct arguments using precise and knowledgeable claims, with evidence from multiple sources, while acknowledging counterclaims and evidentiary weaknesses. (The ratification debate maps directly here: the student must construct Lee's Anti-Federalist case — his October 1787 letters to Samuel Adams and Edmund Randolph — and acknowledge where the Constitution's defenders answered it.)
- **D4.4.9-12** — Critique the use of claims and evidence in arguments for credibility. (His "residuum of human rights" claims against the record of the adopted Bill of Rights; the 1911 *Britannica* claim that his prejudices were "largely removed by its working in practice" — a secondary interpretation to be critiqued, not a fact.)

*Verified against: uk.everfi.com C3 curriculum guide; go.boarddocs.com AP Government curriculum document; cdn.fairfieldschools.org district C3 documents. Official document: College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework for Social Studies State Standards, NCSS, 2013 (socialstudies.org/c3).*

## 2. Common Core ELA — Literacy in History/Social Studies & Speaking/Listening

Grade band: **9–12**. (Verified against the official Common Core State Standards Initiative site, thecorestandards.org.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 9–10

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.6** — Compare the point of view of two or more authors for how they treat the same or similar topics, including which details they include and emphasize in their respective accounts. (Lee's October 1787 letters — "rights of conscience, the freedom of the press, and trial by jury are at mercy" — against the Federalists' defense of the same Constitution.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.8** — Assess the extent to which the reasoning and evidence in a text support the author's claims. (His claim "I lost the vote. I won the argument" — tested against the ratification of 1788 and the Bill of Rights of 1791.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.6** — Evaluate authors' differing points of view on the same historical event or issue by assessing the authors' claims, reasoning, and evidence. (The ratification debate as the canonical case: Lee's Anti-Federalist letters vs. Publius's Federalist numbers — the standard's exact use case, and the encounter's middle third.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.8** — Evaluate an author's premises, claims, and evidence by corroborating or challenging them with other information. (His demand for a bill of rights as "a necessary bottom to this new system," corroborated by the Bill of Rights' adoption in 1791 — and his Tenth Amendment claim, which rests on a 1911 *Britannica* judgment the debrief flags.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.3** — Evaluate various explanations for actions or events and determine which explanation best accords with textual evidence, acknowledging where the text leaves matters uncertain. (The Federal Farmer attribution; the two competing accounts of why he left Philadelphia on June 13, 1776 — the debrief marks both as genuinely unsettled.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.1** — Cite specific textual evidence to support analysis of primary and secondary sources, connecting insights gained from specific details to an understanding of the text as a whole. (The resolution of June 7, 1776, in his own hand — three sentences that unmade an empire.)

### Speaking & Listening, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.1** — Initiate and participate effectively in a range of collaborative discussions (one-on-one, in groups, and teacher-led) with diverse partners on grades 11–12 topics, texts, and issues, building on others' ideas and expressing their own clearly and persuasively. (The encounter *is* a sustained one-on-one cross-examination.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.3** — Evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric, assessing the stance, premises, links among ideas, word choice, points of emphasis, and tone used. (The encounter's whole method: the oratorical periods, the certainty at rest, and the three refusals — the Farmer, the lost speech, the reason for never freeing his slaves.)

### Writing, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.W.11-12.1** — Write arguments to support claims in an analysis of substantive topics or texts, using valid reasoning and relevant and sufficient evidence. (The closing question — conscience or ghost? — is designed as a post-encounter argument prompt, per the teacher instructions.)

## 3. South Carolina — Social Studies College- and Career-Ready Standards (2019), US History and the Constitution

Grade band: **9–12** (high school US History). South Carolina places the founding era at 9–12, which makes it the cleanest on-band state fit.

- **Standard 1** — Demonstrate an understanding of the influence of the Atlantic World on the regional and national development of republicanism and federalism from 1607–1815.
- **USHC.1.CE** — Assess the major developments of the American Revolution through significant turning points in the debates over independence and self-government from 1763 to 1791. (The Lee Resolution of June 7, 1776, adopted July 2 — the motion the Declaration announced; state guidance explicitly names the Declaration, the Constitution, the Federalist Papers, and the Bill of Rights among the founding documents in scope.)
- **USHC.1.P** — Summarize the changing relationship between individuals and the government during the period 1607 to 1800. (State guidance names the Ratification Debates and the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions among its content — Lee is on both sides of this indicator: the Anti-Federalist case against ratification, then the senator who pressed the amendments the Bill of Rights became.)
- **USHC.1.CC** — Analyze the processes of continuity and change in the debates over the proper role of the central government and neutrality in foreign affairs from 1789 to 1815. (His consolidation objection as the opening position in the continuing debate over federal power — the two-party system the state guidance names as this indicator's evidence.)
- **USHC.1.E** — Utilize primary and secondary sources to investigate the impact of Atlantic influence on the development of republicanism and federalism. (His letters to Adams, Randolph, and Mason as the Anti-Federalist source set.)

*Verified against: the official SC Department of Education US History and the Constitution Alignment Guide (August 2024), an official interpretation of the 2019 standards (ed.sc.gov).*

## 4. Other states (quick hits)

### New York (K–12 Social Studies Framework, Grade 7)

- **7.4** key idea — Historical Development of the Constitution: "The newly independent states faced political and economic struggles under the Articles of Confederation. These challenges resulted in a Constitutional Convention, a debate over ratification, and the eventual adoption of the Bill of Rights." (Lee is the ratification-debate side of this key idea — and the Bill of Rights its price.)
- **7.4.C.I** — Students will examine from multiple perspectives arguments regarding the balance of power between the federal and state governments, the power of government, and the rights of individuals. (His exact objections: consolidation, federal vs. state power, the "residuum of human rights.")
- **7.3.C.II** — Students will examine the Declaration of Independence and the arguments for independence stated within it. (His motion, June 7, 1776, is what the Declaration announced.)

*Caveat: verified against the NYS K–12 Social Studies Framework via the commonplanner.com Grade 7 compilation (2017 adoption); Grade 7, not 9–12.*

### Texas (TEKS)

- **§113.44(b)(7)(D)** (US Government, high school — on band) — Evaluate constitutional provisions for limiting the role of government, including republicanism, checks and balances, federalism, separation of powers, popular sovereignty, and individual rights. (The provisions Lee demanded — conscience, press, jury — and the Tenth Amendment his proposals anticipated.)
- **§113.44(b)(7)(C)** (US Government, high school — on band) — Analyze how the Federalist Papers such as Number 10, Number 39, and Number 51 explain the principles of the American constitutional system of government. (The Federalist side of the debate he fought; the student needs Publius to judge Lee.)
- **§113.20(b)(15)(C)** (Grade 8) — Identify colonial grievances listed in the Declaration of Independence and explain how those grievances were addressed in the U.S. Constitution and the Bill of Rights. (Grade-band caveat.)

*Verified against: the official TEA §113.44 breakout document (teadev.tea.texas.gov). Note: §113.41 (high school U.S. History) covers 1877–present and has no founding-era anchor — see verification caveats.*

### Florida (NGSSS / State Academic Standards)

- **SS.912.A.1.2** — Utilize a variety of primary and secondary sources to identify author, historical significance, audience, and authenticity to understand a historical period. (The unattributed Federal Farmer letters; Adams's retrospective Autobiography; Lee's own letters as the controlled source set.)
- **SS.912.A.1.5** — Evaluate the validity, reliability, bias, and authenticity of current events and Internet resources. (The debrief's evidence discipline: what we don't know about the Farmer, and why the figure refuses to claim it.)
- **SS.8.A.3.7** (Grade 8) — Examine the structure, content, and consequences of the Declaration of Independence. (Grade-band caveat; the content anchor for the resolution's consequence.)

*Verified against: flrules.org (official NGSSS rule reference) and CPALMS. Codes stable under the 2023 adoption. Florida's 9–12 American History course has no Revolution-era content benchmark (SS.912.A.2 begins with the Civil War) — the on-band 9–12 anchors are the SS.912.A.1 inquiry skills.*

### California (History–Social Science Content Standards, Grade 8)

- **8.2.2** — Analyze the Articles of Confederation and the Constitution and the success of each in implementing the ideals of the Declaration of Independence. (His refusal of the Philadelphia plan against the Articles' failure — the encounter's central constitutional argument.)
- **8.2.3** — Evaluate the major debates of the Constitutional Convention, including their resolutions on slavery and the rights of individuals. (The rights-of-individuals debate he forced from outside the Convention.)

*Verified against: the CA Department of Education History–Social Science Framework, Appendix C (cde.ca.gov), which reproduces the content standards. Grade 8, not 9–12.*

## Verification caveats

1. **C3 Framework**: indicator texts were verified against three independent curriculum documents that quote the Framework verbatim (they agree with each other); the official NCSS PDF itself was not directly retrievable this session. Treat the official publisher as NCSS (2013), socialstudies.org/c3.
2. **Grade-band split.** Only South Carolina (USHC) and Texas's high-school Government course (§113.44) place founding-era content at 9–12. New York, Florida, and California teach the Revolution and founding documents in grades 7–8; Texas's high-school U.S. History course (§113.41) begins at 1877. The encounter targets 9–12, so those anchors are real but one band below target.
3. **Texas §113.41**: the task brief suggested §113.41 might hold founding-era anchors; it does not — it is "United States History Studies Since 1877." The on-band Texas anchor is §113.44 (US Government), verified above.
4. **No standard asks the encounter's question.** Every code above covers the ratification debate, the founding documents, or source evidence — none asks students to judge whether the man who began the Revolution was its conscience or its ghost. The judgment is the library's design, not a standards artifact; the standards supply the evidence base.
5. All verifications in this document were performed on 2026-10-15.
