# Meet Ibn al-Haytham — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Ibn al-Haytham (c. 965–c. 1040), the Basra-born, Cairo-based scholar whose seven-book *Kitab al-Manazir* (Book of Optics) proved that vision happens by light entering the eye — and who made doubt a duty and testing a rule, six centuries before Bacon. Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student renders the closing judgment: he wrote that truth is found through doubt and experiment, not trust in the ancients. Judge the seeker.

The voice is patient, meticulous, and devout in the philosophical sense: a teacher who explains, and a doubter who tests. He never asks to be believed — only to be repeated. That restraint is the design.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

No content warning is required for this encounter. Ibn al-Haytham discusses no violence, no exploitation, and no distressing personal material. The hardest thing he says is that you should not trust him. Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Ibn al-Haytham page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: 25–30 for the encounter, 20–25 for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the figure's opening carefully: note what he promises (one question at the end) and the two threads he offers (how the eye sees; why he does not trust the ancients).
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "How does the eye see?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. The beats: the tutorial hands off to the figure; the student questions him through his life, his finding, and his method; he closes with the question; the debrief follows. If a student stalls, the figure hands them a question — he never stalls.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with a scholar from a thousand years ago — a man from Basra and Cairo who figured out how vision works by testing it with his own hands. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. At the end he will ask you to judge him: he said truth is found through doubt and experiment, not trust in the ancients. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Ibn al-Haytham answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.
- If a student asks him about "Alhazen," he will not know the name. That is date discipline, not a bug — the debrief explains it.

### Pacing

Opening (life and the seven books): 2–3 exchanges. Don't let students camp here — the encounter's weight is in the middle. Middle (how vision works; the darkened room; the doubts against Ptolemy): the bulk of the time; push students toward the trials, not just the claims. Late (doubt as duty; truth and God; the Nile stories): 3–4 exchanges, then let him close. The closing question lands best when the student has heard the method in his own words first.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer: find out what he believed about how truth is found, and decide whether you believe him. He says: suspect the ancients, suspect yourself, submit only to demonstration. At the end, judge the seeker.

## The debrief — do not skip this

The six fixed sections, in order: (1) his name — al-Hasan ibn al-Hasan ibn al-Haytham, c. 965–c. 1040, "Alhazen" to the Latin West; (2) what the history records — intromission proved by trial, the Doubts against Ptolemy, ~96 known titles; (3) what came after — the Latin *De aspectibus*, Roger Bacon, Witelo, Kepler, Descartes, Huygens, and the Bacon credit problem; (4) the plain record — doubt as duty, testing as rule, published trials anyone could repeat; (5) the mechanism — the voice never asks to be believed, refuses the legend, refuses the future; (6) the question that remains — judge the seeker, which does not close when they leave the room.

Discussion openings (built to produce disagreement):

- Who really invented the experimental method — him, Bacon, or is the question itself wrong? (Suggested pairing: run the Bacon encounter first or second, then ask it outright. Note: Bacon never read him.)
- He says to suspect the ancients *and* suspect yourself. Can you actually suspect yourself? What would that look like in your own schoolwork?
- He refuses to tell the Nile story as his own history. Was that honesty — or evasion?
- Why do textbooks start modern science in Europe? What does his existence do to that map?
- If doubt is a duty, is there anything you may *not* doubt? (His answer would be: the demonstration. Press that.)

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **He calls himself "Alhazen" or talks about "scientists."** That is a voice failure — the prompt forbids both. Restart the turn: remind the model of the corpus-vocabulary rules.
- **He tells the Nile/feigned-madness story as fact.** That is a guardrail failure. The prompt requires the marked-gap answer. Restart the turn.
- **He discusses Roger Bacon, Kepler, or the Latin translation.** That is a date-discipline failure (he died c. 1040). Restart the turn.
- **He lectures or monologues.** The prompt requires 60–100-word answers. Nudge once; if it persists, restart.

## Historical notes for the teacher

The honest answers to "did it really happen like that?" — one bullet per caveat in sources.md:

- **The dates are approximate** (c. 965–c. 1040); mark them as such if pressed.
- **The Nile dam / feigned-madness / house-arrest story is legend-adjacent.** Later biographers conflict; the great Sabra himself both discarded the vivid version (Dictionary of Scientific Biography) and allowed the thirteenth-century story "may well be true" (Harvard Magazine, 2003). The figure's refusal to claim it is the honest position.
- **There is a real scholarly dispute over whether the mathematician and the optician were two different men** (Roshdi Rashed vs. Sabra); mainstream scholarship follows Sabra — one man — but some attributions are genuinely uncertain.
- **"I constantly sought knowledge and truth..."** is very widely quoted, but its precise primary-text location is uncertain (the autobiography is lost, known through quotation). The encounter paraphrases rather than asserting exact wording.
- **He did not "invent the scientific method"** — that is our label, not his, and he had predecessors (notably Ibn Sahl). What is securely his: systematic repeatable trials plus mathematical demonstration, published for repetition, six centuries before Bacon.
- **The translation layer:** he spoke and wrote eleventh-century Arabic. Students meet him through A. I. Sabra's (1989) and A. Mark Smith's (2001–2010) translations. Most of his ~96 known titles have no English translation. Say this out loud in class — it is part of the lesson about how knowledge travels.

## One last thing

Teach them to test, not to trust. When he says "the trial decides," make a student ask him to show the trial — the darkened room, the hole, the sickle sun. The encounter works when a student realizes the proof is something they could do themselves, tonight, with a cardboard box. That realization is the whole point.
