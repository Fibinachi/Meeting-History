## Securely attributed works (beyond the Optics)

Titles he himself listed in his fihrist or that survive under his name; not all survive, and English translations are scarce:

- *Al-Shukuk 'ala Batlamyus* (Doubts concerning Ptolemy) — secure; ed. Sabra & Shehaby 1971
- *Maqala fi surat al-kusuf* (On the Form of the Eclipse) — secure
- *Hall shukuk* (Resolution of Doubts) on Euclid — cited in the Stanford Encyclopedia entry below
- *Maqala fi al-kura al-muhriqa* (On the Burning Sphere); works on parabolic and spherical burning mirrors — fihrist titles
- *On Light*; *The Formation of Shadows*; *The Light of the Moon*; *The Light of the Stars*; *The Rainbow and the Halo* — fihrist titles
- Works on arithmetic, astronomy, music, ethics, politics, and poetry are credited to him by Sabra (Harvard Magazine, 2003); geometry was his forte and the best-preserved part of the corpus

## The translation layer (classroom honesty)

The figure speaks modern English. His real words are eleventh-century Arabic. The English voice of this encounter runs through two scholarly translations: Sabra's (Books I–III of the Arabic, 1989) and Smith's (Books 1–7 of the medieval Latin *De aspectibus*, 2001–2010). Books IV–VII of the Arabic have no complete published English translation. Most of the ~96 known titles have no English translation at all. The short quoted phrases in the method answer are Sabra's rendering; everything else in the voice is paraphrase. Say this plainly in class: the students are meeting him through translators.

## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — say which.

- **The setting: c. 1038, a small domed room by the Azhar mosque in Cairo.** No direct evidence of such a conversation; historically plausible, not documented. The late-life Cairo residence, the teaching, and the copying of Euclid and the Almagest come from Sabra's biographical account and the reports of Qaysar ibn Abi'l-Qasim. The date is chosen deliberately: the great books are behind him, and he does not yet know his death (c. 1040) — which gives the encounter its date discipline.
- **The conversation itself.** A student interviewing Ibn al-Haytham never happened — the standing dramatic device of the library.
- **Modern English diction.** The figure speaks English at all, in an accessible register standing in for Sabra's translation. The substance and the translated terms (*trial*, *demonstration*, *the seeker after truth*) are his; the fluency is an authored concession. See "The translation layer" above.
- **Abridgment of the canonical answers.** The vision argument, the eclipse trial, the method, and the Ptolemy doubts are compressed into 60–100-word answers. Compression is authored; nothing in the substance is invented.
- **The Basra administrative office.** Stated in the "who he is" answer; it comes from later biographical reports (Sabra: "he is said to have been a high administrative official"; Qaysar's report via Ibn Abi Usaybi'a), not from his own writings. Plausible, not documented by him.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Write these before the figure ships, even the ones you decided don't matter.

- **The dates are approximate: c. 965–c. 1040.** Born in Basra in 354 AH / 965 AD; died in Cairo around 430–432 AH (c. 1040). Roshdi Rashed gives "after 432/1040." Mark both as approximate if a student presses.
- **The Nile dam / feigned-madness story is legend-adjacent, not fact.** The famous version — he boasted he could dam the Nile, failed before al-Hakim, feigned madness, and spent 1011–1021 under house arrest writing the Optics — comes from much later biographers (al-Bayhaqi d. 1169/70; al-Qifti d. 1248; Ibn Abi Usaybi'a d. 1270), and the reports conflict: al-Bayhaqi has him fleeing to Syria; another report (Qaysar ibn Abi'l-Qasim) has him feigning madness to leave a ministerial post at Basra, *then* going to Egypt. Sabra's DSB article discards the vivid version as "unsupported by other evidence"; his Harvard Magazine essay (2003) calls the thirteenth-century story one that "may well be true." The figure must treat it as story, never as his history — that is the design, not a gap in the research.
- **Were there two Ibn al-Haythams?** Roshdi Rashed argued that the mathematician and the author of the Optics were two different men (al-Hasan vs. Muhammad ibn al-Haytham of Baghdad); Sabra rejected this. Mainstream scholarship follows Sabra (one man), but the authorship of some attributed works is genuinely uncertain. The SEP entry documents the dispute.
- **Where exactly are the methodological statements from?** The full "seeker after truth / enemy of all that he reads" passage is attributed by Sabra to the *Aporias against Ptolemy* (Harvard Magazine, 2003). Closely related formulations open the Optics. The encounter cites the Aporias attribution; if a student finds the passage quoted against the Optics, that is the same Sabra translation tradition, not a contradiction.
- **"I constantly sought knowledge and truth..."** This sentence is very widely quoted (Wikiquote; Ragep 2007; popular histories), but its precise primary-text location is uncertain — it circulates as part of his autobiographical statement, whose original is lost and known only through quotation. The encounter paraphrases the sentiment rather than asserting the exact wording.
- **How many works?** Ibn Abi Usaybi'a credits him with 200 works and letters; al-Qifti lists 96 titles. Many are lost. Do not quote a precise "extant" count — it is not securely established.
- **Who translated the Optics into Latin, and when?** The *De aspectibus* circulated in the Latin West from the 12th–13th century (Spain/Toledo tradition); the translator is not securely known. Do not name one.
- **Did he "invent the scientific method"?** That is a modern label he never used, and the encounter never puts it in his mouth. He had predecessors in the Arabic optical tradition (notably Ibn Sahl on burning mirrors and lenses). What is securely his: systematic, repeatable trials combined with mathematical demonstration, published so others could repeat them — six centuries before Bacon. The debrief states this plainly; the figure does not.
- **Did Bacon know his work?** No. The Latin *De aspectibus* was read by Roger Bacon, Witelo, and Pecham in the thirteenth century and by Kepler, Descartes, and Huygens later — but not by Francis Bacon. (The Bacon figure's debrief states the same; the two encounters agree.)
- **The "house arrest" detail.** Popular accounts say he wrote the Optics under house arrest 1011–1021. This belongs to the disputed Nile story above. The Optics was composed in the first half of the eleventh century in Cairo; the circumstances are not securely known.
