## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — say which.

- **The February-1808 Wilmington conversation itself** — pure dramatic device. His residence in Wilmington and his failing health in early 1808 are documented; no such encounter occurred.
- **His closing synthesis** ("the contradiction of a man who would not approve what he would not abandon"; "right about the risks and wrong about the moment") — his voice, not his words. No such admission exists in the corpus; marked as authored.
- **His introducing himself as "the Farmer"** — not a device: his authorship of the letters was public knowledge long before 1808.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Written before the figure ships, even the ones that don't matter.

- **Was he a Quaker?** — No. He was born into a practicing Quaker family (his mother was a Friend), but his father left his congregation; Dickinson attended meetings intermittently, held many Quaker beliefs, and never formally affiliated with any church. His wife and daughters were committed Friends. He is buried in the Friends Burial Ground in Wilmington.
- **Did he coin "the United States of America"?** — Traditional attribution, not settled fact. What is documentary: the Articles' opening article, which he drafted, reads "The Stile of this confederacy shall be 'The United States of America'." The encounter does not claim the invention as fact.
- **How many people did he enslave?** — Sources vary (37 to 59). Marked uncertain; the encounter names no number. What is solid: conditional manumission in 1777 (twenty-one years further service), unconditional manumission in 1786.
- **The order of his military service** — Brigadier general in the Pennsylvania Associators (July–December 1776) FIRST, then private in the Kent County militia (August 1777); he declined a Delaware brigadier's commission in October 1777. The popular shorthand "enlisted as a private" compresses and inverts the sequence; the encounter states it correctly.
- **He did not sign the Constitution in person** — illness forced him to leave the Convention early; he authorized fellow Delaware delegate George Read to sign for him (39 signatures, 38 signers).
- **The July 1, 1776 speech** — no full text survives in his own hand. His position is recorded in the congressional record and in his one documented sentence ("My conduct this day, I expect will give the finishing blow…"). The encounter invents no further text and marks the gap.
- **Calvert's reading of his motive** — her argument that protecting Quakers and religious dissenters from wartime persecution was central to his refusal is scholarly interpretation, not his stated reason. His stated reasons (confederation unfinished, no foreign alliance, unreadiness, opposition to violence) are what the voice gives.
- **Birth date** — November 1732; sources vary on the day (Old/New Style reckoning). The encounter uses 1732–1808 only.
- **"Penman of the Revolution"** — a 19th-century epithet, not his own. The debrief may use it; his voice does not.
- **The Liberty Song** — his authorship is documented ("Then join hand in hand, brave Americans all").
- **His wife Mary Norris** — a wealthy Philadelphia Friend; married 1770. (Standard biographical fact; not load-bearing for the encounter.)
