# Meet John Dickinson — Standards Alignment (verified 2026-10-15)

Encounter: the student meets John Dickinson (1732–1808) in Wilmington in early 1808 — the lawyer who wrote *Letters from a Farmer in Pennsylvania* (1767–68), the most-read American political argument before *Common Sense*, who opposed independence on the floor of Congress on July 1, 1776, absented himself from the July 2 and July 4 votes, and refused to sign the Declaration — then took a commission as brigadier general in the Pennsylvania militia and, after resigning, served as a private in the Delaware militia. It closes with the judgment: *he wrote the case against Britain, refused the Declaration, and served anyway. Was the moderate right? Judge him.*

**Grade band fit for every framework below: 9–12** (high school U.S. History / Government / ELA literacy). Founding-era content sits in grades 7–8 in most states (see caveats); South Carolina places it at 9–12.

## 1. C3 Framework (National Council for the Social Studies, 2013)

Grade band: **9–12**. (Indicator texts verified against multiple independent curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim; the official NCSS PDF was not directly retrievable in this session — see verification note at end.)

### Dimension 2 — Civics

- **D2.Civ.9.9-12** — Use appropriate deliberative processes in multiple settings. (The encounter's load-bearing civic concept: his July 1, 1776 speech — confederation unfinished, no foreign alliance secured, the colonies not of one mind — is loyal opposition offered as deliberation; his "moderation" was the word he actually used. Fabius's rule of judgment closes the arc: "a little experience will cast more light upon the subject, than a multitude of debates.")
- **D2.Civ.12.9-12** — Analyze how people use and challenge local, state, national, and international laws to address a variety of public issues. (The Farmer letters challenging Parliament's duties — "a duty laid for the sole purpose of raising money is a tax"; the Olive Branch Petition's appeal; his own manumission and the failed 1786 Delaware abolition bill as challenges to slave law.)

### Dimension 2 — History

- **D2.His.2.9-12** — Analyze change and continuity in historical eras. (The Farmer of 1768 to Fabius of 1788: the man who wrote "the Articles served the revolution, and failed the peace" and then defended the stronger Constitution — change in the frame, continuity in the principle.)
- **D2.His.5.9-12** — Analyze how historical contexts shaped and continue to shape people's perspectives. (The Quaker peace conscience — peace the first duty, violence the last resort — shaping his opposition to the war; the refusal and the service both come from it.)

### Dimension 4 — Communicating Conclusions and Taking Informed Action

- **D4.1.9-12** — Construct arguments using precise and knowledgeable claims, with evidence from multiple sources, while acknowledging counterclaims and evidentiary weaknesses. (The closing judgment — was the moderate right? — is this standard as a student task: the letters, the refusal, the service, the cost.)
- **D4.4.9-12** — Critique the use of claims and evidence in arguments for credibility. (The caricatures — the preening aristocrat of the musical *1776*, the scowling naysayer of HBO's *John Adams* — critiqued against his documented sentence on July 1 and the general-then-private service record.)

## 2. Common Core ELA

### Reading: Informational Text / History-Social Studies, 11–12 and 9–10

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RI.11-12.9** — Analyze 17th-, 18th-, and 19th-century foundational U.S. documents of historical and literary significance for themes, purposes, and rhetorical features. (The Farmer letters as 18th-century public advocacy — the tax-as-consent argument, the "dutiful children" passage; the *Letters of Fabius* — nine letters defending the Constitution against the "high-toned" charge.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.6** — Evaluate authors' differing points of view on the same historical event or issue by assessing the claims, reasoning, and evidence. (His July 1, 1776 opposition against the signers' position — the same vote, two readings of the moment; his refusal speech against his own war service, the two halves the closing question holds together.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.6** — Compare the point of view of two or more authors for how they treat the same or similar topics, including which details they include and emphasize in their respective accounts. (Letter XII's "we should behave like dutiful children who have received unmerited blows from a beloved parent — firm, but not violent" against the independence case of 1776 — what each includes and emphasizes about reconciliation.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.9** — Compare and contrast treatments of the same topic in several primary and secondary sources. (The refusal across his one documented sentence, the popular caricatures, and the debrief's plain record — the historiography is the encounter's fourth beat.)

### Speaking & Listening, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.3** — Evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric, assessing the stance, premises, links among ideas, word choice, points of emphasis, and tone used. (His measured, melancholy explanation — reasons given in order, cost stated plainly, verdict never claimed — is the rhetoric students must evaluate: what does it mean that he never says "I was right" and never says "I was wrong"?)

### Writing, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.W.11-12.1** — Write arguments to support claims in an analysis of substantive topics or texts, using valid reasoning and relevant and sufficient evidence. (The closing judgment is designed as a post-encounter argument prompt, per the teacher instructions.)

## Verification caveats

1. **C3 Framework**: indicator texts were verified against multiple independent curriculum documents that quote the Framework verbatim (they agree with each other); the official NCSS PDF itself was not directly retrievable this session. Treat the official publisher as NCSS (2013), socialstudies.org/c3.
2. **Grade-band split.** Only South Carolina places the founding era in 9–12 courses (USHC). New York, Texas, Florida, and California all teach the Revolution and founding documents in grades 7–8. The honest high-school anchors outside SC are: Texas §113.41(b)(1) (Celebrate Freedom Week) and Florida's SS.912.A.1 inquiry benchmarks (the historiography method, not the content).
3. **"Moderate" is not his word.** The closing question's "was the moderate right?" is the encounter's framing; his guardrails forbid the voice from calling itself "a moderate." The D2.Civ.9 deliberation fit uses the historical deliberation (the July 1 reasons), not the modern label.
4. **The July 1, 1776 speech has no surviving full text in his own hand** (per the figure's own sources.md) — the RH.9-10.9 historiography fit and the D4.4 caricature critique must be framed around the documented sentence and position, not invented speech.
5. **No standard asks the encounter's question.** Every code above covers deliberation, dissent, the Articles, or the sources — none asks whether the moderate was right. The judgment is the library's design, not a standards artifact; the standards supply the evidence base.
