# Meet John Dickinson — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with John Dickinson (1732–1808), run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student renders the closing judgment: he wrote the case against Britain, refused the Declaration, and served anyway. Was the moderate right? Judge him.

He explains his own argument (the Farmer letters), his own reasons for the refusal (from the record, honestly), and his own service after it — general, then private, in that order. He speaks from complete conviction: measured, principled, melancholy. He explains the refusal but never defends it, never asks for absolution, never claims vindication. The student decides.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

- Students will discuss slavery: he held people in bondage and freed them in 1786. He uses his own vocabulary ("slaves," "property") — stated plainly, not softened.
- Students will discuss war: he served as a brigadier general and then as a private. No graphic detail of violence; the encounter's drama is a man saying no and then serving anyway, not battle.
- The encounter's subject is a political refusal. Students should know in advance that the tension is constitutional and moral, not violent.
- No graphic content, no sexual content.

Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the John Dickinson page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: 25–30 minutes for the encounter, 20–25 minutes for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

**Suggested pre-reading:** selections from *Letters from a Farmer in Pennsylvania* — Letters I and II for the tone, Letter XII for the famous passage ("we cannot be happy, without being free…"). The letters are public domain; a readable PDF of Letters I–II is at americainclass.org: http://americainclass.org/sources/makingrevolution/crisis/text4/dickinsonletters1767.pdf

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the opening carefully: he introduces himself in Wilmington in early 1808, explains the format in character ("you ask, I answer"), and hands the student the thread — the Farmer, and the refusal.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "Why didn't you sign the Declaration?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. Then try a hard one: "You were a general — why did you serve as a private?" Then the hardest: "Were you right?" Notice the voice — measured, melancholy, the refusal explained but never defended — and the bracketed nudges that point at what he doesn't say. That held refusal to judge himself is the pedagogy.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with John Dickinson — the man who wrote the most-read American political argument of the 1760s, and then refused to sign the Declaration of Independence. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. He wrote the case against Britain better than anyone, said no at the vote, and fought in the war anyway. Your job is to decide whether the man who said no was right. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The no-image frame

Most students arrive with no picture of Dickinson at all — he is the founder the stories skip. A few will know him from the musical *1776*, which makes him a preening aristocrat; the record shows a plain-dressing lawyer whom even John Adams respected. Let the encounter replace the caricature; do not pre-lecture it. The debrief does the plain stating.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Dickinson answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- When the conversation has run its course — ideally after the student has heard the Farmer's argument, the refusal and its reasons, and the service — he asks the closing question himself and stops: "I wrote the case against Britain, refused the Declaration, and served anyway. Was the moderate right? Judge me."
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Pacing

- Opening and the Farmer letters: 4–6 exchanges. The argument is the on-ramp — don't let students camp on tax history; the refusal is the destination.
- The refusal, the service, the Articles: 6–8 exchanges. This is the core. The order of service (general, then private) always lands — check that it did before you move on.
- Fabius, the Quaker conscience, slavery: 3–4 exchanges. These deepen the judgment — especially Fabius, which shows him answering his own 1776 fear in 1788.
- The closing: he asks it when the conversation has run its course. If a student types something like "I'm done" or "I want to judge you now," he closes. He never delivers the verdict himself.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — the answer: "Understand what he wrote, why he refused, and what he did after refusing — then judge him: was the moderate right?"

## The debrief — do not skip this

Six sections, in this order: names, what the history records, what came after, the plain record, the mechanism, the question that remains. The full debrief text is in the prompt — you read it out of character and run it as discussion, not a read-aloud. The moderation-vs-timing frame lives in sections 4 and 5; make sure it lands.

Discussion openings (built to produce disagreement):

- He said the confederation should be finished and a foreign alliance secured before independence was declared. The alliance (France, 1778) came two years later; the Articles weren't ratified until 1781. Was he wrong about the timing — or was the timing wrong about him?
- He refused to sign and then served as a private. Does the service redeem the refusal, or does the refusal stain the service — or are they two honest answers to two different questions?
- In 1788 he defended a Constitution far stronger than the Articles he wrote in 1776. Did he change his mind about power, or did the Articles change his mind for him?
- He freed the people he held in 1786 — among the first of his generation to do it — while his fellow founders kept theirs. Does that change how you weigh the rest of him?
- He never called himself a moderate; the label is ours. What do we gain and what do we lose by judging an 18th-century man with a 21st-century political category?

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **The voice lectures.** Dickinson's answers should stay near 60–100 words. If the model starts monologuing on confederation theory, remind it of the global rules or restart — the canonical answers are the baseline.
- **The voice claims modern knowledge.** If he discusses anything after February 1808 — his death date, the Civil War, the musical *1776*, his reputation, the Dickinson Writings Project — that's a date-discipline failure. The prompt forbids it; in a supervised run, treat the slip as a teaching moment: "What just happened? He couldn't have known that."
- **The voice invents the July 1 speech.** If he quotes his 1776 speech beyond the one documented sentence, that's a corpus failure — stop and correct it. The canonical answer is the boundary; the rest is a marked gap.
- **The voice claims vindication.** If he says history proved him right, that's a voice failure. He cannot know how history judged him, and the prompt forbids the claim.
- **The voice softens the refusal into an apology — or hardens it into defiance.** Both are voice failures. The register is explanation, not defense and not defiance.
- **The voice reverses the service order.** If he says he was a private first and a general after, that's a factual failure — correct it to the record: general in Pennsylvania, then private in Delaware.
- **The voice uses modern labels.** If he calls himself "a Founding Father" or "a moderate" as an identity, that's a vocabulary failure. "Moderation" is his word; the labels are ours.

## Historical notes for the teacher

- He was not a Quaker. Born into a practicing Quaker family, attended meetings intermittently, held Quaker beliefs, never formally affiliated with any church. His wife and daughters were committed Friends.
- "Coined 'the United States of America'" is traditional attribution, not settled fact. Documentary: the Articles' opening article, which he drafted, gives the confederation that name.
- The number of people he enslaved is uncertain (sources say 37 to 59). Solid: conditional manumission 1777 (twenty-one years further service), unconditional 1786. His 1786 Delaware abolition bill failed.
- He did not sign the Constitution in person — illness; he authorized George Read to sign for him. No full text of his July 1, 1776 speech survives in his own hand.
- Jane Calvert's reading (protecting Quakers/religious dissenters as central to his refusal) is scholarly interpretation, not his stated reason.
- Suggested pairing: the `jefferson` encounter — the man who signed and wrote the words Dickinson would not. (Same library.)

## One last thing

He explains so beautifully that students will stop pressing him. Teach them the second question. The first question gets his reasons — the confederation unfinished, the alliance unsecured, the colonies unready. The second is the one he can't answer for them: "When would it have been soon enough?" That question is where the judgment lives.
