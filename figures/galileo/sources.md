## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — say which.

- **The meeting itself.** Galileo at the Villa Il Gioiello in 1640, blind, receiving a student for questioning. No direct evidence of any such meeting; pure dramatic device. The setting's facts (house arrest at Arcetri from December 1633, blindness by ~1638, the *Discorsi* printed at Leiden 1638) are documented.
- **The figure speaks English.** Galileo wrote in Tuscan Italian and Latin. The voice follows the English of Drake's and Finocchiaro's translations, not his Italian — a translation device, stated here honestly.
- **The refusal on motives.** The canonical answer on *why* he recanted deliberately declines to supply inner motives. This is a design decision, not a historical claim: the record contains his words and signature, not his private reasons, and the encounter marks the gap rather than filling it.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Written before the figure ships, even the ones decided not to matter.

- **"Eppur si muove" is almost certainly legend.** The earliest printed account is Giuseppe Baretti's *The Italian Library* (1757) — more than a century after Galileo's death, from a literary critic who also wrote, falsely, that Galileo spent six years in the Inquisition and was tortured. Viviani's 1655–56 biography (written by Galileo's own disciple) does not mention it; the trial records do not cite it. The figure must NEVER claim he said it — this is a worked example of the library's anti-myth discipline.
- **He was not tortured.** On June 21, 1633 he was verbally threatened — "tell the truth, otherwise we shall have recourse against him to the appropriate remedies of law" — and answered "I am here to obey." The rack was not used on him. Students who say he was tortured are repeating Baretti's error.
- **The Simplicio-as-Pope-insult is reported, not documented as Galileo's intent.** The Pope's divine-omnipotence argument (which Urban VIII had made to Galileo in person in 1624 and asked him to include) does appear as Simplicio's closing speech in the Dialogue — that is in the text. That Galileo *meant* "Simplicio" as a personal insult to the Pope was the allegation of his enemies and the special commission; "Simplicio" was also the name of a genuine 6th-century Aristotelian commentator, which complicates the charge. The encounter presents it fairly: what he did vs. what was alleged.
- **The 1616 memorandum is unsigned.** The judges relied on an unsigned memorandum in the Holy Office file ("neither to hold, defend, nor teach in any manner whatsoever"). Galileo produced Bellarmine's signed certificate of May 26, 1616, stating he had neither abjured nor been sentenced. The encounter lets both papers speak.
- **"Aged seventy years."** The abjuration describes Galileo as "aged 70 years"; by modern reckoning (born 15 February 1564, abjuring 22 June 1633) he was 69. Period documents count inclusively; not an error worth litigating in class.
- **The trial record is adversarial.** Galileo's testimony was given under examination by a tribunal seeking a conviction, and the proceedings were shaped by what the judges wanted on paper. The encounter treats his words as his recorded words, not as a transcript of his soul.
- **DEAD RULE satisfied.** Galileo Galilei died January 8, 1642, at Arcetri. Date discipline ends there.
