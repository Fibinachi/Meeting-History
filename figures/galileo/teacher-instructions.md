# Meet Galileo — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Galileo Galilei (1564–1642), run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student answers the closing question: he recanted to survive, and kept working. Coward, pragmatist, or the only man in the room who understood what survival was for? Judge him.

Galileo is the library's trial figure: the parallel to Socrates and John Brown, tried and condemned and punished — except he recanted. He speaks in two registers: brilliant and combative about the heavens, careful about Rome. The carefulness is the evidence. He will not explain why he knelt; the record holds his words and his signature, and he marks that gap rather than filling it.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

- Students will encounter the mechanics of an Inquisition trial: interrogation, the verbal threat of torture ("the remedies the law provides"), a forced public abjuration, imprisonment commuted to house arrest.
- The encounter names Giordano Bruno's burning (1600) as the threat Galileo understood.
- There is no gore, no torture described, no slur, no sexual content. The material is intellectual and moral pressure, not physical violence.
- The hardest moment is designed, not accidental: the figure refuses to say why he recanted, and the student must judge without that knowledge.

Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Galileo page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: 25 minutes for the encounter, 25 minutes for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the opening carefully: he tells the student up front that he will give them what he did and said, but not what was in his heart — the record does not contain it.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "What did you see through the tube?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. Then ask: "Why did the Dialogue anger Rome?" Then: "Did you say 'and yet it moves'?" Watch the second answer in particular — this encounter kills a famous legend on sight. That is the library's anti-myth discipline at work.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Galileo Galilei — the man who found moons around Jupiter, and the old man who knelt before the Inquisition and took it all back. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. At the end you'll be asked to judge him: he recanted to survive, and kept working. Was that cowardice, pragmatism, or the only sane thing a man could do? There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Galileo answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- When the student is finished, the figure asks the closing question and stops. That is the end of the encounter; the debrief begins.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Pacing

- Opening and the tube (early pool): 5–7 exchanges. Don't let students camp here — the moons are the setup, not the subject.
- The Dialogue, the trial, the abjuration (middle pool): 8–10 exchanges. This is the heart. Push them past the facts toward the Simplicio question: whose argument did he put in the fool's mouth?
- Arcetri, the legend, the judgment (late pool): 5–7 exchanges. End with the closing question.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer: to hold the trial's record in one hand and the legend in the other, knowing which is which, and to decide for yourself what a recantation means when the work continues. The question does not close when you leave the room.

## The debrief — do not skip this

The six fixed sections, in order: names; what the history records; what came after (the ban lifted in stages, the legend grown, the 1992 acknowledgment); the plain record; the mechanism (the two registers, the deliberate refusal on motives, the legend killed on sight); the question that remains.

Discussion openings — built to produce disagreement:

- **Was the abjuration a lie?** He swore with his hand on the Gospels that he had always believed what the Church taught. Did he believe what he swore — or is a forced oath not a statement of belief at all?
- **Compare the fates.** Bruno burned in 1600 and never recanted; Galileo knelt in 1633 and wrote the *Discourses* in the years after. Does survival have a moral price — and who pays it, the survivor or the dead?
- **Who does the legend serve?** "Eppur si muove" was first printed in 1757, by a man who got nearly everything else wrong. Why do we want Galileo to have whispered defiance? What does the story do for us that the record doesn't?
- **The Simplicio question.** The Pope's own argument about divine omnipotence appears in the mouth of the book's fool. Accident, arrogance, or a trap Galileo set for himself? What he did vs. what was alleged — where do you land?
- **The gap.** The figure refuses to tell you why he knelt. Is that refusal honest — or is it the encounter dodging the one question that matters? (Push back on this one. The design is defensible; make them defend or attack it.)

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **The model lets Galileo claim "Eppur si muove."** This is the known failure mode for this figure — the legend is everywhere in the training data. If it happens, correct it in the debrief (section 4, the plain record: he never said it) and report it to the library owner. The guardrails forbid it explicitly.
- **The model softens him toward Rome** (apologetic, grateful, at peace with the sentence). The prompt holds the careful register, not a reconciled one. If it drifts, steer the student to ask about June 21.
- **The model invents motives** ("he recanted because he wanted to finish his book"). That is exactly what the canonical answer refuses to do. Correct it in the debrief: the record does not contain his reasons, and the encounter marks the gap on purpose.

## Historical notes for the teacher

The honest answers to "did it really happen like that?" — one bullet per caveat in sources.md:

- **"Eppur si muove": legend.** First printed 1757 (Baretti's *The Italian Library*), over a century after his death. His disciple Viviani's 1655–56 biography omits it; the trial records never mention it. A man who had just bought his life with an oath does not spend it on a whisper in front of the cardinals.
- **He was not tortured.** He was verbally threatened on June 21, 1633 ("the remedies the law provides") and answered "I am here to obey." Students repeating "he was tortured" are repeating Baretti's 1757 error.
- **The Simplicio insult is alleged, not proven.** The Pope's divine-omnipotence argument really is Simplicio's closing speech in the Dialogue. That Galileo meant "Simplicio" as a personal insult to Urban VIII was the charge of his enemies; the name also belonged to a genuine 6th-century Aristotelian commentator. Present both.
- **The 1616 memorandum is unsigned.** The judges' key paper had no signature; Galileo countered with Bellarmine's signed certificate. Both are in the record.
- **The trial record is adversarial.** His testimony was given under examination by a tribunal building a conviction. The encounter quotes his recorded words, not his soul.
- **Date discipline.** Everything after January 8, 1642 — the lifting of the ban (1758, 1835), the 1992 Vatican statement — belongs to the debrief, never to the figure.
- **Suggested pairing: newton (not yet built).** Galileo's falling-bodies work in the *Discorsi* is the ground Newton builds on — and Newton's century is the one that stopped punishing men for describing the sky. When the Newton encounter ships, run them as a pair: the man who knelt, and the man who never had to.

## Standards alignment

Grade band 9–12 (adaptable to undergraduate survey). Strongest alignments: historical thinking (sourcing, corroboration, and the distinction between record and legend); the relationship between science and authority in early modern Europe; argumentative writing from evidence (the closing judgment). Link the full per-figure alignment document when the library's standards pack ships.

## One last thing

Teach them to ask what the record doesn't say. The encounter's central discipline is the marked gap: the figure will not supply his motives, and the legend will not supply his defiance. Students who learn to notice what a source refuses to give them — and to judge anyway — have learned the skill this figure exists to teach.
