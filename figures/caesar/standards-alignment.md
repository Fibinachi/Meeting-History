# Meet Caesar — Standards Alignment (verified 2026-10-15)

Encounter: you meet Gaius Julius Caesar in Rome in mid-February 44 BC — days after the Lupercalia, dictator perpetuo, preparing the eastern campaign — as he gives his own account of Gaul, the Rubicon, the civil war, and clemency, all in the third person, all drawn from the Commentaries: propaganda-as-literature in his own voice. The encounter closes on the judgment: reformer who saved Rome from itself, or tyrant who murdered the Republic and wrote the alibi? Judge him.

**Grade band fit for every framework below: 9–12** (high school ELA literacy / World History). Note: most states' *content* anchors for classical Rome sit in Grade 6 (see caveats); the 9–12 weight of this encounter comes from skills standards — source criticism, reading against the text, argumentation — which are band-matched.

## 1. C3 Framework (National Council for the Social Studies, 2013)

Grade band: **9–12**. (Indicator texts verified against curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim; the official NCSS PDF was not directly retrievable in this session — see verification note at end.)

### Dimension 2 — History

- **D2.His.4.9-12** — Analyze complex and interacting factors that influenced the perspectives of people during different historical eras. (His two registers: in Gaul the cool surveyor — "All Gaul is divided into three parts" — and at home the injured party, defending the tribunes and his dignitas; the debrief names the device — notice how the campaign and the audience change the voice.)
- **D2.His.6.9-12** — Analyze the ways in which the perspectives of those writing history shaped the history that they produced. (The encounter's thesis: the Commentaries became the most-read Latin prose in history, the school text for two thousand years — his version is the one every student meets first, which means his perspective *is* the history most students inherit.)
- **D2.His.10.9-12** — Detect possible limitations in various kinds of historical evidence and differing secondary interpretations. (What his evidence cannot do: the Gallic War gives no death toll for Gaul as a whole; his own account never names the Rubicon; Plutarch's million slain and million captive are Plutarch's, not his — and ancient numbers are unreliable, so the limitation cuts both ways.)
- **D2.His.11.9-12** — Critique the usefulness of historical sources for a specific historical inquiry based on their maker, date, place of origin, intended audience, and purpose. (The natural fit: the Commentaries as a self-serving source — maker (Caesar, author of his own history), date (written year by year from the field), intended audience (the Roman people and Senate), purpose (self-justification published to keep his name before them while his enemies worked against him in Rome). "Notice what he counts: legions, days, marches, census tablets. Notice what he never counts: the dead.")
- **D2.His.16.9-12** — Integrate evidence from multiple relevant historical sources and interpretations into a reasoned argument about the past. (The closing judgment requires integrating his account with Plutarch's figures, Suetonius's secondhand tags, Cato's charge over the broken truce, and Cicero's praise of the Commentaries — into one verdict on reformer vs tyrant.)

### Dimension 4 — Communicating Conclusions and Taking Informed Action

- **D4.1.9-12** — Construct arguments using precise and knowledgeable claims, with evidence from multiple sources, while acknowledging counterclaims and evidentiary weaknesses. (The closing question is the argument prompt: both cases at full strength — the debrief's plain record supplies the counterclaim his account omits, including the evidentiary weakness that ancient casualty figures are unreliable.)
- **D4.4.9-12** — Critique the use of claims and evidence in arguments for credibility. (Students test his claims against his own arithmetic: 368,000 Helvetii marched out, 110,000 returned (BG 1.29) — he never states the missing 258,000; "his own numbers do the accusing; the reader has to do the arithmetic he won't do.")

*Verified against: multiple independent curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim (2026-10-15; they agree with each other — womenshistory.org lesson plans, thinkport.org inquiry kits, curriculum.llilasbenson.utexas.edu units, electionlab.org alignment). Official document: College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework for Social Studies State Standards, NCSS, 2013 (socialstudies.org/c3).*

## 2. Common Core ELA — Literacy in History/Social Studies & Speaking/Listening

Grade band: **9–12**. (Verified against the official Common Core State Standards Initiative site, thecorestandards.org.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 9–10

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.1** — Cite specific textual evidence to support analysis of primary and secondary sources, attending to such features as the date and origin of the information. (His case vs the record around him: the Commentaries (his own, 58–48 BC) vs Plutarch, Suetonius, Appian (secondhand, a century or more later) — students cite by date and origin, and the prompt marks the boundary between his words and other men's memory.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.6** — Compare the point of view of two or more authors for how they treat the same or similar topics, including which details they include and emphasize in their respective accounts. (The Helvetii campaign: his account counts census tablets (368,000 souls, 110,000 returned — BG 1.29) and never counts the dead; Plutarch counts a million slain and a million captive (Life of Caesar 15) — compared for which details each includes and emphasizes.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.8** — Assess the extent to which the reasoning and evidence in a text support the author's claims. (His clemency claim — "Caesar's clemency is not weakness; it is policy," "a man who has been spared does not forget who spared him" — tested against the evidence: Corfinium (BCiv 1.23), the pardoned Brutus and Cassius, and what the debrief supplies that he cannot know.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.3** — Evaluate various explanations for actions or events and determine which explanation best accords with textual evidence, acknowledging where the text leaves matters uncertain. (The Rubicon: his explanation — the Senate's final decree, the fleeing tribunes, his dignitas — vs the debrief's — "an act of treason under Roman law" — with the acknowledged uncertainty that his own account never names the river at all.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.6** — Evaluate authors' differing points of view on the same historical event or issue by assessing the authors' claims, reasoning, and evidence. (The Usipetes and Tencteri: his account (BG 4.13–15 — the truce, the attack, the destroyed camp) vs Cato's demand in the Senate that he be handed over to the Germans for breaking faith (Plutarch, Life of Cato) — assessed claim by claim, with the sources' asymmetry named.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.8** — Evaluate an author's premises, claims, and evidence by corroborating or challenging them with other information. (His premises corroborated or challenged with other information: the Kessel/Meuse excavations' evidence for the German campaign; Plutarch's figures flagged as unreliable; his unrevised Civil War breaking off mid-campaign.)

### Speaking & Listening, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.1** — Initiate and participate effectively in a range of collaborative discussions (one-on-one, in groups, and teacher-led) with diverse partners on grades 11–12 topics, texts, and issues, building on others' ideas and expressing their own clearly and persuasively. (The encounter *is* a sustained one-on-one examination: "Ask. Caesar will answer... ask precisely; Caesar answers precisely" — and he hands the student threads to pull.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.3** — Evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric, assessing the stance, premises, links among ideas, word choice, points of emphasis, and tone used. (Students evaluate the third person as a device — "Caesar crossed," never "I," with exactly one scripted break ("veni, vidi, vici") — the cool, balanced periods, the composure that never breaks; "he never boasts — he records, and the recording is the boast.")

### Writing, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.W.11-12.1** — Write arguments to support claims in an analysis of substantive topics or texts, using valid reasoning and relevant and sufficient evidence. (The closing question — reformer who saved Rome from itself, or tyrant who murdered the Republic and wrote the alibi? — is designed as a post-encounter argument prompt, per the teacher instructions.)

## 3. South Carolina — Social Studies College- and Career-Ready Standards (2019)

Grade band: **9–12** encounter weight is skills-based; the content anchor is Grade 6.

- **6.1.P** (Grade 6) — Analyze the shift from early to classical civilizations and the enduring contributions of classical civilizations. (State guidance: inquiry into "turning points in the organization and transformation of the four major classical civilizations in China, Greece, India, and Rome" — the Republic-to-Empire transition is the encounter's turning point, lived through and written by its maker — and into the "cultural and technological advancements... that continue to influence modern societies": his calendar, his colonies and citizenship extensions, and the Commentaries themselves, the school text for two thousand years. Grade-band caveat: SC's only classical-civilizations content anchor sits in Grade 6; no high-school SC anchor covers ancient Rome — Modern World History begins at 1300, and USHC/USG cover American history.)

*Verified against: the official 2019 South Carolina Social Studies College- and Career-Ready Standards document on ed.sc.gov (Grade 6 standards; indicator and state guidance quoted verbatim 2026-10-15).*

## Verification caveats

1. **C3 Framework**: indicator texts were verified against multiple independent curriculum documents that quote the Framework verbatim (they agree with each other); the official NCSS PDF itself was not directly retrievable this session. Treat the official publisher as NCSS (2013), socialstudies.org/c3.
2. **Grade-band split**: the 9–12 alignments for Caesar are skills standards (RH, SL, W, D4) plus the perspective/source-analysis C3 indicators. State content anchors for classical Rome sit below the 9–12 band except NY 9.3 (Grades 9–10), TX §113.42 (World History), and FL SS.912.W.2.13 (9–12).
3. **RH fit is debrief-driven**: the encounter proper is conversational; the reading standards engage through the debrief's primary-text work (BG 1.29's arithmetic, BG 4.13–15 vs Cato's charge, the Rubicon omission, the debrief's Plutarch figures) and the teacher-supplied excerpts of the Commentaries.
4. **No adopted SC high-school anchor for ancient Rome**: checked the 2019 SC high-school courses — Modern World History begins at 1300; USHC and US Government cover American history. 6.1.P (Grade 6) is the only SC content anchor.
