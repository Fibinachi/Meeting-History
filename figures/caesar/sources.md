# Sources — Gaius Julius Caesar (100–44 BC)

DEAD RULE: satisfied. Caesar died March 15, 44 BC (the Ides of March), assassinated in the Theater of Pompey complex at Rome.

## What is Caesar's own words vs. what is the record around him

- **Caesar's own words:** the two Commentaries (the Gallic War in 7 books; the Civil War in 3 books) — his diction, cadence, third-person instrument, and the cool surveyor's register all come from these; the letters as transmitted in Cicero's correspondence; the harangues embedded in the Commentaries. The voice's vocabulary, facts, and silences are bounded by these.
- **Around him, not his:** every famous tagline. "Alea iacta est" is Suetonius's Latin (and possibly a copyist's corruption of an imperative); Plutarch's Greek is from Menander. "Veni, vidi, vici" comes down through Appian, Plutarch, and Suetonius — not through Caesar's own writings. "Kai su, teknon" is Suetonius reporting what "some have written"; Plutarch says he said nothing. "Et tu, Brute?" is Shakespeare. The prompt never lets the voice present secondhand words as his own text.
- **Notably thin or absent in Caesar's own corpus:** the Rubicon is never named in *de Bello Civili*; the Gallic War gives no death toll for Gaul as a whole; Vercingetorix's execution is not in the Commentaries (they end with the surrender); the casualty figures the debrief uses are Plutarch's, not his. These silences are scripted as boundaries, not filled.

## The honest caveat (for the teacher — read before running)

The Commentaries are self-justification written for a Roman audience: dispatches from the field, published to keep his name before the people and the Senate while his enemies worked against him in Rome. The prompt treats them as **his case** — the best-written alibi in history — and the debrief supplies what they omit: Plutarch's million dead and million enslaved in Gaul (flagged as Plutarch's figures, with the scholarly caution that ancient numbers are unreliable); the arithmetic of his own Helvetii census (368,000 marched out, 110,000 returned — *de Bello Gallico* 1.29); the civilians starved at Alesia; the truce-breaking attack on the Usipetes and Tencteri; Cato's demand. The encounter's whole pedagogy is reading the source against itself. Do not let the student leave with only his version. That is what the debrief is for.

## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — say which.

- **The meeting itself (a single sitting, one conversation covering his life)** — pure dramatic device. No such interview occurred.
- **The setting: Rome, mid-February 44 BC, days after the Lupercalia** — the Lupercalia (Feb 15), the dictatorship perpetuo, and the Parthian preparations are all documented; placing the encounter in this window is historically plausible, not documented. No record exists of his conversations in this period.
- **Compression of a lifetime into an hour** — the encounter has him speak on events eight years apart as present-tense memory. Dramatic compression.
- **The scripted phrasings** — the boundaries they mark (what the corpus supports vs. what is secondhand) are documented; the sentences themselves are authored, including the English renderings of his Latin.
- **The "men remember a phrase" Rubicon answer** — the device is honest: the phrase is secondhand and his account omits the scene; the sentences refusing it are authored.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Written before the figure ships, even the ones that don't matter.

- **Birth year: 100 or 102 BC?** 100 BC is the widely accepted date (July 12 or 13). Mommsen argued for 102; his case was rejected by Nipperdey and is generally viewed as incorrect. The figure uses 100.
- **When exactly did he cross the Rubicon?** January 49 BC, most commonly given as the night of January 10–11. The figure says "January of 49" without the day. His own account never names the river at all.
- **Did he say "the die is cast"?** Unverifiable. Suetonius (writing ~170 years later) gives the Latin "iacta alea est"; Plutarch gives Greek from Menander's comedy, in the imperative — "let the die be cast" — and some scholars argue Suetonius's text originally read the imperative too, before a copyist dropped a letter. The prompt treats the phrase as secondhand and the figure never claims it.
- **What were his last words?** Unknown. Suetonius reports — as what "some have written" — the Greek "kai su, teknon" ("you too, my child?"); Plutarch says he said nothing, pulling his toga over his head when he saw Brutus. "Et tu, Brute?" is Shakespeare's Latin invention. The encounter is set before the Ides, so the figure never speaks of any of this.
- **How was Vercingetorix executed?** After the triumph of 46 BC he was executed in the Tullianum — commonly given as strangulation, but the method is uncertain (a plaque at the Mamertine Prison even claims beheading in 49 BC). The figure says "executed" and no more.
- **When was he to leave for Parthia?** Most accounts give March 18, 44 BC; one scholarly account gives March 19. The figure says "in March" without the day.
- **Are Plutarch's casualty figures reliable?** They are Plutarch's, not Caesar's: 800+ towns, 300 peoples, 3 million engaged, 1 million slain, 1 million captive (*Life of Caesar* 15). Ancient casualty figures are routinely inflated and modern scholars treat them with caution. The debrief states them as Plutarch's with that caution — and pairs them with Caesar's own Helvetii arithmetic, which needs no caution: 368,000 marched out, 110,000 returned (*BG* 1.29). He never states the missing 258,000.
- **Book 8 of the Gallic War** is by Aulus Hirtius, written after Caesar's death to cover 51–50 BC. It is not Caesar's and is not used for voice.
- **The Civil War may be unrevised.** The three books break off mid-campaign (48 BC) and show signs of incomplete revision — consistent with composition in haste during the war. The voice does not depend on this either way.
- **The "pale and the thin" remark** (about Brutus and Cassius) is Plutarch's (*Caes.* 62), secondhand. The prompt flags it as such and forbids the figure from drawing any inference from it.
- **The exact wording of his reply to the plot warnings** (the "die once" substance in Suetonius 86) is uncertain in the Latin; the prompt gives the substance in authored phrasing and says so.
- **Cicero's praise of the Commentaries** ("like naked statues, straight and beautiful" — *Brutus* 262) is Cicero's, contemporary, and secondhand as quoted by the figure.
- **The pirates, Sulla's mercy, the early career** are from Suetonius and Plutarch (secondhand) but concern his own life; the figure may know them as his own past. They are non-load-bearing and belong to classroom Q&A, not the scripted material.
