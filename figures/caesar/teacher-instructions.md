# Meet Caesar — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Gaius Julius Caesar (100–44 BC) — the general who conquered Gaul, crossed the Rubicon, won Rome's civil war, and was named dictator — run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student renders the closing judgment: reformer who saved Rome from itself, or tyrant who murdered the Republic and wrote the alibi? Judge him.

This encounter is the library's best lesson in reading a source against itself. Caesar's voice is built from the Commentaries — propaganda as literature, in the third person — and the whole pedagogy is the student feeling the art working on them in real time, then doing, in the debrief, the arithmetic he never does.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

Students will encounter: mass killing in war, stated plainly and without euphemism — the Helvetii census (368,000 marched out, 110,000 returned), the starvation of civilians at Alesia, the destruction of the Usipetes and Tencteri during truce negotiations, the execution of Vercingetorix; enslavement on a massive scale (Plutarch's figure: a million captives); and assassination — the figure himself is weeks from the Ides of March, though he does not know it, and the debrief states the killing plainly (some sixty conspirators; Suetonius counts twenty-three wounds — stated without wound detail). There is no sexual content and no slur language in this encounter. The hardest material is the voice itself: students will find him reasonable, and that reasonableness is the evidence. Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Caesar page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: 25–30 minutes for the encounter, 20–25 minutes for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the tutorial, then Caesar's scripted opening. Note the last line — it hands the student three doors (Gaul, the war with Pompey, the dictatorship) and demands precision.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "Why did you cross the Rubicon?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, in the third person — and then, outside the voice, in brackets and italics, the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. The encounter's beats: (1) the tutorial hands off; (2) the student questions Caesar through early, middle, and late suggestion pools; (3) Caesar closes with the closing question and stops; (4) you run the debrief. There is no button to press — the student types "I am ready for your closing question" (or the equivalent) to bring on the close.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Julius Caesar — the general who conquered Gaul, crossed the Rubicon, and became dictator of Rome. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. He wrote the most famous war memoirs in history, and he wrote them about himself in the third person. Pay attention to that. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Caesar answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- To bring on the closing, the student asks for it (e.g., "I am ready to judge you"). Caesar then asks the closing question and stops.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Pacing

Opening: 2–3 exchanges. Don't let students camp on biography — the suggestion pools escalate toward the Rubicon and the hard questions. Middle (Gaul, the civil war, clemency): the bulk of the encounter, 10–15 exchanges. Late (the kingship question, the plots, Parthia): 3–5 exchanges, then the close. If a student is still asking about his childhood at exchange twelve, point at the bracketed suggestions.

## The goal, stated plainly

The student should leave having felt propaganda working on them in real time — the coolest, most reasonable voice in the room, explaining mass killing the way a surveyor explains a boundary line — and carrying one question they cannot put down: reformer who saved Rome from itself, or tyrant who murdered the Republic and wrote the alibi? When a student asks "what's the goal?", the answer is: "You are going to judge Julius Caesar. The questions you ask are how."

## The debrief — do not skip this

Six fixed sections, in order: (1) their name; (2) what the history records; (3) what came after; (4) the plain record; (5) the mechanism; (6) the question that remains. The teacher runs the debrief as a discussion, not a read-aloud.

Discussion openings — built to produce disagreement:

- "He never says 'I' — except once, in three words. Why does he write about himself in the third person? What does it do to you as a reader?"
- "His own census says 368,000 Helvetii marched out and 110,000 returned. He never says the other number out loud. Who has to do that arithmetic — him, or you?"
- "Cato wanted him handed over to the Germans for breaking a truce. Caesar's own book describes the attack. Whose account do you trust, and why?"
- "He calls it clemency. The men he pardoned killed him. Was the mercy real, or was it the same instrument as the sword?"
- "Reformer who saved Rome, or tyrant who murdered the Republic and wrote the alibi — where did you land, and which sentence of his put you there?"

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **Caesar lectures instead of answering.** The prompt caps answers at 60–100 words. If the model drifts into monologue, resend the last question with "Answer briefly, in one paragraph."
- **Caesar says "I."** He must not — with exactly one scripted exception, the Zela answer ("veni, vidi, vici"). If he uses the first person anywhere else, that is a failure of the prompt — correct him in the debrief ("the third person is the whole design; he just broke it") and note it under "If the model misbehaves" for the library.
- **Caesar volunteers "the die is cast."** He must never claim the phrase. If the student quotes it, he redirects to the record. If he claims it as his own line, correct in the debrief.
- **Caesar foreshadows the assassination.** He knows plots exist in general and dismisses them; he must never name conspirators, describe the Ides, or speak of his death. If he does, correct in the debrief and note it for the library.
- **Caesar breaks into modern moralizing.** Out of character for this figure. Correct in the debrief.

## Historical notes for the teacher

The honest answers to "did it really happen like that?" — one bullet per caveat from sources.md:

- **The Commentaries are self-justification.** Written as dispatches from the field to keep his name before Rome while his enemies worked against him. The prompt treats them as his case; the debrief supplies what they omit. Do not let the student leave with only his version.
- **The casualty figures are Plutarch's, not Caesar's** — 800 towns, 300 peoples, 3 million engaged, 1 million slain, 1 million captive (*Life of Caesar* 15). Ancient numbers are routinely inflated; state them as Plutarch's with that caution. What needs no caution is Caesar's own Helvetii arithmetic: 368,000 marched out, 110,000 returned (*Gallic War* 1.29). He never states the missing 258,000.
- **"The die is cast" is secondhand.** Suetonius gives the Latin; Plutarch gives Greek from Menander, in the imperative ("let the die be cast"). It appears nowhere in Caesar's own account — which never even names the Rubicon. The figure never claims the phrase.
- **His last words are unknown.** Suetonius reports — as what "some have written" — the Greek "kai su, teknon"; Plutarch says he said nothing. "Et tu, Brute?" is Shakespeare.
- **Cato really did demand he be handed to the Germans** after the destruction of the Usipetes and Tencteri (55 BC), and Caesar really did answer with a letter to the Senate. The attack itself is in Caesar's own book (*Gallic War* 4.14–15) — the students can read the primary source against Cato's charge.
- **Vercingetorix** spent six years in a Roman prison, was paraded in the triumph of 46 BC, and was executed; the method is uncertain.
- **The Parthian departure** is given as March 18, 44 BC in most accounts (one scholarly account says the 19th). The figure says "in March."
- **Book 8 of the Gallic War is Hirtius's**, not Caesar's; the Civil War may be unrevised. Neither affects the voice.
- **Suggested pairing:** Cicero — the Republic's defender vs. its destroyer. Noted here as a future figure; it is not built. Do not attempt a double encounter until both figures exist.

## Standards alignment

Condensed: grades 9–12, World History (ancient Rome). Strongest fits: C3 Framework D2.His.3 / D2.His.4 (analyze change and continuity; analyze multiple and complex causes and effects), D2.His.9 (analyze the relationship between historical sources and the historical claims they are used to support), D2.His.12 (use questions generated about multiple historical sources to identify further areas of inquiry); Common Core RH.11-12.1 (cite textual evidence), RH.11-12.2 (determine central ideas), RH.11-12.6 (evaluate authors' differing points of view — here, Caesar's account against Plutarch's, Cato's, and Cicero's). ## One last thing

Teach them to count. Caesar gives numbers the way other men give excuses — precisely, and always about the wrong thing. The single skill that matters most for this figure: when he gives you a census, do the arithmetic he won't do. When he shifts registers — the cool surveyor in Gaul, the injured party at home — ask what changed. The boast is never stated. It is enacted. Watch for it.
