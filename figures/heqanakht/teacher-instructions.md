# Meet Heqanakht — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Heqanakht — a ka-servant (mortuary priest) and farmer in Egypt, around 1950 BC — run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him by typing questions: about his fields, his grain accounts, his household, the Nile flood. At the end each student answers the closing question: four thousand years away, worried about grain and tenants — stranger or neighbor? Judge what his letters show you.

This is the library's fragmentary-corpus test case. Everything the figure knows comes from five letters and a handful of grain accounts — the Heqanakht papyri. Where the letters go silent, he goes blank, and the blankness is the lesson: this is what historians actually have.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

What students will encounter: a household quarrel — Heqanakht orders a servant woman, Senen, thrown out of the house, and blames his eldest son for letting her mistreat his wife; a family that rejects his wife, and his fury at them; hunger kept at bay by rations counted to the half-hekat; a world of masters and servants in which his authority is total and never questioned. Nothing is described graphically. The power dynamic is the material — a man who can expel a woman from his house with a dictated sentence — and students will need to process it. Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Heqanakht page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 40–50 minutes: 20–25 for the encounter, 15–20 for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the tutorial and Heqanakht's opening carefully. Note what he offers as the first thread: the inundation — whether the flood has come.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "Has the inundation come?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. The beats: the tutorial hands off to Heqanakht; the student questions him (farm, grain, household, the quarrel, the things he doesn't know); Heqanakht closes with the question — "Stranger or neighbor? Judge what my letters show you" — and the debrief begins.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with a farmer from four thousand years ago — an Egyptian who ran his whole household by writing letters home. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. At the end you'll judge him: stranger or neighbor? There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Heqanakht answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.
- Suggested opening if the room freezes: ask him about the Nile flood. ("Has the inundation come?") It is the thread he offers, and it opens the farm, the grain, and the calendar of his year.

### Pacing

This is a shorter encounter than most in the library — the corpus is small, and that is the point. Opening (the flood, the farm): 3–4 exchanges, don't let students camp here. Middle (the debts, the rations, the quarrel): the heart of it, 6–8 exchanges. Late (what he doesn't know): 2–3 exchanges, then let him close. If a student exhausts the letters, that is success, not failure — hand them the closing.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer: find out what five letters and some grain accounts can and cannot tell you about a man four thousand years dead, and then judge him — stranger or neighbor — on that evidence alone.

## The debrief — do not skip this

The six fixed sections, in order: his name; what the history records; what came after (the translators, the economists, Agatha Christie); the plain record (stated without his vocabulary: a patriarch with total authority, counting rations, defending his wife, expelling a servant); the mechanism (the ordinariness was the evidence; the double filter of 4,000 years and a translator; the designed ignorance); the question that remains.

Discussion openings — built to produce disagreement:

- Stranger or neighbor — where did you land, and what single detail decided it?
- He orders Senen out of the house and defends Hetepet with fury. Is he a good man? Does the evidence answer that?
- What did you expect him to know that he didn't? (The king's name? The pyramids? What "Egypt" is?) Why did you expect it?
- Every word he spoke reached you through a translator, across four thousand years. How much does that change what you think you heard?
- We have five letters. What would a sixth letter — one that didn't survive — most change?

## If the model misbehaves

This figure has not yet been through a full solo test run — see "Validation status" in sources.md. Run it yourself first and log what you find here.

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this. Or hand the student the flood: "Has the inundation come?"
- **The model makes him quaint or cute.** The prompt forbids it explicitly ("not quaint and not cute"), but watch for it — folksy-uncle warmth is the failure mode for this figure. If it appears, redirect: ask about the grain debts, where his voice is hardest.
- **The model invents beyond the letters** (a king's name, the farm's location, his death). The prompt marks every gap; if the model fills one, treat it as a break and correct it in the debrief — it is itself a teachable moment about the corpus boundary.

## Historical notes for the teacher

The honest answers to "did it really happen like that?" — one bullet per caveat from sources.md.

- **The corpus is five letters and some accounts.** Everything else is scholars' reconstruction. Say this out loud in the debrief.
- **Hetepet's title is disputed.** Translators have called her his wife, his new wife, his concubine, his bride. The prompt follows Allen ("my wife"). None of it is settled — that disagreement is itself worth five minutes.
- **Three Ipis.** The vizier Ipi (his master), a woman named Ipi in the household, and "Ipi the Younger" (a debtor). Students will merge them; don't let them.
- **"Short rations" is an interpretation.** Baer's reading of the accounts — that Heqanakht kept the household tight to speculate on grain — is economics, not Heqanakht's words. Keep the two separate.
- **The farm's location is unknown.** "Nebsyt" on the letter docket; "Perhaa" in older books; neither on a modern map.
- **Agatha Christie** read the papers (via Stephen Glanville) and set *Death Comes as the End* (1944) in this world. A good "what came after" hook.

## One last thing

Teach them to notice the blank. The most important student move in this encounter is not a clever question about grain — it is asking about something big (the king, the future, "Egypt") and watching him not know it. When a student finds a blank, stop the room and ask: what would a historian do with exactly this much? That is the whole lesson.
