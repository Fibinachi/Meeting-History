# Meet Heqanakht — Standards Alignment (verified 2026-10-15)

Encounter: students converse with Heqanakht — a ka-servant and farmer in Egypt, c. 1950 BC — bounded strictly by what five letters and a handful of grain accounts show. Where the letters go silent, he goes blank, and the blankness is the lesson: this is what historians actually have. At the end each student answers: "Four thousand years away, worried about grain and tenants. Stranger or neighbor? Judge what his letters show you."

**Grade band fit for every framework below: 9–12**, except where noted. This is the library's fragmentary-corpus case: the strongest alignments are source-criticism standards, not content-coverage standards.

---

## 1. C3 Framework (NCSS, 2013) — the strongest section for this figure

- **D2.His.9.9-12** — Analyze the relationship between historical sources and the secondary interpretations made from them. (The translator disagreements — Gunn, James, Allen — and Baer's "short rations" reading are the encounter's live instance of this.)
- **D2.His.10.9-12** — Detect possible limitations in various kinds of historical evidence and differing secondary interpretations. (Five letters, dictated to a scribe, found in tomb debris: the lesson is what this much evidence cannot settle.)
- **D2.His.11.9-12** — Critique the usefulness of historical sources for a specific historical inquiry based on their maker, date, place of origin, intended audience, and purpose. (Letters dictated for the household's eyes, never meant for us — the figure's designed ignorance of the king's name, the farm's location, the future.)
- **D2.His.1.9-12** — Evaluate how historical events and developments were shaped by unique circumstances of time and place as well as broader historical contexts. (The inundation calendar, the ka-servant's office, grain-as-money.)
- **D4.1.9-12** — Construct arguments using precise and knowledgeable claims, with evidence from multiple sources, while acknowledging counterclaims and evidentiary weaknesses. ("Stranger or neighbor?" must be argued on the letters alone, with the gaps named.)

*Indicator texts verified against multiple curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim (see the library's Trial of John Brown alignment for the verification set); the official NCSS PDF was not directly retrievable.*

## 2. Common Core ELA

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.1** — Cite specific textual evidence to support analysis of primary and secondary sources, attending to such features as the date and origin of the information. (The ration account down to the half-hekat; the Senen expulsion order.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.2** — Determine the central ideas or information of a primary or secondary source; provide an accurate summary of how key events or ideas develop over the course of the text.
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.3** — Evaluate various explanations for actions or events and determine which explanation best accords with textual evidence, **acknowledging where the text leaves matters uncertain**. (Hetepet's disputed standing; the farm's unknown location; the "what the letters cannot tell us" exchange.)

*Verified against thecorestandards.org (texts confirmed in this library's Trial of John Brown alignment, 2026-10-15).*

## Verification caveats

1. **C3 Framework**: indicator texts verified against three independent curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim; the official NCSS PDF was not directly retrievable. Official publisher: NCSS (2013), socialstudies.org/c3.
2. **No high-school ancient-Egypt content anchor in several states.** SC and CA place Egypt content in Grade 6; NY in Grade 9; TX's WH.2.C is in-band but general ("river valley civilizations," Egypt not named); FL's in-band fit is the W.1 inquiry strand rather than Egypt-specific content. The encounter's honest 9–12 fit is methodological (source criticism), not content coverage — say so.
3. **D2.His.9.9-12**: the text ("Analyze the relationship between historical sources and the secondary interpretations made from them") was verified against three independent curriculum sources; some older reproductions phrase it differently ("analyze the past as interpreted and contested"). The quoted text is the Framework's.
