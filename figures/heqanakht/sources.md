## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — say which.

- **The meeting itself.** Heqanakht's letters were one-way dictation, sealed and sent home. He never sat answering a stranger's questions. Pure dramatic device — the library's standard conceit.
- **The setting (dictating to a scribe while away from home).** Documented that the papyri were written "by Heqanakht and one or more scribes" and that the letters were sent from wherever he was to the household — the scene of live dictation is assembled from that, not recorded. Historically plausible, not documented.
- **His spoken English.** The letters are Middle Egyptian. His "speech" is Allen's English made conversational — a double filter (4,000 years + a translator) the debrief names explicitly.
- **"The scribe will seal it."** The encounter's present tense — letters waiting to be sealed — is a dramatic convenience. Some papyri were in fact found still folded, tied and sealed; the *scene* is not documented.
- **The closing question's word "tenants."** Heqanakht's own vocabulary is "those who owe me grain" / "the men who work my fields." The closing uses the library's plain modern word deliberately — the one place his diction yields to the student's.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Written before the figure ships, even the ones decided not to matter.

- **DEAD RULE — satisfied.** Heqanakht lived around 1950 BC, in Egypt's early Middle Kingdom (12th Dynasty) — roughly four thousand years ago. His death is unrecorded, but the date discipline holds by four millennia.
- **The corpus is tiny.** Five letters and a handful of accounts. That is the whole of him. The encounter's central teaching point is that historians work from exactly this much — every gap in the prompt is a real gap in the record, not a simplification.
- **Hetepet's standing.** The translators disagree: Gunn, James and Baer render the defended woman as his "concubine"; Wente and Parkinson as his "new wife"; Goedicke as his "bride"; Allen's rendering has Heqanakht call her "my wife." The prompt follows Allen ("that is my wife") and marks the dispute here. Do not let students treat any one title as settled.
- **Sihathor vs. Sahathor.** Transliteration variants of the same name (zA-Hwt-Hr) appear across sources — the letter-carrier in Letter III and a ration-list recipient. The prompt uses "Sihathor" throughout and notes the variant.
- **Where the farm was.** The letter docket addresses the household at "Nebsyt" (Allen's reading); older literature placed the farm near "Perhaa." Neither is fixed on a modern map. The figure does not name a location the scholarship cannot.
- **Which king.** One letter bears a "regnal year 8," but the letters never name the king, and scholars differ on the attribution. The figure is not given a king's name the letters do not supply.
- **"Short rations."** The observation that Heqanakht valued only new barley and kept the household on tight rations while speculating on grain is Baer's economic interpretation of the accounts (via the secondary literature), not Heqanakht's own statement. The prompt has him speak only of counting rations exactly — the interpretation belongs to the debrief/teacher notes, not the voice.
- **Dictated or written himself.** The Met notes the papyri were written by Heqanakht "and one or more scribes." The encounter stages dictation; it is possible he wrote some himself. The device is marked above.
- **Agatha Christie.** *Death Comes as the End* (1944) was inspired by Stephen Glanville pointing Christie at the Heqanakht papers as a source for everyday life c. 2000 BC. Documented; belongs to "what came after," never to the figure.
- **Ipi, Ipi, and Ipi.** Three different people share the name in the papers: the vizier Ipi (Heqanakht's master), a woman named Ipi in the household ration list, and "Ipi the Younger," a grain-debtor at Yusebek. The prompt keeps them distinct; students will conflate them — watch for it.
- **No pyramids, no pharaohs, no "ancient Egypt."** None of these appear in the letters. The figure's blankness on them is the corpus boundary made visible, not a character flaw.
