# Meet George Mason — Standards Alignment (verified 2026-10-15)

Encounter: the student meets George Mason (1725–1792) at Gunston Hall in early 1792 — the Virginia planter who drafted the Declaration of Rights adopted June 12, 1776, and who in 1787 printed his *Objections*, refused to sign the Constitution for want of a bill of rights, fought ratification at Richmond, and lost. The ten amendments were ratified on December 15, 1791 — he knows it. And he called the slave trade "diabolical in itself, and disgraceful to mankind" while his own will freed none of the people he held. It closes with the judgment: *he wrote the rights and refused the Constitution — and the rights won. Judge him.*

**Grade band fit for every framework below: 9–12** (high school U.S. History / Government / ELA literacy). Founding-era content sits in grades 7–8 in most states (see caveats); South Carolina places it at 9–12.

## 1. C3 Framework (National Council for the Social Studies, 2013)

Grade band: **9–12**. (Indicator texts verified against multiple independent curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim; the official NCSS PDF was not directly retrievable in this session — see verification note at end.)

### Dimension 2 — Civics

- **D2.Civ.14.9-12** — Analyze historical, contemporary, and emerging means of changing societies, promoting the common good, and protecting rights. (The encounter's civic mechanism: the printed *Objections* and the Richmond fight are the means; the ten amendments ratified December 15, 1791 are the change — "the rights are in the frame of the government." His qualified satisfaction in the Griffin letter is the evidence.)
- **D2.Civ.8.9-12** — Evaluate social and political systems in different contexts, times, and places, that promote civic virtues and enact democratic principles. (The encounter's load-bearing move: measuring the 1776 Declaration's "all men are by nature equally free and independent" against the Gunston Hall will that names thirty-six people and frees none of them.)
- **D2.Civ.12.9-12** — Analyze how people use and challenge local, state, national, and international laws to address a variety of public issues. (The *Objections* as a printed challenge to the Constitution's law — "There is no Declaration of Rights, and the laws of the general government being paramount" — and his September 12, 1787 motion for a bill of rights, defeated with no state voting for it.)

### Dimension 2 — History

- **D2.His.2.9-12** — Analyze change and continuity in historical eras. (The Virginia Declaration of 1776 to the amendments of 1791: Madison worked from Mason's language, and the phrases run through the First, Fifth, Sixth, and Eighth Amendments — the continuity is the encounter's vindication.)
- **D2.His.5.9-12** — Analyze how historical contexts shaped and continue to shape people's perspectives. (The slave-trade objection read against Charles Cotesworth Pinckney's charge at Philadelphia — that Virginia would gain by ending importation, her slaves rising in value, since she had more than she wanted — the economic context that makes the objection's motive a live question.)

### Dimension 4 — Communicating Conclusions and Taking Informed Action

- **D4.1.9-12** — Construct arguments using precise and knowledgeable claims, with evidence from multiple sources, while acknowledging counterclaims and evidentiary weaknesses. (The closing judgment — "one of those does not sit easy with the other" — is this standard as a student task: the Declaration, the *Objections*, and the will are the three sources; neither cancels the other.)

## 2. Common Core ELA

### Reading: Informational Text / History-Social Studies, 11–12 and 9–10

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RI.11-12.9** — Analyze 17th-, 18th-, and 19th-century foundational U.S. documents of historical and literary significance for themes, purposes, and rhetorical features. (The Virginia Declaration of Rights as the 1776 founding text — the first three sections as he wrote them; the *Objections* as founding-era public advocacy. His rhetorical feature is the absence of ornament: short declarative sentences, legal precision — "he states, he doesn't argue.")
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.6** — Evaluate authors' differing points of view on the same historical event or issue by assessing the claims, reasoning, and evidence. (The ratification debate proper: Mason's *Objections* against the Federalist case for the Constitution — the same document, two verdicts on whether it was safe without a bill of rights.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.1** — Cite specific textual evidence to support analysis of primary and secondary sources, connecting insights gained from specific details to an understanding of the text as a whole. (His denunciation in print — "diabolical in itself, and disgraceful to mankind" — cited against the will's silence; his September 12 motion's defeat cited from the convention record.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.9** — Compare and contrast treatments of the same topic in several primary and secondary sources. (The *Objections* against the Federalist Papers' treatment of the same Constitution — the Anti-Federalist and Federalist accounts of what the frame did and did not do.)

### Speaking & Listening, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.3** — Evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric, assessing the stance, premises, links among ideas, word choice, points of emphasis, and tone used. (The bluntness is the rhetoric: a man who enumerates like a man reading articles, gets blunter when challenged, and marks his one silence — "I will not reconcile them for you" — by refusing to speak.)

### Writing, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.W.11-12.1** — Write arguments to support claims in an analysis of substantive topics or texts, using valid reasoning and relevant and sufficient evidence. (The closing judgment is designed as a post-encounter argument prompt, per the teacher instructions.)

## 3. South Carolina — Social Studies College- and Career-Ready Standards (2019)

Course: **United States History and the Constitution** (9–12).

- **USHC.1.CE** — Assess the major developments of the American Revolution through significant turning points in the debates over independence and self-government from 1763–1791. (The official development notes explicitly name the Bill of Rights among the founding documents in scope — his September 12, 1787 bill-of-rights motion, the walkout, and the fight that produced the amendments.)
- **USHC.1.CX** — Contextualize significant republican developments within North America's connection to the Atlantic World. (The development note for this indicator names its purpose: inquiry into "how republican ideals helped some citizens, though marginalized groups still sought better opportunities and treatment" — the Declaration's "all men by nature equally free" against the will that frees none.)
- **USHC.1.P** — Summarize the changing relationship between individuals and the government during the period 1607–1800. (The rights he wrote as inherent, not granted — "Rights are not granted. They are inherent" — and the government he refused to sign for want of them.)
- **USHC.1.E** — Utilize primary and secondary sources to investigate the impact of the Atlantic influence in the regional and national development of republicanism and federalism. (The Virginia Declaration, the printed *Objections*, the Griffin letter, the Jefferson letter, the will — the encounter's evidence base.)

## Verification caveats

1. **C3 Framework**: indicator texts were verified against multiple independent curriculum documents that quote the Framework verbatim (they agree with each other); the official NCSS PDF itself was not directly retrievable this session. Treat the official publisher as NCSS (2013), socialstudies.org/c3.
2. **Grade-band split.** Only South Carolina places the founding era in 9–12 courses (USHC). New York, Texas, Florida, and California all teach the Revolution and founding documents in grades 7–8. The honest high-school anchors outside SC are: Texas §113.41(b)(1) (Celebrate Freedom Week) and Florida's SS.912.A.1 inquiry benchmarks (the method, not the content).
3. **The number he held is uncertain** (per the figure's own sources.md — "about 90" to "more than 100"; his will named 36 and freed none). Standards alignments cite the documented facts only: the will's silence, the denunciation's words, the convention record.
4. **No standard asks the encounter's question.** Every code above covers the rights, the ratification debate, or slavery — none asks students to judge the contradiction of a man who wrote liberty and kept people. The judgment is the library's design, not a standards artifact; the standards supply the evidence base.
