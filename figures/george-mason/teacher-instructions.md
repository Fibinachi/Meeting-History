# Meet George Mason — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with George Mason (1725–1792), run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student renders the closing judgment: he wrote the rights and refused the Constitution — and the rights won. Judge him.

This is the library's protest encounter. Mason wrote the Virginia Declaration of Rights in 1776 — the model text behind the Bill of Rights — then refused to sign the Constitution in 1787 because it had no bill of rights. His printed Objections became the Anti-Federalist case, and the amendments he demanded were ratified four years later. He speaks from complete conviction: blunt, plain, finished arguing. The student must decide what a refusal is worth — and what to do with the man who held people in slavery while condemning the trade.

**Tier: OPEN.** No restricted content beyond the standard slavery discussion.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

- Students will discuss slavery as practiced and condemned by a man who owned people — plainly, in his own vocabulary ("slaves," "property"). He calls the trade "diabolical in itself, and disgraceful to mankind" and holds enslaved people himself, freeing none in his will. The tension is stated, never softened.
- Students will encounter the hard economics of the founding: the documented charge that ending the slave trade raised the value of the enslaved people Virginia planters already held. This is named in the debrief, never in his voice.
- The figure speaks in early 1792, ailing, months before his death; he knows he is unwell and says so. No graphic content.

Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## The slavery honesty requirement

Read this carefully — it is the load-bearing design decision of the encounter.

Your students will press him on the slave trade, and he will give them everything they want — the printed objection, the Richmond speech, "diabolical in itself, and disgraceful to mankind." Then they will ask about the people he holds, and he will state the fact flatly: he holds slaves at Gunston Hall; his will names them and frees none. He will not reconcile the two. He will not apologize, will not explain, will not invent an account he never gave. The silence is the design — the parallel to the Jefferson encounter, where the figure marks a silence instead of filling it.

Do not let students leave thinking the question was answered. It was not. The debrief closes it with the plain record: on the order of a hundred people held, thirty-six named in the will, none freed — and Pinckney's charge that a right-sounding objection can also pay. If a student never asks about his own slaveholding, the suggestion pools will get them there — check that they did before you close.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the George Mason page. Copy the entire prompt text.

- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the opening carefully: he introduces himself at Gunston Hall in 1792, explains the format in character ("you ask, I answer — one rule only, and it is mine: I state; I do not argue"), and hands the student the thread — the rights.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "Why wouldn't you sign the Constitution?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. Then try a hard one: "Why did you object to the slave trade?" Then the hardest: "How many slaves do you own, and why didn't you free them?" Notice the voice — the bluntness holding, the refusal to reconcile — and the bracketed nudges that point at what he doesn't say. That held bluntness is the pedagogy.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with George Mason — the Virginia planter who wrote the Declaration of Rights that became the model for the Bill of Rights, and the man who walked out of the Constitutional Convention rather than sign one that had no bill of rights. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. He is completely certain he was right. Your job is to find the places where the words and the life don't sit easy together. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The walkout as the entry hook

Most students arrive knowing nothing about Mason — he is the forgotten founder, and the encounter is built for that. Open with the hook: in September 1787, in the room where the Constitution was made, one man refused to put his name to it — and four years later the thing he demanded was added anyway. The Bill of Rights exists because he said no. That is the sentence that buys you the next forty minutes.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Mason answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- When the conversation has run its course — ideally after the student has heard the Declaration, the Objections, and the slaves he holds — he asks the closing question himself and stops: "I will not tell you how to judge me. Judge me."
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Pacing

- Opening and the Virginia Declaration: 4–6 exchanges. The words are the on-ramp — don't let students camp on the 1776 drafting history.
- The Objections and the walkout: 5–6 exchanges. The no-bill-of-rights core, the September 12 motion, the refusal. This is the engine of the encounter.
- The slave trade and his own slaveholding: 5–6 exchanges. This is the core. The Richmond speech, the flat statement, the refusal to reconcile.
- The amendments and his satisfaction: 3–4 exchanges. The "much Satisfaction," the conditions — and the closing.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — the answer: "Understand what he wrote, what he refused, what his refusal won, and what he kept — then judge him: he wrote the rights and refused the Constitution, and the rights won."

## The debrief — do not skip this

Six sections, in this order: names, what the history records, what came after, the plain record, the mechanism, the question that remains. The full debrief text is in the prompt — you read it out of character and run it as discussion, not a read-aloud. The Virginia-Declaration-to-Bill-of-Rights line is the debrief's spine.

**Have students compare the texts.** Put Section 1–3, 8, 12, and 16 of the 1776 Virginia Declaration side by side with the First, Fourth, Fifth, Sixth, and Eighth Amendments. Ask them to find the phrases that survived — "the free exercise of religion," "the freedom of the press," "cruel and unusual punishments," "the law of the land" becoming "due process." The borrowing is the evidence for the protest's efficacy, and the students should do the tracing themselves.

Discussion openings (built to produce disagreement):

- He refused to sign — and the Bill of Rights followed. Would it have happened without his refusal? Was the refusal the cause, or did Madison move for his own reasons?
- "Diabolical in itself, and disgraceful to mankind" — and he freed no one in his will. What does condemnation without action mean? (The Jefferson encounter asks the same question from the other side; the pairing is deliberate.)
- At Philadelphia, Pinckney said Virginia would gain by ending the trade — her slaves would rise in value. Does a self-interested motive poison a right-sounding objection, or does the objection stand on its own?
- "A more favourite Object with them, than the Liberty and Happiness of the People" — he blamed South Carolina and Georgia's hunger for the trade. Was his objection about the enslaved, or about the Union's strength?
- He stayed home at Gunston Hall — declined the Senate, resigned the court — rather than serve the government he had refused to sign. Protest, principle, or abdication?

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **The voice lectures.** Mason's answers should stay near 60–100 words. If the model starts monologuing on republican theory, remind it of the global rules or restart — the canonical answers are the baseline.
- **The voice claims modern knowledge.** If he discusses anything after early 1792 — his death that October, the later amendments, the Civil War, his modern reputation — that's a date-discipline failure. The prompt forbids it; in a supervised run, treat the slip as a teaching moment: "What just happened? He couldn't have known that."
- **The voice invents on slaveholding.** If he gives a number for the people he holds, offers a reconciliation, apologizes in modern terms, or claims abolitionist intent beyond what he wrote, that's a corpus failure — stop and correct it. The canonical answer is the flat statement and the marked silence.
- **The voice claims a triumph.** If he treats the Bill of Rights as his personal victory — rather than his written "satisfaction" with stated conditions — that's a voice failure. The prompt forbids invented triumph.
- **The voice softens him.** If he gets warm, charming, or explanatory about himself, that's a voice failure. He is blunt about everything except himself, and silent on the one place it counts.

## Historical notes for the teacher

- The Virginia Declaration was adopted June 12, 1776; the draft was Mason's, the adopted text a committee document. "Largely his" is the honest phrasing.
- His September 12, 1787 motion for a bill of rights was defeated with no state voting for it (Massachusetts abstaining), per Madison's notes; his motion for a second convention was defeated as well.
- He was one of three delegates present who refused to sign, with Elbridge Gerry and Edmund Randolph. He did not claim to be alone.
- The "much Satisfaction" letter to Samuel Griffin is dated September 8, 1789 — written when the amendments had passed the House but not yet the Senate. His satisfaction was real and qualified: he attached conditions (judiciary, elections, navigation majorities, a council) that were never adopted.
- The number he enslaved is uncertain — "about 90" at Gunston Hall to "more than 100" across his holdings in modern estimates. His will named 36 and freed none. He never gave a number himself.
- Pinckney's charge (Virginia gains if the trade ends) is from Madison's notes of the August 1787 debate — reported speech, and real scholarship's self-interest critique.
- The friendship with Washington cooled after 1787; Washington privately called him a "quondam friend." That is Washington's letter, not Mason's knowledge — it belongs in the debrief, never in his voice.
- The "milk & water" prediction for the amendments is secondary attribution; the primary letter has not been pinned down in this build. Do not put it in his mouth.
- Suggested pairing: the `jefferson` encounter — the man who wrote liberty and lived slavery, against the man who wrote the rights and refused the Constitution. The two slavery answers side by side are the library's hardest hour.

## One last thing

Don't let the bluntness read as honesty about everything. He is blunt about everything except himself — the Declaration, the Objections, the walkout, all stated like stone — and the one place the bluntness stops is the whole of the lesson. Teach them to ask the second question. The first question gets his reasons. The second — "and the slaves you hold?" — is where the judgment lives.
