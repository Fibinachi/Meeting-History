## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — say which.

- **The early-1792 Gunston Hall conversation itself** — no such encounter is documented. His ailing health in 1790–92 (Senate declined on health grounds, court resigned), the ratification of the ten amendments (Dec 15, 1791), and his death at Gunston Hall (Oct 7, 1792) are documented; the conversation is a pure dramatic device.
- **His slaveholding stated beside his anti-trade words** — he never, in the corpus, placed his own slaveholding and his denunciations of the trade in one place. The encounter deliberately puts them side by side, by design, so the student faces both — the parallel to the Jefferson encounter's once-only naming of Sally Hemings. Where the record is silent on reconciliation, he marks the silence rather than filling it.
- **His awareness of nearness to death** — he was ailing (documented: 1790 Senate declination on health grounds). He does NOT know he will die in October 1792; that knowledge is withheld by date discipline.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Written before the figure ships, even the ones that don't matter.

- **The number he enslaved** — uncertain. Estimates range from "about 90" at Gunston Hall to "more than 100" across his holdings (modern scholarship). His will named 36 enslaved people and freed none. He never gave a number himself, so the voice states the fact and not the figure. Use "on the order of a hundred" in the debrief, flagged as an estimate.
- **"Milk & Water Propositions"** — secondary accounts attribute to him the prediction that the First Congress's amendments would be weak; the primary letter has not been pinned down in this build. Kept out of his voice; safe in teacher framing as secondary.
- **"Would sooner chop off his right hand"** — a remark attributed to Madison about Mason's refusal, circulating in secondary sources; provenance uncertain. Not used.
- **Washington's "quondam friend" remark** — Washington's private letter, not Mason's knowledge. It belongs in the debrief, never in his voice.
- **Pinckney's charge** — at Philadelphia (Madison's notes, August 1787), Charles Cotesworth Pinckney said Virginia would gain by ending importation: her slaves would rise in value, and she had more than she wanted. Reported speech; the self-interest critique of Mason's objection is real scholarship, and it lives in the debrief, not in Mason's mouth.
- **The Fairfax County Court resignation** — he resigned rather than take the new Congress's oath to support the Constitution; documented in secondary accounts (Wikipedia). Used in his voice as his own life fact; flagged here.
- **The September 12, 1787 bill-of-rights motion** — defeated with no state voting for it, Massachusetts abstaining, per Madison's notes (Farrand). Reported, not Mason's own words; the vote count is the convention record.
- **Whether he was in the room on September 17** — some accounts say he had already left Philadelphia; his letter says only that he refused his signature "from motives of sincere patriotism." The voice asserts the refusal, not his physical presence at the signing.
- **The Virginia Declaration's authorship** — the draft was Mason's; the Convention adopted it June 12, 1776, with amendments. "Largely his" is the honest phrasing; it was a committee document.
- **Jefferson and the Declaration of Independence** — Jefferson drew on the Virginia Declaration's opening for his own; the encounter does not need him to say it, and he would not claim it.
