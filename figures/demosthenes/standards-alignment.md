# Meet Demosthenes — Standards Alignment (verified 2026-10-15)

Encounter: the student sits as juror over the Athenian orator who spent thirty years warning Athens against Philip of Macedon — thundering in the assembly, quiet on Chaeronea, steel on the gold. His defense (*On the Crown*) and the jury's verdict (the Harpalus gold, 20 talents, fined 50) are held side by side and never resolved. It closes with the handoff question: *he tried to save Athens and took the gold — patriot or fraud? Judge him.*

**Grade band fit for every framework below: 9–12** (high school ELA literacy / Government / World History). Note: most states' *content* anchors for classical Greece sit in lower grades (see caveats); the 9–12 weight of this encounter comes from skills standards — rhetoric evaluation, deliberation, argumentation — which are band-matched.

---

## 1. C3 Framework (National Council for the Social Studies, 2013)

Grade band: **9–12**. (Indicator texts verified against curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim; the official NCSS PDF was not directly retrievable in this session — see verification note at end.)

This encounter's strongest C3 fits are **Dimension 4 and civics** — the whole design is a rhetoric trial — with history indicators doing the source work underneath.

### Dimension 2 — History

- **D2.His.11.9-12** — Critique the usefulness of historical sources for a specific historical inquiry based on their maker, date, place of origin, intended audience, and purpose. (Every surviving word of Demosthenes is adversarial oratory crafted to win — *On the Crown* to a jury, the Philippics to the assembly; the sources.md caveat states this outright. The gold beat forces the student to weigh his defense by maker, audience, and purpose: who is he speaking to when he says "nothing entered my house"?)
- **D2.His.9.9-12** — Analyze the relationship between historical sources and the secondary interpretations made from them. (His speeches vs. Aeschines's *Against Ctesiphon* — two primary accounts of the same career — vs. the jury's verdict vs. two millennia of "the perfect orator" reputation; the student sees how each layer of interpretation is built from the same disputed record.)
- **D2.His.16.9-12** — Integrate evidence from multiple relevant historical sources and interpretations into a reasoned argument about the past. (The closing question — patriot or fraud? — cannot be answered from any single source; it demands integrating the speeches, the prosecution's account, the conviction, and the debrief's plain record into one argument.)

### Dimension 2 — Civics

- **D2.Civ.9.9-12** — Use appropriate deliberative processes in multiple settings. (The encounter's frame is the deliberative process itself: he addresses the student as a juror, and the Philippics are Athenian assembly deliberation in action — the student practices citizen judgment over a contested public record.)

### Dimension 4 — Communicating Conclusions and Taking Informed Action

- **D4.1.9-12** — Construct arguments using precise and knowledgeable claims, with evidence from multiple sources, while acknowledging counterclaims and evidentiary weaknesses. (The closing judgment requires the student to hold the warnings-were-right evidence and the conviction simultaneously — acknowledging the evidentiary weakness on the gold, where the record is genuinely uncertain, rather than resolving it.)
- **D4.4.9-12** — Critique the use of claims and evidence in arguments for credibility. (The encounter's central discipline: evaluating a master rhetorician's claims — thunder on Philip, quiet on Chaeronea, steel on the gold — for what the tone and the evidence actually establish.)

*Verified against: resources.finalsite.net (Greenwich Public Schools C3 curriculum); mnhs.org and historicfortsnelling.com (Minnesota Historical Society standards-connections documents); learninglab.si.edu; closeup.org; teach.mikvachallenge.org. Official document: College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework for Social Studies State Standards, NCSS, 2013 (socialstudies.org/c3).*

## 2. Common Core ELA — Literacy in History/Social Studies, Speaking/Listening & Writing

Grade band: **9–12**. (Verified against the official Common Core State Standards Initiative site, thecorestandards.org.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 9–10

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.6** — Compare the point of view of two or more authors for how they treat the same or similar topics, including which details they include and emphasize in their respective accounts. (Demosthenes's *On the Crown* vs. Aeschines's *Against Ctesiphon*: both treat the crown decree and Chaeronea — the debrief notes they select and emphasize opposite details.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.1** — Cite specific textual evidence to support analysis of primary and secondary sources, attending to such features as the date and origin of the information. (The Harpalus sequence — 324 BC custody, Areopagus inquiry, 1,500-juror conviction — is built for date-and-origin citation.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.6** — Evaluate authors' differing points of view on the same historical event or issue by assessing the authors' claims, reasoning, and evidence. (The Harpalus charge: Demosthenes's "what I took, Athens spent" defense as attributed by the prosecution vs. Aeschines's framing — same event, opposed claims, each assessed on reasoning and evidence.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.3** — Evaluate various explanations for actions or events and determine which explanation best accords with textual evidence, acknowledging where the text leaves matters uncertain. (The gold is the textbook case: bribe-taker vs. public-money borrower vs. political frame-up — the encounter deliberately leaves the matter uncertain, and the standard asks students to sit in that uncertainty rather than fill it.)

### Speaking & Listening, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.1** — Initiate and participate effectively in a range of collaborative discussions (one-on-one, in groups, and teacher-led) with diverse partners on grades 11–12 topics, texts, and issues, building on others' ideas and expressing their own clearly and persuasively. (The encounter is a sustained one-on-one examiner-witness discussion; the student must press him clearly while he answers in character.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.3** — Evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric, assessing the stance, premises, links among ideas, word choice, points of emphasis, and tone used. (The load-bearing nudges train exactly this: thunder on Philip, the dropped voice on Chaeronea, the steel that refuses to plead on the gold — stance, word choice, and points of emphasis as evidence.)

### Writing, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.W.11-12.1** — Write arguments to support claims in an analysis of substantive topics or texts, using valid reasoning and relevant and sufficient evidence. (The closing question — patriot or fraud? — is designed as a post-encounter argument prompt, per the teacher instructions.)

## 3. South Carolina — Social Studies College- and Career-Ready Standards (2019)

Grade band: **9–12** for USG; Grade 6 for the classical-civilizations anchors.

- **USG.1.ER** (United States Government, Foundations of Democracy) — Analyze the philosophical influences on core political principles in the American constitutional democracy. (The encounter examines the Athenian assembly's deliberative practice — citizen oratory, jury judgment, the theoric-fund debate — the civic-philosophical lineage (direct democracy, rule of law, citizen deliberation) that the American founders studied and adapted.)
- **6.1.P** (Grade 6) — Analyze the shift from early to classical civilizations and the enduring contributions of classical civilizations. (Classical Athens is among the classical civilizations in the Grade 6 course's scope; Demosthenes's deliberative oratory is one of its enduring contributions. Grade-band caveat: this is SC's only classical-Greece content anchor, and it sits in Grade 6.)
- **6.1.E** (Grade 6) — Analyze multiple perspectives on the political, intellectual, and social achievements of classical societies through a variety of primary and secondary sources. (Demosthenes vs. Aeschines on the achievements of fourth-century Athens — primary sources in direct tension; the encounter's two-sides device does this work live.)

*Verified against: IXL's South Carolina standards pages (grade-6 and high-school social studies); the official 2019 standards text as reproduced in the SC State House Tour standards document (scprt.widen.net); commonplanner.com SC Grade 6 compilation. (The 2019 standards are sometimes dated "adopted 2020" in compilations — same document.)*

## 4. Other states (quick hits)

### New York (K–12 Social Studies Framework, Grades 9–10)

- **9.3b** — Empires used belief systems, systems of law, forms of government, military forces, and social hierarchies to consolidate and expand power. (The Philippics are Athens's alarm at exactly this process: Philip of Macedon using military force to consolidate the Greek cities — the encounter's central historical fact.)
- **9.3c** — A period of peace, prosperity, and cultural achievements can be designated as a Golden Age. (Students "examine the achievements of Greece" to determine whether the civilization experienced a Golden Age — fourth-century Athenian oratory and democratic deliberation are among the achievements under examination.)

*Caveat: 9.3b's performance guidance names Mauryan, Qin, and Roman civilizations as its exemplars — Philip's Macedon fits the indicator's general principle but is not a named exemplar. 9.3c names Greece explicitly.*

*Verified against: the NYS K–12 Social Studies Framework via nysacourse.weebly.com (official framework PDF mirror, revised February 2017).*

### Texas (TEKS, World History Studies, adopted 2022)

- **WH.1.B** (§113.42) — Identify major causes and describe the major effects of the following events from 500 BC to AD 600: the development of the classical civilizations of Greece, Rome, Persia, India (Maurya and Gupta), China (Zhou, Qin, and Han), and the development of major world religions. (Demosthenes's Athens and Philip's Macedon sit inside the 500 BC–AD 600 band; the encounter's cause-and-effect terrain is Athens's failed resistance to Macedonian expansion.)
