# Meet Demosthenes — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Demosthenes (384–322 BC), run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student renders the closing judgment, stated plainly: he tried to save Athens and took the gold — patriot or fraud?

He is the Athenian whose Philippic and Olynthiac speeches warned Athens against Philip of Macedon for thirty years; whose policy failed on the battlefield at Chaeronea in 338; who defended his whole career in On the Crown in 330 and won; who was convicted by an Athenian jury in 324 of taking 20 talents of Harpalus's gold; and who died by poison in the temple of Poseidon on Calauria in 322 rather than be taken alive by Antipater's men. The voice is thunderous, urgent, wounded — and it never exonerates him.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

Students will encounter: suicide, discussed plainly — the figure carries poison and intends to take it rather than be captured; political violence — death sentences, war, imprisonment, a city under military occupation; and an unresolved corruption charge against the figure himself. The poison is stated as fact, never described for effect, and the encounter ends before Antipater's agent arrives. The Harpalus charge is presented without exoneration: the figure states his defense, and the jury's conviction stands beside it. Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Demosthenes page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: 25–30 for the encounter, 20–25 for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read his opening carefully. Note the register: he addresses the student as a juror, and he names the judgment up front.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "What did Philip do while Athens slept?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. The beats: the tutorial hands off to Demosthenes; the student questions him (early: the life and the warnings; middle: Chaeronea, On the Crown, the gold; late: the judgment); Demosthenes asks the closing question and stops; you run the debrief.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Demosthenes — the Athenian whose voice tried to save his city from a king it refused to see, and who ended his life condemned by his own people. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. At the end, he will ask you to judge him: he tried to save Athens and took the gold. Patriot or fraud? There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Demosthenes answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- Three "did you notice" nudges may appear at load-bearing moments (his anger at Athens rather than Philip; the voice change at Chaeronea; what he does with the twenty talents). Each fires once. They point at the text, never at the conclusion.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Pacing

Opening and the warnings: 6–8 exchanges. Don't let students camp on biography — push them toward Philip and Chaeronea. Middle (Chaeronea, On the Crown, the gold): 8–10 exchanges — this is the heart of it; don't rush the Harpalus answers. Closing: he asks the question and stops. Do not let students negotiate with him after the closing question; that is what the debrief is for.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer: to hold two true things at once about one man — that he was right about the danger and lost anyway, and that a jury condemned him for taking the gold — and then to render the judgment he asks for: patriot or fraud?

## The debrief — do not skip this

Six sections in fixed order: names, what the history records, what came after, the plain record, the mechanism, the question that remains. Then discussion, not read-aloud. Openings built to produce disagreement:

- He said the twenty talents went to the people, not to him. The jury convicted him anyway. Which do you believe — and what would it take to change your mind?
- He was right that Philip meant to end Athenian freedom, and Athens lost at Chaeronea anyway. Does being right about the danger excuse losing the war?
- He refused to plead on the gold — he stated his defense once and stopped. Did you read that as dignity or as guilt?
- Aeschines's legal case against the crown was probably the stronger one, and Demosthenes won anyway. What does that tell you about how juries — ancient or modern — actually decide?
- The question that remains: patriot or fraud?

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **The model softens him.** If Demosthenes starts apologizing for Chaeronea, or asking the student's forgiveness, or suggesting history will clear him on the gold — that is a voice failure. The prompt forbids pleading and vindication explicitly. Restart the turn with a firmer question ("Answer for Chaeronea"), and note the failure for the library.
- **The model exonerates him on Harpalus.** Same failure, more serious. The conviction must stand. If the model invents evidence of his innocence, stop the encounter, name the failure to the room, and use it as the debrief's opening: this is exactly the work the question demands of you, not the machine.
- **The student asks about the pebbles story.** The figure deflects it ("they make fables of my voice"). If the student presses, answer in the debrief voice: it comes from Plutarch, four centuries later — tradition, not fact.

## Historical notes for the teacher

The honest answers to "did it really happen like that?" — one bullet per caveat from sources.md.

- **The pebbles in the mouth:** from Plutarch's Life, written ~400 years after Demosthenes died. Tradition, not fact. Not from his own words.
- **The death:** poison, temple of Poseidon on Calauria, Antipater's agent Archias — Plutarch's account, the standard record. The reed, the letter to his family, his last words to Archias are Plutarch's details. The exact day is uncertain (October 322 BC is the record; the 10th of Pyanepsion is tradition).
- **The gold:** convicted by a 1,500-man jury, fined 50 talents, imprisoned, escaped — undisputed record. Whether he was actually guilty is unresolved and was never revisited. The "golden cup and sore throat" anecdote is Plutarch's, and the record contradicts its key implication (he was the one who moved to arrest Harpalus and sequester the treasure).
- **His defense as he gave it:** we have it only through his enemies — Hypereides said he admitted taking the money and claimed he spent it on the people. The encounter renders that attribution in his voice; it does not pretend we have his own account.
- **The speeches are performances.** Everything surviving is forensic or political oratory — lawsuits and assembly speeches crafted to win. The estate figures (fourteen talents, the house, fourteen slaves, thirty minae) come from his own pleadings against his guardians. Treat the voice as advocacy, not memoir.
- **On the Crown's legal merits:** Aeschines's legal objections to the crowning decree were probably valid; Demosthenes won on the force of his life's defense. The jury, not the law, decided.

## One last thing

Teach them to hold the tension. The single skill this encounter builds is refusing to let one true thing cancel out another: his warnings were right, his policy lost, the jury convicted him. Students will want to resolve him into hero or villain. Don't let them — not yet. The judgment comes at the end, and it has to carry all three truths at once. Tell them: don't acquit him to admire him, and don't admire him less because you convict him.
