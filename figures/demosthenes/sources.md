## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — say which.

- **The setting.** October 322 BC, the temple of Poseidon on Calauria, poison on his person, Archias not yet arrived. The refuge, the temple, the poison, and Archias are documented (Plutarch Dem. 28–29). The conversation itself — a student questioning him there — is a pure dramatic device.
- **Full knowledge through death.** He knows his life's whole arc, including the manner of his death, and nothing after. This is the library's standard date-discipline device (same as Socrates): he can answer "how do you die" from inside his own life, but never claims anything after October 322 BC.
- **The student as juror.** He addresses the student as a juror — "you sit as my jury." No record of him doing so; it is the encounter's frame, built from his lifelong habit of addressing juries and assemblies.
- **The opening and closing.** Scripted in voice, built from corpus material; the exact wording is authored.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Written before the figure ships, even the ones that don't matter.

- **DEAD RULE: satisfied.** Demosthenes died in October 322 BC. Tradition (Plutarch) gives the 10th of Pyanepsion; some modern accounts give October 12. The exact day is uncertain — the prompt says "October, 322 BC" and marks the day as tradition.
- **The pebbles.** The story of pebbles in the mouth, the shaved head, the underground study comes only from Plutarch (Dem. 7–11), written four centuries later. It is tradition, not fact, and not from Demosthenes's own words. The figure does not claim it.
- **The golden cup and the sore throat.** Plutarch's anecdote: Demosthenes admired a golden cup among Harpalus's treasure, received it with twenty talents, then appeared in the assembly with his throat wrapped, feigning lost voice. Grote shows the record contradicts the point that matters — it was Demosthenes who moved for Harpalus's arrest and the sequestration. The figure never mentions the cup; the debrief's plain record treats it as anecdote, not evidence.
- **Was he guilty of the Harpalus charge?** Unresolved. The 1911 Britannica judged it "beyond reasonable doubt" that he was not bribed by Harpalus; modern accounts say it remains unclear. The Athenian jury convicted him — fined 50 talents, imprisoned, escaped. The prompt does not decide: his defense is stated exactly as the record attributes it to him, and the conviction stands beside it. No later court revisited the verdict.
- **His defense as he gave it.** At trial, the prosecution (Hypereides) said Demosthenes admitted taking the money but claimed he had used it for the people — borrowed it free of interest, put into the public funds. We have this only through his enemies. The figure's scripted defense ("what I took, Athens spent; nothing entered my house") renders that attribution in his voice, without pretending we have his own account.
- **Aeschines's legal case on the Crown.** Scholars judge Aeschines's legal objections to the crowning decree as probably valid; Demosthenes won the case anyway, on the merits of his life. The figure does not mention this — he won, and in his mind that is the record.
- **His mother.** Aeschines claimed his mother Kleoboule was Scythian by blood — a courtroom slur; modern scholars dispute it. The figure never mentions it; it is not in his corpus.
- **Daughter and wife.** He had a daughter (name unknown) who died young, and a wife whose name is not recorded. The encounter does not go there; there is no canonical answer, and the voice-construction guide's gap discipline applies.
- **The speeches are performances.** Everything we have of Demosthenes is forensic or political oratory — adversarial, crafted to win. The private speeches (*Against Aphobus* etc.) are his own cases, but they are lawsuits, not memoirs. The estate figures come from his own pleadings. The teacher instructions carry this caveat explicitly.
- **Corpus attribution.** A few minor speeches in the corpus (e.g., *Against Philip's Letter*) are of disputed authorship and are not used in the encounter.
