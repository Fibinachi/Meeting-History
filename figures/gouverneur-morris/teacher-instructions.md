# Meet Gouverneur Morris — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Gouverneur Morris (1752–1816), run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student renders the closing judgment, stated plainly: **he gave the republic its voice, and he never quite trusted the people to use it. Judge him.**

Morris is the most quotable founder and the least known: the penman of the Constitution, author of the Preamble's final words, diarist of the French Revolution, and an unapologetic aristocrat who wrote the charter of popular sovereignty. The voice charms; the debrief makes the students reckon with the charm. Tier: **OPEN**.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

Students will encounter, in plain period language: **slavery** — Morris calls enslaved people "slaves," denounces the institution as "nefarious," and then admits he signed the compromise that protected it; **the French Revolution's violence** — the September massacres, the guillotine, and mob killings are named plainly (never described for effect); **his candid love life** — he alludes to liaisons with married women in Paris, never explicitly; and **a grotesque death** — the disputed account that he died after using a sliver of whalebone as a catheter, which he alludes to with a joke. His aristocratic contempt for ordinary people is the encounter's central provocation and is not softened. **Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.**

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Gouverneur Morris page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: 25–30 for the encounter, 15–20 for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read Morris's opening carefully — it contains the entry hook (see below). **Do not spoil it for students beforehand.**
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: *"Who wrote 'We the People'?"* Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. The beats: the opening reveal (2–3 exchanges) → the Convention and the pen → the diary's Paris → the aristocrat-democrat contradiction → his closing question → the debrief.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with a founder you've probably never heard of. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. He wrote words you already know by heart, and he had opinions about the people he wrote them for that will not flatter you. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

**Do not tell students in advance that Morris wrote the Preamble.** The opening reveal — "We the People of the United States... Mine." — is the entry hook the encounter is built on. Students know the words; the discovery is the hand.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Morris answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.
- If a student stalls, Morris hands them a question in character ("Ask me about the leg. Everyone wants to ask about the leg."). Point stalled students at the bracketed suggestions.

### Pacing

Opening reveal: 2–3 exchanges. Don't let students camp here — the hook is the doorway, not the room. Convention and the pen: ~6–8 exchanges. The diary's Paris: ~5–6. The contradiction and the close: ~4–5, ending on his scripted closing question. If students are still asking factual questions at 25 minutes, steer them to the Late pool: the judgment is the destination.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer: meet the hand behind the Preamble, hear him admit he distrusted the people he wrote it for, and decide whether writing "We the People" while doubting the people makes those words truer or cheaper.

## The debrief — do not skip this

The six fixed sections, in order: (1) name him — Gouverneur Morris (1752–1816); (2) what the history records — penman of the Constitution, minister to France through the Terror, lost his leg in 1780; (3) what came after — the diary published in 1939, his reputation revived by biographers, the Preamble famous while his name vanished from it; (4) the plain record — he wrote "We the People" and held the people in contempt, denounced slavery and signed its protection, sheltered aristocrats and recorded the Terror with detachment; (5) the mechanism — the wit was the instrument, and the judgment must be rendered against the charm, not with it; (6) the question that remains — he gave the republic its voice and never trusted the people to use it; judge him.

Discussion openings (built to produce disagreement):

- He told you plainly that he distrusted the people. Does his honesty make him better or worse than a founder who flattered the people and meant it less?
- He denounced slavery as "a nefarious institution" and then signed the compromise that protected it. Is that courage, cowardice, or politics?
- "We the People" — do the words belong to the man who wrote them, or to everyone who has read them since?
- He sheltered aristocrats from the guillotine while recording the Revolution's victims with detachment. Was his sympathy with the victims of the Terror, or with his own class?
- Was the wit wisdom — or was the wit the disguise?

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **The model softens his elitism.** This is the known failure mode and the encounter's load-bearing risk: a model that makes Morris warm toward "the people" or apologetic about the Convention has broken the voice. The prompt forbids it explicitly; if it happens, regenerate or restart — do not let a softened Morris stand.
- **The model moralizes in modern terms.** Morris never uses modern vocabulary about himself. Same remedy: regenerate or restart.
- **Not yet tested on live models.** No model-specific quirks are recorded for this figure. Note what you find during your solo test drive.

## Historical notes for the teacher

The honest answers to "did it really happen like that?" — one bullet per caveat from sources.md.

- **Preamble authorship:** it was committee work he drafted, not sole authorship. He was the Committee of Style's pen; Madison said "the finish given to the style and arrangement of the Constitution fairly belongs to the pen of Mr. Morris."
- **The speeches are reported, not verbatim:** everything from the Convention floor comes through Madison's Notes — substantially his, not tape recordings.
- **The leg:** the documented cause is the May 14, 1780 carriage accident (amputation below the knee, age 28). The lover's-window story is rumor; the figure debunks it himself.
- **The lamppost story** (waving his wooden leg at a Paris mob) is a biographers' anecdote, not in his diary. Treat it as legend the figure enjoys, not record.
- **Slavery:** the record is genuinely murky. Slaveholding family; he manumitted what came to him and joined the manumission society; one popular account claims the 1810 census shows him owning two. Don't resolve it for students — the uncertainty is the lesson.
- **The death:** date and place are firm (Nov 6, 1816, Morrisania). The whalebone-catheter mechanism comes from a friend's letter and is rated "not verifiable" — grotesque, possibly true, not certain.
- **Not the Declaration:** he did not sign it (his half-brother Lewis did); he is not related to Robert Morris the financier.

## Standards alignment

Grade band 8–12; strongest in civics/government (origins and principles of the Constitution), U.S. history (founding era; the French Revolution's American reception), and ELA (primary-source voice, rhetoric, point of view). A full per-figure alignment document will be added separately.

## One last thing

Teach them to ask the second question. When Morris says "We the People" was his, the first question is "really?" — the second is "who did you think the people were?" Everything load-bearing in this encounter lives in the second question.
