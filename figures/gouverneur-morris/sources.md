## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — say which.

- **The setting: Morris at Morrisania in November 1816, speaking across his whole life with knowledge through his death.** Pure dramatic device — the library's standard frame. No such deathbed interview occurred.
- **The lamppost story** (mob surrounds him; he waves his wooden leg and proclaims himself an American wounded for liberty; the crowd cheers). Reported anecdote from Duff Cooper's *Talleyrand* (1923) via later biographers — **not in Morris's diary or letters**. The figure may tell it as a story others tell; the prompt does not present it as his recorded words.
- **The lover's-window version of the amputation.** Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century rumor; the documented record is the May 14, 1780 carriage accident. The figure explicitly debunks the rumor in voice.
- **The Preamble rationale** ("the draft named the states one by one; the Committee dropped the list partly because no one could know which states would ratify"). The standard scholarly explanation for the change — **not Morris's recorded explanation**. Marked as such in the prompt's source line.
- **The whalebone-catheter death mechanism.** Reported in a friend's (Rufus King's) letter of November 5, 1816; Snopes rates the claim "not verifiable"; historians treat it as the accepted account with thin sourcing. The figure states the date and place firmly and attributes the mechanism to the letter, with the caveat written here and in teacher instructions.
- **Paraphrase of his diary's language toward servants.** The diary's period vocabulary is archaic; the prompt keeps the voice to "slaves" and "servants" per the guardrails (no slurs, paraphrase around them).

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Written before the figure ships, even the ones that don't matter.

- **Did Morris really write the Preamble?** He was the pen of the five-man Committee of Style (Morris, Hamilton, Madison, William Samuel Johnson, Rufus King), September 8–12, 1787. Catherine Drinker Bowen called him the committee's "amanuensis"; Madison later wrote that "the finish given to the style and arrangement of the Constitution fairly belongs to the pen of Mr. Morris." It was committee work that he drafted — "penman," not sole author. The seven famous words are his drafting.
- **His Convention speeches are reported, not verbatim.** Everything "he said on the floor" comes through Madison's Notes (published 1840), plus Farrand's other records. Madison paraphrased and condensed; treat the famous lines as substantially his, not as tape recordings.
- **How many speeches?** Counts differ: 171 (some Madison-based counts), 173 (National Constitution Center). The figure refuses to pick a number.
- **The leg: carriage accident is the documented cause** (May 14, 1780, Philadelphia; amputated below the knee; he was 28). The lover's-window story is rumor, contradicted by contemporary correspondence. Mark the rumor as rumor if a student raises it.
- **Was he a slaveholder?** The honest answer is murky, and teachers should know it. Born into a slaveholding family (Morrisania was worked by enslaved labor); he manumitted enslaved people who came to him and was a founding member of the New York Manumission Society with John Jay; the Columbia & Slavery project finds no record of him holding slaves at Morrisania. Against that: one popular account cites the 1810 census as showing him owning two. The scholarship does not fully agree. The encounter's plain record states the undisputed parts and does not resolve the disputed part.
- **Pronunciation.** Modern scholarship pronounces his first name like the word "governor."
- **The marriage.** Anne Cary "Nancy" Randolph, married December 25, 1809; she was about twenty years younger and carried a Virginia scandal (an 1793 infanticide accusation against her and her brother-in-law; acquittal). One son, Gouverneur Morris Jr. The encounter keeps this to one canonical answer; it is not a load-bearing thread.
- **His late politics.** He opposed the War of 1812, supported the Hartford Convention (1814), chaired the Erie Canal commission (1810–16), and by some accounts came to judge the Constitution a failure. None of this is load-bearing for the encounter; the figure will answer if asked, from his letters.
- **He was not related to Robert Morris**, the financier, despite the shared surname — a confusion worth heading off.
- **He did not sign the Declaration of Independence.** His half-brother Lewis Morris did. Students will conflate them; the figure corrects it.
