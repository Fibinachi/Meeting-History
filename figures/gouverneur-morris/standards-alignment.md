# Meet Gouverneur Morris — Standards Alignment (verified 2026-10-15)

Encounter: the student meets Gouverneur Morris (1752–1816) at Morrisania in November 1816 — the peg-legged New York wit who spoke more than any other delegate at the Constitutional Convention and then, as the pen of the five-man Committee of Style, put the frame into its final words, including "We the People of the United States." He also told the Convention that "there never was, nor ever will be, a civilized society without an aristocracy," distrusted the people he gave the voice to, denounced slavery as "a nefarious institution" on August 8, 1787, and signed the compromise that protected it anyway. The Terror he watched from his Paris windows is his evidence. It closes with the judgment: *he gave the republic its voice, and he never quite trusted the people to use it. Judge him.*

**Grade band fit for every framework below: 9–12** (high school U.S. History / Government / ELA literacy). Founding-era content sits in grades 7–8 in most states (see caveats); South Carolina places it at 9–12.

## 1. C3 Framework (National Council for the Social Studies, 2013)

Grade band: **9–12**. (Indicator texts verified against multiple independent curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim; the official NCSS PDF was not directly retrievable in this session — see verification note at end.)

### Dimension 2 — Civics

- **D2.Civ.3.9-12** — Analyze the impact of constitutions, laws, treaties, and international agreements on the maintenance of national and international order. (The Committee of Style's final drafting: the articles named the states one by one, and Morris wrote "We the People of the United States" instead — authority from the people, not a list. Madison said the finish given to the style and arrangement fairly belonged to Morris's pen.)
- **D2.Civ.10.9-12** — Analyze the impact and appropriate roles of personal interests and perspectives on the application of civic virtues, democratic principles, constitutional rights, and human rights. (His August 7, 1787 suffrage speech — give the votes to people who have no property and they will sell them — his aristocratic perspective applied to democratic principles; and the diary's Parisian aristocrats sheltered while the sans-culottes are "the mob.")
- **D2.Civ.4.9-12** — Explain how the U.S. Constitution establishes a system of government that has powers, responsibilities, and limits that have changed over time and that are still contested. (His design arguments on the floor — a strong executive, restricted suffrage, his "endeavor was only to keep [aristocracy] from doing mischief" — measured against the frame he signed with most of it lost.)

### Dimension 2 — History

- **D2.His.5.9-12** — Analyze how historical contexts shaped and continue to shape people's perspectives. (The September Massacres and the guillotine, watched from his Paris windows as the only foreign envoy who would not run, shaping his fear of the multitude — "a republic suits America; I told Paris plainly it did not suit France.")
- **D2.His.11.9-12** — Critique the usefulness of historical sources for a specific historical inquiry based on their maker, date, place of origin, intended audience, and purpose. (Madison's Notes as reported, not verbatim speech — "substantially his, not tape recordings"; the diary kept "for myself, not for you" — maker and audience do real work in this encounter.)

### Dimension 4 — Communicating Conclusions and Taking Informed Action

- **D4.1.9-12** — Construct arguments using precise and knowledgeable claims, with evidence from multiple sources, while acknowledging counterclaims and evidentiary weaknesses. (The closing judgment — was the wit wisdom or disguise? — is this standard as a student task: the Preamble, the floor speeches, the diary, and the signature are the evidence.)

## 2. Common Core ELA

### Reading: Informational Text / History-Social Studies, 11–12 and 9–10

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RI.11-12.9** — Analyze 17th-, 18th-, and 19th-century foundational U.S. documents of historical and literary significance for themes, purposes, and rhetorical features. (The Preamble as founding document — his cadence, his long periodic sentences, the famous seven words as rhetorical design; the standard explicitly names founding documents, and the Preamble is the founding document he drafted.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.6** — Evaluate authors' differing points of view on the same historical event or issue by assessing the claims, reasoning, and evidence. (His August 8, 1787 denunciation of slavery as "a nefarious institution, the curse of heaven on the states where it prevailed" against his signature on the compromise that protected it — the same author, two positions on the same issue.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.8** — Evaluate an author's premises, claims, and evidence by corroborating or challenging them with other information. (His claim "I would never concur in upholding domestic slavery" tested against the signature; his diary's casual counting of Black servants against the manumissions he made.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.9** — Compare and contrast treatments of the same topic in several primary and secondary sources. (The leg: the lover's-window rumor against the May 14, 1780 carriage accident in contemporary correspondence — the encounter teaches sourcing through gossip.)

### Speaking & Listening, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.3** — Evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric, assessing the stance, premises, links among ideas, word choice, points of emphasis, and tone used. (The wit is the rhetoric: epigrams, long periodic sentences, short stabs to score points — students must evaluate whether the charm is evidence or misdirection. "The wit was the instrument.")

### Writing, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.W.11-12.1** — Write arguments to support claims in an analysis of substantive topics or texts, using valid reasoning and relevant and sufficient evidence. (The closing judgment is designed as a post-encounter argument prompt, per the teacher instructions.)

## 3. South Carolina — Social Studies College- and Career-Ready Standards (2019)

Course: **United States History and the Constitution** (9–12).

- **USHC.1.CE** — Assess the major developments of the American Revolution through significant turning points in the debates over independence and self-government from 1763–1791. (The official development notes explicitly name the Constitution among the founding documents in scope — the Convention's most frequent speaker, then the Committee of Style's final draft.)
- **USHC.1.P** — Summarize the changing relationship between individuals and the government during the period 1607–1800. (His creed in voice: "The people are the fountain of power, and power in their hands is the most dangerous of all" — the individual-to-government relationship as he understood it.)
- **USHC.1.E** — Utilize primary and secondary sources to investigate the impact of the Atlantic influence in the regional and national development of republicanism and federalism. (The diary of the French Revolution, the Madison-reported speeches, the Paris dispatches — the encounter's evidence base, and the D2.His.11 sourcing questions.)
- **USG.1.IN** — Interpret founding documents and principles that led to the creation of the American constitutional democracy. (The Preamble — his drafting, its cadence, its "We the People.")

## Verification caveats

1. **C3 Framework**: indicator texts were verified against multiple independent curriculum documents that quote the Framework verbatim (they agree with each other); the official NCSS PDF itself was not directly retrievable this session. Treat the official publisher as NCSS (2013), socialstudies.org/c3.
2. **Grade-band split.** Only South Carolina places the founding era in 9–12 courses (USHC / US Government). New York, Texas, Florida, and California all teach the Revolution and founding documents in grades 7–8. The honest high-school anchors outside SC are: Texas §113.41(b)(1) (Celebrate Freedom Week) and Florida's SS.912.A.1 inquiry benchmarks (the method, not the content).
3. **USG.1.IN** was verified against a secondary compilation (SC State House Tour standards doc) rather than ed.sc.gov directly; recheck before citing in print.
4. **Convention words are Madison's reporting** (per the figure's own sources.md); the RH.11-12.6 and D2.His.11 fits should be framed as reported speech, not transcript.
5. **The Preamble rationale is scholarly, not his** (per sources.md — the standard explanation for dropping the state names, not Morris's recorded explanation). The D2.Civ.3 fit is the drafting act and its effect, not his stated motive.
6. **No standard asks the encounter's question.** Every code above covers the Preamble, the Convention, the sources, or the diary — none asks whether writing "We the People" while distrusting the people makes those words truer or cheaper. The judgment is the library's design, not a standards artifact; the standards supply the evidence base.
