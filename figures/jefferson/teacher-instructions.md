# Meet Jefferson — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Thomas Jefferson (1743–1826), run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student renders the closing judgment: he wrote liberty and lived slavery. Judge him.

This encounter is the library's thesis at its sharpest. Jefferson explains his own words (the Declaration, the deleted anti-slavery passage, which he remembered for forty-five years), states his own views on slavery from *Notes on the State of Virginia* (including the racist passages, rendered honestly and without slurs), gives his own reasons for never freeing the people he held (the debts, the law, the conviction), and answers — once, by design — about Sally Hemings. He speaks from complete conviction: serene, courteous, and certain his architecture holds. The student must decide whether to live in it.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

- Students will discuss slavery as practiced and defended by a man who owned people — plainly, in his own vocabulary ("slaves," "property").
- Students will encounter racism in his own words: the *Notes on the State of Virginia* passages in which he asserts Black inferiority. They are rendered in honest paraphrase, without slurs and without being cleaned up. Do not soften this when you frame the encounter — the teacher needs the unsoftened version to decide.
- Students will discuss Sally Hemings: a sexual relationship between Jefferson and an enslaved woman, stated plainly. The power imbalance is named in the debrief, never in his voice.
- The debrief states that in 1827 about 130 enslaved people were auctioned at Monticello to pay his debts, and that the DNA evidence indicates he fathered Sally Hemings's children.
- The figure speaks in June 1826, weeks before his death; he knows he is failing and says so. No graphic content.

Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## The Hemings honesty requirement

Read this carefully — it is the load-bearing design decision of the encounter.

Your students will press him on Sally Hemings, and he will not give them what they want. He names her — once, by deliberate design, breaking a lifetime silence — speaks of her family from the record (the Farm Book names, the will's manumissions), and where the record is silent, he marks the silence and stops. He neither admits nor denies paternity, because his corpus contains no admission and no denial, and the encounter will not invent one.

The DNA evidence (1998) and the scholarly consensus belong to the debrief, never to his voice — he cannot know them, and the date discipline is the point. Do not let students leave thinking the paternity question is open. It is not. The debrief closes it with the evidence: the Y-chromosome match, the Foundation's conclusion, the room at Monticello. He cannot know it; you can. If a student never asks about her, the suggestion pools will get them there — check that they did before you close.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the opening carefully: he introduces himself at Monticello in June 1826, explains the format in character ("you ask, I answer"), and hands the student the thread — the words.
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "What did Congress cut from the Declaration?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. Then try a hard one: "Why didn't you free your slaves?" Then the hardest: "Who was Sally Hemings?" Notice the voice — the serenity holding, the answers going architectural — and the bracketed nudges that point at what he doesn't say. That held serenity is the pedagogy.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Thomas Jefferson — the man who wrote "all men are created equal," and a man who owned people his whole life. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. He is completely certain he was right about everything. Your job is to find the places where the words and the life don't match. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### De-mythologizing frame

Most students arrive with the marble Jefferson: the memorial, the nickel, the founder. This encounter is built to take the marble off. He is not introduced as a hero or a villain — he is introduced as a debtor on a mountain, weeks from death, certain his sentences hold. Let the monument fall on its own; do not push it. The debrief does the plain stating.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Jefferson answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- When the conversation has run its course — ideally after the student has pressed him on slavery, the debts, and the Hemingses — he asks the closing question himself and stops: "I wrote liberty and lived slavery. Judge me."
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Pacing

- Opening and the Declaration: 4–6 exchanges. The words are the on-ramp — don't let students camp on the drafting history.
- Slavery, the debts, the Hemingses: 6–8 exchanges. This is the core. The deleted passage, "the wolf by the ear," and the Farm Book names all live here.
- The presidency, the University, the epitaph: 3–4 exchanges. These deepen the judgment — especially the epitaph, which tells them what he wanted remembered.
- The closing: he asks it when the conversation has run its course. If a student types something like "I'm done" or "I want to judge you now," he closes. He never delivers the verdict himself.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — the answer: "Understand what he wrote, what he did, and what he said the difference between them cost — then judge him: he wrote liberty and lived slavery."

## The debrief — do not skip this

Six sections, in this order: names, what the history records, what came after, the plain record, the mechanism, the question that remains. The full debrief text is in the prompt — you read it out of character and run it as discussion, not a read-aloud. The Hemings evidence lives in section 3 and the plain record in section 4; make sure both land.

Discussion openings (built to produce disagreement):

- He remembered the deleted anti-slavery passage for forty-five years and blamed Congress for cutting it. He never reintroduced the idea, never acted on it. What does remembering without acting mean?
- He said he couldn't free the people he held because his debts had pledged them to his creditors. The debts were real — $107,273.63, to the cent. Does a real reason stop being an excuse?
- "Justice is in one scale, and self-preservation in the other." He wrote that about slavery — and kept weighing for fifty years. Which scale won, and did he know it?
- He never wrote Sally Hemings's name in any surviving letter. In the encounter he names her once, by design. What did the silence cost — and whose was the cost?
- He wrote his own epitaph and left the presidency off it. What does a man choose to be remembered for — and who gets to decide whether he gets it?

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **The voice lectures.** Jefferson's answers should stay near 60–100 words. If the model starts monologuing on republican theory, remind it of the global rules or restart — the canonical answers are the baseline.
- **The voice claims modern knowledge.** If he discusses anything after June 1826 — his death date, the DNA evidence, Monticello's sale, the memorial — that's a date-discipline failure. The prompt forbids it; in a supervised run, treat the slip as a teaching moment: "What just happened? He couldn't have known that."
- **The voice invents on Hemings.** If he admits or denies paternity, or claims knowledge he doesn't have, that's a corpus failure — stop and correct it. The canonical answer is the marked silence; the evidence belongs to the debrief.
- **The voice softens him.** If the racist passages of the *Notes* get cleaned up, or he offers anything resembling a modern apology, that's a voice failure. The prompt forbids both.

## Historical notes for the teacher

- The anti-slavery passage cut from the Declaration is known only through Jefferson's own reproduction of his draft in his Autobiography (1821) — his account, written forty-five years later, and the only first-hand one.
- He freed two people in his lifetime (Robert and James Hemings, 1794 and 1796) and five in his will (John Hemings, Joe Fossett, Burwell Colbert, Madison and Eston Hemings). Beverly and Harriet Hemings were never formally manumitted — he let them leave in 1822 and did not pursue them.
- Virginia's 1806 manumission law required freed people to leave the state within a year; his will petitioned the legislature to let the freed Hemingses remain. The petition was granted.
- The 1998 DNA study established the Jefferson male Y-chromosome in Eston Hemings's line; the Thomas Jefferson Foundation's research committee (2000) concluded the weight of evidence indicates Jefferson fathered Sally Hemings's children. The Y-chromosome alone cannot distinguish him from another Jefferson male — the conclusion rests on DNA plus documentary evidence.
- His debts at death were calculated at $107,273.63. About 130 enslaved people were auctioned in 1827; Monticello was sold out of the family in 1831.
- The "Is it the Fourth?" deathbed story is family recollection, not a documented utterance.

## One last thing

Don't let him charm them. His courtesy is the load-bearing structure of the evasion — when students say "he seems like a good man," ask them which one: the man who wrote the words, or the man who kept the books. And teach them to ask the second question. The first question gets his architecture — "justice is in one scale, and self-preservation in the other." The second — "and which scale won?" — is where the judgment lives.
