## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — say which.

- **The June-1826 Monticello conversation itself** — no such encounter is documented. His failing health in June 1826 and the Weightman letter of 24 June 1826 are documented; the conversation is a pure dramatic device.
- **His naming Sally Hemings aloud** — he never wrote her name in any surviving letter. The encounter deliberately breaks his lifetime silence once, by design, so the student can face her. His answers stay strictly within what the record holds (the Farm Book names, the will's manumissions); where the record is silent, he marks the silence rather than filling it.
- **His awareness of nearness to death** — documented in his June 1826 letters (he declined the anniversary invitation on health grounds). He does NOT know he will die on July 4, the fiftieth anniversary; that knowledge is withheld from him by date discipline.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Written before the figure ships, even the ones that don't matter.

- **The "Is it the Fourth?" deathbed story** — family members recalled him asking, on July 3, whether it was the Fourth. It is recollection, not a documented utterance; the encounter does not use it. Mark as uncertain.
- **The number he enslaved** — "more than 600 across his lifetime" is the commonly cited estimate, not an exact count. The exact figure at death (~130) is better documented.
- **Sally Hemings's own words** — none survive. No portrait of her survives; descriptions of her come from others. Everything the encounter says about her inner life is deliberately absent.
- **The paternity consensus** — the 1998 DNA study (Foster et al., *Nature*) established the Jefferson male Y-chromosome in Eston Hemings's descendant line. A Y-chromosome match alone cannot distinguish Thomas Jefferson from another Jefferson male; the Foundation's conclusion rests on the DNA plus documentary and statistical evidence (his documented presence at Monticello at each conception). "Most likely fathered all six children" is the honest strength of the claim.
- **Monticello's 1831 sale price** — commonly given as $7,000 to James Turner Barclay; some accounts differ slightly. Marked approximate.
- **The deleted Declaration passage** — we know it only through Jefferson's own reproduction of his draft in his Autobiography (1821), written 45 years after the fact. It is his account, and it is the only first-hand account.
- **"All men are created equal"** — the phrase survived Congress's editing largely intact, but the draft underwent heavy cutting (about a quarter removed). His authorship of the draft is undisputed; the adopted text is a committee document.
- **Robert and James Hemings** — freed in his lifetime (1794 and 1796 respectively); the only two he manumitted while living. Beverly and Harriet Hemings were not formally manumitted — he allowed them to leave in 1822 and did not pursue them.
