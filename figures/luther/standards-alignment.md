# Meet Luther — Standards Alignment (verified 2026-10-15)

Encounter: the student presses Martin Luther (November 1545) on the 95 Theses and Tetzel's indulgence trade, the stand at Worms, justification by faith (*On the Freedom of a Christian*, 1520), the Peasants' War of 1525 ("smite, slay and stab"), and — stated plainly, never softened — the 1543 treatise *On the Jews and Their Lies* and its seven measures, which he never retracted. It closes with his scripted question: *the reformer whose words fed both liberation and slaughter. Judge him.*

**Grade band fit for every framework below: 9–12** (high school World History / ELA literacy). The Reformation content anchors here are on-band in high school — this is one of the few figures in the library whose content standards don't sit in Grade 6. Tier: guided.

**Honesty note on this encounter's content.** The antisemitic writings and the Peasants' War are the encounter, not background — the standards below are chosen for what they actually teach: religious conflict, persecution, the uses of text, and the moral judgment of historical actors. Nothing below is softened to make a standard fit, and no standard is stretched to cover the 1543 treatise where the fit isn't genuine. Where a standard names Luther, it is quoted. Where a standard covers persecution of Jews, it is cited as an adjacent anchor, not as if it covered his treatise.

## 1. C3 Framework (National Council for the Social Studies, 2013)

Grade band: **9–12**. (Indicator texts verified against independent curriculum documents quoting the Framework verbatim; the official NCSS PDF was not directly retrievable in this session — see verification note at end.)

### Dimension 2 — History

- **D2.His.4.9-12** — Analyze complex and interacting factors that influenced the perspectives of people during different historical eras. (The peasants heard liberation in his Gospel and took up the sword; he heard rebellion against God's order and called down the princes' swords — interacting factors producing two readings of one text.)
- **D2.His.6.9-12** — Analyze the ways in which the perspectives of those writing history shaped the history that they produced. (His euphemism "harsh mercy" for the seven measures is his framing; the debrief refuses to adopt it. Table Talk is students' notes of his table conversation — recorded by others, with all the distortion that implies, per the encounter's own caveats.)
- **D2.His.11.9-12** — Critique the usefulness of historical sources for a specific historical inquiry based on their maker, date, place of origin, intended audience, and purpose. (The 1543 treatise as princely advice — addressed to rulers, prescribing state violence; the 1523 tract as conversion outreach; the debrief's honest disagreement among scholars about the treatise's centuries-long influence.)
- **D2.His.14.9-12** — Analyze multiple and complex causes and effects of events in the past. (The Reformation's fragmentation of Christendom — religious division, political conflict, the century of religious war the debrief records — is a multiple-causes, multiple-effects event, and his words are one of the causes.)
- **D2.His.16.9-12** — Integrate evidence from multiple relevant historical sources and interpretations into a reasoned argument about the past. (The closing judgment must integrate the theses, the peasants pamphlet, the 1543 treatise, and the debrief's post-1546 record — including the Nazi use and the Lutheran denunciations.)

### Dimension 4 — Communicating Conclusions and Taking Informed Action

- **D4.1.9-12** — Construct arguments using precise and knowledgeable claims, with evidence from multiple sources, while acknowledging counterclaims and evidentiary weaknesses. (The judgment: liberation and slaughter from one mouth, with the evidentiary honesty the encounter models — e.g., "Here I stand" is marked as uncertain, not asserted.)
- **D4.4.9-12** — Critique the use of claims and evidence in arguments for credibility. (His certainty is the evidence — "he states his reasons at full strength, and the reasons are the indictment" — and the student must critique the credibility of his self-defense without borrowing modern vocabulary he never used.)

*Verified against: crfcap.org Washington State Standards/C3 dashboard; cdn.fairfieldschools.org district C3 documents; c3teachers.org blueprint documents. Official document: College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework for Social Studies State Standards, NCSS, 2013 (socialstudies.org/c3).*

## 2. Common Core ELA — Literacy in History/Social Studies, Speaking/Listening, Writing

Grade band: **9–12**. (Verified against the official Common Core State Standards Initiative site, thecorestandards.org.)

### Reading in History/Social Studies, 9–10 and 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.1 / RH.11-12.1** — 9–10: "Cite specific textual evidence to support analysis of primary and secondary sources, attending to such features as the date and origin of the information." / 11–12: "Cite specific textual evidence to support analysis of primary and secondary sources, connecting insights gained from specific details to an understanding of the text as a whole." (The corpus is primary texts — the 95 Theses, the 1525 pamphlet, the 1543 treatise — and date/origin discipline matters: the 1523 tract and the 1543 tract are twenty years apart and must not be blurred.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.2 / RH.11-12.2** — 9–10: "Determine the central ideas or information of a primary or secondary source; provide an accurate summary of how key events or ideas develop over the course of the text." / 11–12: "Determine the central ideas or information of a primary or secondary source; provide an accurate summary that makes clear the relationships among the key details and ideas." (Justification by faith — "a Christian is the most free lord of all, subject to none; a Christian is the most dutiful servant of all, subject to all" — and, separately, the seven measures of 1543, stated plainly.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.9-10.6 / RH.11-12.6** — 9–10: "Compare the point of view of two or more authors for how they treat the same or similar topics, including which details they include and emphasize in their respective accounts." / 11–12: "Evaluate authors' differing points of view on the same historical event or issue by assessing the authors' claims, reasoning, and evidence." (The 1523 tract urging kindness toward the Jews vs. the 1543 tract prescribing state violence against them — his point of view on the same people, compared across his own writings; his account of the Peasants' War vs. the chroniclers' death count, which he refuses to keep.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.RH.11-12.9** — Integrate information from diverse sources, both primary and secondary, into a coherent understanding of an idea or event, noting discrepancies among sources. (The debrief's honest record: the Nazi display of the treatise at Nuremberg rallies, Streicher's trial statement, the Lutheran churches' post-1980s denunciations, and the scholarly disagreement about how continuous the treatise's influence was in between — discrepancies the encounter refuses to flatten.)

### Speaking & Listening, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.1** — Initiate and participate effectively in a range of collaborative discussions (one-on-one, in groups, and teacher-led) with diverse partners on grades 11–12 topics, texts, and issues, building on others' ideas and expressing their own clearly and persuasively. (The encounter is a sustained one-on-one examination of a witness who never moderates his tone to be liked.)
- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.SL.11-12.3** — Evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric, assessing the stance, premises, links among ideas, word choice, points of emphasis, and tone used. (The debrief teaches students to notice what he does with guilt — moving it onto the peasants, onto Christians who "shield" Jews — and what he never calls the seven measures except "harsh mercy.")

### Writing, 11–12

- **CCSS.ELA-Literacy.W.11-12.1** — Write arguments to support claims in an analysis of substantive topics or texts, using valid reasoning and relevant and sufficient evidence. (The closing judgment — the reformer whose words fed both liberation and slaughter — is designed as a post-encounter argument prompt. The tier is guided: the debrief supplies the post-1546 record the figure cannot know.)

## 3. South Carolina — Social Studies College- and Career-Ready Standards (2019): Modern World History

Grade band: **9–12** — on-band. (Indicator texts verified via a commonplanner compilation of the official 2019 standards PDF and the HMH Modern World History correlation to the 2019 standards; the ed.sc.gov PDF was not fully re-opened this session — see caveats.)

- **MWH.2.CX** — Contextualize the impact of the Scientific Revolution and Enlightenment in expanding global interactions in commerce and innovations. (The indicator's official inquiry note explicitly names "the philosophies surrounding the Scientific Revolution, the Enlightenment, and the **Protestant Reformation in Europe**" — the honest SC 9–12 anchor for Luther content; the encounter covers the Reformation's religious and political upheaval that the inquiry note points at.)
- **MWH.1.CO** — Compare and contrast the major political, social, and belief systems and their spatial distribution in the early modern world. (The Catholic/Protestant split of Christendom — the encounter's 1517–1546 arc is the splitting itself; 7.9.4 in California's parallel anchor maps the same division.)
- **MWH.2.E** — Utilize a variety of primary and secondary sources to analyze multiple perspectives on global interactions. (The encounter's corpus — theses, treatises, catechisms, Table Talk, letters — is a primary-source set built for exactly this skill; the 1523-vs-1543 comparison is multiple perspectives from one author.)

## 4. Other states (quick hits)

Grade band: **9–12** for NY/TX/FL; Grade 7 for CA (caveat stated).

### New York (K–12 Social Studies Framework, 2014, Grades 9–10)

- **9.9b** — "The Reformation challenged traditional religious authority, which prompted a counter reformation that led to a religiously fragmented Western Europe and political conflicts. This religious upheaval continued the marginalization of Jews in European society." (Verified verbatim against the official NYS framework PDF via the ewsdonline.org mirror. The content specification names "the roles of key individuals, including **Martin Luther**, John Calvin, Elizabeth I, and Ignatius Loyola" and directs students to "trace the discrimination against and persecution of Jews." This is the strongest single state anchor in the library for this encounter: it names Luther and it covers the persecution thread without sanitizing it. Note: the task brief suggested 9.5b; the Reformation indicator is actually 9.9b — verified, not assumed.)

### Texas (TEKS, World History Studies, adopted 2022; §113.42)

- **WH.5.B** — Explain the political, intellectual, artistic, economic, and religious impact of the Reformation. (Verified against the commonplanner compilation of the 2022-adopted TEKS; the full knowledge statement reads "The student understands the causes, characteristics, and impact of the European Renaissance and the Reformation from 1450 to 1750." The encounter's liberation-and-slaughter judgment is the religious and political impact in miniature.)

### Florida (State Academic Standards, 2023 adoption; codes stable from NGSSS)

- **SS.912.W.4.7** — "Identify criticisms of the Roman Catholic Church by individuals such as Wycliffe, Hus and Erasmus, and the impact on later reformers." (The pre-Luther critics — the ground the theses grew in; verified against the official flrules.org standards document.)
- **SS.912.W.4.8** — "Summarize religious reforms associated with Luther, Calvin, Zwingli, Henry VIII, and John of Leyden and the effects of the Reformation on Europe." (Names Luther explicitly; verified against flrules.org.)
- **SS.912.W.4.9** — "Analyze the Roman Catholic Church's response to the Protestant Reformation in the forms of the Counter and Catholic Reformation." (The religious-conflict thread — verified against flrules.org.)

### California (History–Social Science Content Standards, Grade 7)

- **7.9.1** — List the causes for the internal turmoil in and weakening of the Catholic church (e.g., tax policies, selling of indulgences). (Tetzel's indulgence trade, the encounter's opening beat — verified against the CA Department of Education framework, Appendix C.)
- **7.9.2** — Describe the theological, political, and economic ideas of the major figures during the Reformation (e.g., Desiderius Erasmus, **Martin Luther**, John Calvin, William Tyndale). (Names Luther explicitly — verified against cde.ca.gov.)
- **7.9.7** — Describe the Golden Age of cooperation between Jews and Muslims in medieval Spain that promoted creativity in art, literature, and science, including how that cooperation was terminated by the religious persecution of individuals and groups (e.g., the Spanish Inquisition and the expulsion of Jews and Muslims from Spain in 1492). (Adjacent anchor for the encounter's persecution content — it covers religious persecution of Jews, not his treatise; cited honestly as context, not as a fit. Grade-band caveat: the entire CA Reformation anchor sits in Grade 7.)
