## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — say which.

- **The setting (the Black Cloister, Wittenberg, November 1545, speaking to a student).** Pure dramatic device. Luther never held such a conversation; the retrospective synthesis of thirty years into short answers is invented. The facts stated are not. (He was in fact seriously ill in late 1545 and did travel to Eisleben in January 1546, where he died; the November Wittenberg table is the author's.)
- **English speech.** The corpus is German and Latin. The figure's English wording follows the public-domain translations linked above; any phrasing beyond them is the author's, in the translators' register.
- **Condensation.** Each canonical answer compresses a pamphlet or a life-period into ~100 words. Traced to the texts above, not to single passages.
- **The closing question as a direct address.** Luther never posed it; the encounter's judgment frame is the library's design, not his.
- **The Worms exchange.** Uses the documented substance of his 18 April 1521 statement ("captive to the Word of God… God help me. Amen"). The famous German sentence is handled honestly in-voice: he says the clerks recorded only "God help me. Amen."

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Written before the figure ships, even the ones decided not to matter.

- **"Hier stehe ich, ich kann nicht anders."** Not in the Diet transcripts or eyewitness accounts; only "God help me. Amen" (in Latin, *Deus adjuvet me*) is contemporary. The German sentence appears in the first printed transcript; MacCulloch attributes its insertion to Georg Rörer, editor of Luther's collected works. The encounter states this honestly in Luther's own mouth rather than asserting the famous words.
- **The nailing of the theses.** Tradition; not documented fact. What is documented: on 31 October 1517 Luther sent the theses with a letter to Albrecht of Mainz, Archbishop of Magdeburg. The encounter says "sent," never "nailed."
- **Table Talk.** Students' notes of Luther's table conversation (Veit Dietrich, Johannes Mathesius, and others), compiled in the 1530s–1540s and partly published posthumously. The Socrates precedent applies: these are recorded words, but recorded by others, with all the distortion that implies. The encounter names this honestly in teacher instructions.
- **Death.** 18 February 1546, Eisleben, aged 62.
- **The Peasants' War dead.** Estimates run roughly 70,000 to 100,000, almost all peasants; ~100,000 is the commonly cited figure. It is an estimate, not a count. The figure himself keeps no tally — that refusal is in the encounter, not invented.
- **No retraction.** Luther never retracted the 1525 peasants pamphlet or the 1543 tract; he defended the positions in later writings and Table Talk. The canonical "what he would take back" answer states this directly.
- **Katharina.** Katharina von Bora fled the Cistercian convent at Nimbschen in 1523; married Luther 13 June 1525; six children (Hans, Elisabeth, Magdalena, Martin, Paul, Margarete — Elisabeth and Magdalena died young); she managed the Black Cloister household and brewery. His 1542 will made her his heir, against Saxon law — documented and unusual.
- **The seven measures.** Summarized from *On the Jews and Their Lies*, Section XI, in *Luther's Works* vol. 47, pp. 268–293 (Bertram translation): burn synagogues and schools; raze Jewish homes; confiscate religious writings; forbid rabbis to teach (on pain of life and limb); abolish safe-conduct; prohibit usury and confiscate gold and silver; force the young into manual labor — with expulsion ("drive them out like mad dogs") as the final step. The encounter lists them plainly, in his own framing.
- **Quoted material.** Total quoted Luther across the encounter files is brief (Thesis 1, the Freedom of a Christian two-sentence thesis, the documented Worms substance, "smite, slay and stab") — all within brief fair-use quotation, with translations noted.
- **The Nazi use (debrief, never in his mouth).** Documented: the Nazis displayed the 1543 treatise at Nuremberg rallies; the city of Nuremberg presented a first edition to Julius Streicher, editor of *Der Stürmer*; at his Nuremberg trial Streicher said Luther "would probably sit in my place in the defendant's dock." Scholarly views differ on how continuous the treatise's influence was in the centuries between (Johannes Wallmann argues it was largely ignored in the 18th–19th centuries; Hans Hillerbrand cautions against making Luther the whole of German antisemitism). The debrief states the Nazi facts and notes the disagreement honestly.
- **Lutheran denunciations.** Since the 1980s several Lutheran church bodies have formally denounced and dissociated from the anti-Jewish writings; the Evangelical Lutheran Church in Bavaria issued its statement in November 1998, on the sixtieth anniversary of Kristallnacht.
