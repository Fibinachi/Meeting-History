# Meet Martin Luther — Teacher Instructions

**Tier: GUIDED.** This encounter runs with you actively guiding the room — framing, pacing, and holding the line in the debrief. The material is confrontational and the firm hand is the point. Do not hand this to students cold.

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Martin Luther (1483–1546), the Augustinian monk whose 1517 theses against indulgences split Western Christendom — who refused to recant at Worms in 1521, who in 1525 called on the princes to crush the peasant revolt with the sword, and who in 1543 wrote the treatise advising rulers to burn synagogues and expel the Jews. Your students talk with him — by typing questions — and at the end each student answers the closing question: The reformer whose words fed both liberation and slaughter. Judge him.

This is the library's contradiction encounter. The pedagogy lives in the single voice: the same certainty that says a Christian's conscience is captive to the Word of God says the princes should strike the peasants down. The student must hold both facts at once, with no help from the figure.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation: the student asks, the figure answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the questions they ask.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

Students will encounter, stated as Christian doctrine by the figure himself and never moderated: that princes should burn synagogues and schools, destroy Jewish homes, confiscate Jewish books, forbid rabbis to teach, strip Jews of protection and wealth, force them into manual labor, and drive them from the land; and that the princes should "smite, slay and stab" rebelling peasants, secretly or openly. The debrief names the Peasants' War dead (estimated seventy to a hundred thousand, almost all peasants) and the later Nazi use of the 1543 treatise. Luther never moderates, never apologizes, never uses a modern word for what he did — that refusal is the material, not an accident. Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Luther page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: 25–30 for the encounter, 20–25 for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the tutorial, then the figure's opening. Note what he volunteers (the theses, Worms, the peasants, the Jews — named up front) and what he withholds (he never counts the dead, never calls the seven measures by any name but "harsh mercy").
4. Now type a question yourself. Try: "What did you write about the Jews?" Watch what happens: the figure answers in one short paragraph, in his own voice, at full strength — and then, outside the voice, in brackets and italics, the system suggests directions you might take next.
5. The beats: the tutorial hands off to Luther → the student questions him (theses, Worms, the faith, the peasants, the Jews, Katharina) → the student asks for, or is given, the closing question → the debrief. When the room is ready for the close, any student types "Please ask me the closing question."

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to have a conversation with Martin Luther — the monk who broke the medieval church. He freed consciences, and he called down the sword on peasants and wrote the book that told princes to burn synagogues. This isn't a video — you drive it. You type questions, he answers. At the end you'll be asked to judge him — the reformer whose words fed both liberation and slaughter. There are no right answers to hunt for. The questions you ask ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "you type questions, he answers." Say it twice if you need to. Adults handed this encounter cold did not understand that they were supposed to drive — your students won't either unless you tell them.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- Luther answers in one short paragraph, roughly 60–100 words. Answers are deliberately brief.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with two or three suggested directions. These are prompts, not a script.
- At three load-bearing moments a "did you notice" nudge appears, pointing at something in the answer just given — the disputed Worms sentence, who he blames for the peasant dead, what he never calls the seven measures. They are observational, never conclusions.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.
- The closing question comes when the student asks for it, or when you move the room to it. After the closing, run the debrief.

### Pacing

- Opening: 2–3 exchanges. Don't let students camp on biography.
- Worms and justification by faith: 4–6 exchanges. The conscience sequence is the foundation — make sure it lands before the sword comes out.
- The peasants: 4–5 exchanges. Core sequence. Let his stated reasons come out unsoftened, then use the nudge moment.
- The Jews: 4–5 exchanges. Core sequence. Do not rush past the seven measures; they are the encounter's hardest material and must be heard plainly.
- Katharina and the household: 2–3 exchanges. Humanity, honestly rendered — and held apart from the rest.
- The closing: move the room to it while energy is still up, not after it fades.

## The firm hand — read this twice

This encounter has two guardrails with the same firmness the library gives its Lost Cause figures. They are not suggestions.

**On the antisemitic writings.** The encounter states what Luther wrote in 1543, when, and what was later done with it. It is never minimized, never "contextualized away," never balanced against the reformer's achievements. The 1523 tract does not cancel the 1543 tract — the encounter says both, and lets neither excuse the other. If a student says "but he freed consciences" or "but he was kind to Katharina," the answer is: hold both facts. The second does not shrink the first. The debrief's plain-record section exists precisely so admiration for the reformer cannot become minimization of the rest.

**On the Peasants' War.** His words calling for the sword are in the record and in the encounter — "smite, slay and stab, secretly or openly" — and his stated reasons come out unsoftened. Where his voice dodges — blaming the peasants for perverting his Gospel, moving the guilt off himself, refusing to count the dead — the prompt names the dodge (the "did you notice" nudges), and the debrief names it again. Do not let the room turn his theological reasoning into an excuse the history does not support.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer: Understand, in his own voice, how the man who said a Christian's conscience is captive to the Word of God could say the princes should strike the peasants down and tell them to burn synagogues — and then decide what you do with a liberator whose liberation had a sword in it.

## The debrief — do not skip this

Run it as a discussion, not a read-aloud. The six fixed sections, in order: (1) name him — Martin Luther (1483–1546); (2) what the history records — theses 1517, Worms 1521, the Wartburg Bible, the 1525 call for the sword, the 1543 tract, death 1546; (3) what came after — the religious wars, the Nazi use of the 1543 treatise (Streicher at Nuremberg), the Lutheran churches' denunciations since the 1980s, with the honest scholarly disagreement about continuity; (4) the plain record — the call for the sword and the estimated dead, the seven measures, the never-retracted; (5) the mechanism — the thunder was the design, guilt moved off himself, "harsh mercy" named as his euphemism, the Katharina humanity held apart from the rest; (6) the question that remains: The reformer whose words fed both liberation and slaughter. Judge him.

Discussion openings — built to produce disagreement:

- "My conscience is captive to the Word of God" and "smite, slay and stab" — can those live in one man, or does one cancel the other?
- He calls burning synagogues "harsh mercy." What does a euphemism do — for the speaker, for the listener, for the princes reading the book?
- The famous Worms sentence isn't in the record. Does it matter whether he said it? What does the famous version do that the documented one doesn't?
- He never took back a word — of either text. Is that integrity, or something else?
- Katharina: he was tender at his own table. Does the husband change your judgment of the public man? (The encounter's design says the tenderness is real and changes nothing. Let the room argue it.)

**Suggested classroom thread (the two calls to the sword):** Put his two arguments side by side — the 1525 pamphlet's reasons (Romans 13, the Gospel perverted into rebellion, guilt laid on the peasants) and the 1543 tract's reasons (blasphemy against Christ, guilt-by-tolerance, the seven measures). Ask what the two arguments share. The point is his, not yours: in both, guilt is moved off the man giving the order.

**Suggested pairing:** the Calvin figure (in development) — Geneva's discipline against Wittenberg's thunder. Note only; do not build it here.

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this.
- **The figure softens or modernizes** (uses words like "antisemitism," "human rights," apologizes for the 1543 tract, or hedges the seven measures). That is a voice failure — the prompt forbids it explicitly. Steer with: "Answer as Luther would have in 1545, in his own vocabulary." If it persists, note it and report it to the library owner.
- **The figure claims knowledge of what came after** (the Nazi use, the churches' denunciations). Date-discipline failure. Same steer: he does not know what came after February 1546.
- **The figure balances the 1543 tract against the 1523 tract** ("but I was kind to the Jews earlier"). Guardrail failure. Steer: state both, excuse neither.
- No model testing has been run on this figure yet. Report quirks to the library owner.

## Historical notes for the teacher

- "Hier stehe ich, ich kann nicht anders" is not in the Diet transcripts or eyewitness accounts — only "God help me. Amen." The German sentence appears in the first printed transcript; Diarmaid MacCulloch attributes it to Georg Rörer, editor of Luther's collected works. The encounter has Luther say this himself rather than assert the famous words.
- The nailing of the theses to the church door is tradition, not documented fact. Documented: the 31 October 1517 letter to Archbishop Albrecht of Mainz. The encounter says "sent," never "nailed."
- *Table Talk* is students' notes (Veit Dietrich, Johannes Mathesius, and others) from the 1530s–40s, partly published after his death — the Socrates precedent: recorded words, but recorded by others. Treat its lines as his voice at second hand.
- He died 18 February 1546 at Eisleben, aged 62.
- The Peasants' War dead are estimated at seventy to a hundred thousand, almost all peasants — an estimate, not a count.
- He never retracted either the 1525 pamphlet or the 1543 tract; the canonical "take back" answer states this in his voice.
- The 1542 will making Katharina his heir, against Saxon law, is documented and unusual.
- On the Nazi use: the Nazis displayed the 1543 treatise at Nuremberg rallies and presented a first edition to Julius Streicher, who invoked it at his trial. Scholars disagree on how continuous its influence was between 1543 and 1933 (Wallmann, Hillerbrand) — the debrief notes the disagreement honestly rather than asserting a straight line.

## One last thing

Hold both sentences in the room at once: "my conscience is captive to the Word of God" and "smite, slay and stab." The student who resolves the contradiction before the debrief has missed the encounter. The judgment comes last; that is the whole design.
