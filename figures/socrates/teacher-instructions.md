# Meet Socrates — Teacher Instructions

## What this is

A supervised classroom encounter with Socrates (c. 470–399 BC), run through a conversational AI (Claude or ChatGPT). This one is different from every other encounter in the library: **the form is inverted.** Your students do not question him — he questions them. It is the platform's introduction. By being interrogated, students learn what questioning does, before they take up the questioning themselves in later encounters.

Each student talks with him — by typing answers — and at the end each answers for themselves: **is the unexamined life worth living?** Then the questioning passes to them: in the rest of the library, they do the asking.

## What this is not

It is not a lecture, a video, or a quiz. There is nothing to click through and no right answers to find. The entire activity is a conversation — but a backwards one: he asks, the student answers. If a student sits waiting for something to happen, they have misunderstood the format — the thing that happens is the answers they give, and what he does to them.

It is not a debate with the AI. The figure will not break character to discuss modern views. That restraint is the design.

## Content warning — read this before you decide to use it

Students will sit with a man in a prison cell on the night before his execution by hemlock. The death itself happens offstage — the encounter ends before the cup comes — but its certainty is present throughout. He is calm about it. That calm is the point, and it may unsettle some students more than distress would.

Separately: students may find being questioned uncomfortable at first — exposed, even. That discomfort is the pedagogy, not a flaw in it. Name it in your framing ("he will test your answers; that is the assignment"), and give the room permission to say "I don't know" — he has a reply for that, and it is a kind one.

Do not run this encounter without planning to run the debrief.

## What you need

- A Claude or ChatGPT account. Either works. (Note any model-specific quirks found in testing under "If the model misbehaves.")
- The encounter prompt, from the Socrates page. Copy the entire prompt text.
- One class period, roughly 45–60 minutes: ~25–30 for the encounter, ~20–25 for the debrief. It also works as a homework assignment followed by an in-class debrief, but the first run should be supervised.
- A way to display the conversation if you run it as a class (projector or shared screen), or one device per student/group if they run it themselves.

## Supervision

This encounter runs only in a supervised educational setting: you present, a classroom, a structured debrief to follow. If you are a teacher testing or preparing the encounter on your own, that satisfies the requirement — you are the supervision. Do not assign it as unsupervised homework on the first run.

## Setup — do this yourself first (15 minutes)

Do not walk into class cold. Run the encounter once yourself, alone, before your students ever see it. This is the single most important instruction on this page.

1. Open Claude or ChatGPT in a new conversation.
2. Paste the entire encounter prompt and send it.
3. Read the tutorial, then his opening. He makes you a bargain — he asks, you answer — and his first question is: what is one thing you claim to know?
4. Now type an answer yourself. Try something ordinary, for example: "I know my own name." Watch what happens: he takes your answer apart gently, in short exchanges, and then — outside the voice, in brackets and italics — the system suggests directions you might take next. Note that the suggestions model possible *answers and positions*, since you are the one answering.
5. Try asking him something between answers — "Who are you?" or "Why are you in a cell?" He answers in one short paragraph, then returns at once to questioning you. That is the loop: the student answers, occasionally asks, and he always comes back to the examination.
6. When you have had enough, let him close: he hands you the questioning ("from here, you do the questioning") and asks the closing question. Then run the debrief on yourself before you run it on anyone else.

## Running the room

### Frame it before anyone touches a keyboard (2 minutes)

Say something close to this, in your own words:

> You're going to meet Socrates — the Athenian who was executed for asking questions. This one works backwards: he asks, you answer. That's the whole of it. There are no wrong answers, but he will test the ones you give. At the end he'll ask you one question: is the unexamined life worth living? The answers you give ARE the assignment.

The sentence that matters most is "he asks, you answer." Say it twice if you need to. Students handed this encounter cold will try to interview him — the inversion has to be explicit.

### The mechanics, as your students will experience them

- He asks short questions, one at a time. Answers are deliberately brief — the student drives by answering, not by asking.
- When a student does ask him something, he answers in one short paragraph (roughly 60–100 words) and then asks his next question.
- After every answer, a bracketed italic line appears with suggested positions the student might take or questions they might slip in. These are prompts, not a script.
- If a student stalls ("I don't know what to say"), he does not lecture them about answering — he hands them an easier question and keeps moving.
- If a student addresses the frame itself ("Are you Claude?"), the encounter steps out of character, answers plainly, and offers to continue or move to the debrief. This is intentional.

### Pacing

Opening: his bargain and first question — 2–3 exchanges. Don't let students camp on biography; his job is to return to questioning *them*. Middle: 8–12 exchanges of examination is a full encounter; watch for the moment a student's answer breaks under a calm question — that is the whole pedagogy, and it usually arrives faster than you expect. Closing: when the exchanges thin or you call time, let him give the handoff and the closing question. Do not rush the closing; it is the sentence the debrief is built on.

## The goal, stated plainly

Students will ask "what's the goal?" — this is the answer: to feel, from the inside, what it is like to have your own answer taken apart by a calm question — and to leave carrying one question you answer for yourself: is the unexamined life worth living?

## The debrief — do not skip this

The six fixed sections, in order: (1) name him — Socrates, c. 470–399 BC, who wrote nothing; (2) what the history records — the 399 BC trial, the narrow guilty verdict, the hemlock, the refused escape; (3) what came after — Plato's dialogues, the Socratic problem, 2,400 years of argument he never knew; (4) the plain record — a democratic jury voted to kill a man for asking questions, and the charges carried a political subtext about his associates; (5) the mechanism — the inverted form, the elenchus felt from the inside, certainty at rest, the platform handoff; (6) the question that remains — is the unexamined life worth living?

Discussion openings (built to produce disagreement):

- He asked you what you claim to know. What did you say — and what happened to it?
- He says doing wrong is worse than suffering it, and claims to know nothing else. Is that a contradiction?
- He refused to escape when he could have lived. Was that courage, stubbornness, or something else?
- A democratic jury voted to kill him for asking questions. What does that say about Athens — and about questions?
- Is the unexamined life worth living? Answer for yourself, not for him.

## If the model misbehaves

- **The conversation stalls.** Point at the bracketed suggestions. They exist for exactly this — and for this figure they model answers, not questions.
- **The model lectures instead of questioning.** This is the most likely failure mode for this figure: the model wants to *explain* the elenchus instead of *being* Socrates. If it starts lecturing, stop the conversation and re-paste the prompt; do not try to repair it mid-stream.
- **The model claims vindication.** If he ever says anything like "history will remember me" or "Plato will write this down," that is a date-discipline break. Stop, re-paste, continue.

## Historical notes for the teacher

The honest answers to "did it really happen like that?" — one bullet per caveat from sources.md:

- We have no writing of Socrates. Everything is secondhand, mainly Plato (who knew him and was at the trial) and second Xenophon — a plainer, more conventional portrait. Scholars call this the Socratic problem; the encounter leans on Plato and says so.
- "I know that I know nothing" is a later slogan, verbatim nowhere in Plato. His actual claim (*Apology* 21d) is narrower — "what I do not know I do not think I know either" — and at 29b-c he claims positive moral knowledge. The prompt uses his words, not the slogan.
- Plato was present at the trial but ill and absent on the final day (*Phaedo* 59b); the serene death scene is Phaedo's report, not eyewitness.
- The jury was 501; the vote was close (thirty votes the other way would have acquitted, *Apology* 36a). His birth year, c. 470 BC, is scholarly convention. The exact execution date is unknown — spring 399 BC, after the sacred ship's return from Delos.
- The charges carried a political subtext (his associates Critias and Alcibiades were enemies of the restored democracy). Scholarly consensus, not stated in the dialogues; the debrief labels it as such.
- The closing handoff ("from here, you do the questioning") is the platform's device, not history's. So is the compression of trial, prison, and final day into one cell scene, and so is his questioning a modern student at all. All three are listed as authored devices.
- No text shows Socrates encountering Democritus's atomism by name. When a student asks about atoms, his honest unfamiliarity — asking what the words mean, then redirecting to the examined question — is the historically grounded move.

## One last thing

Teach them that the question is the answer. The single skill this encounter builds is staying with a question until your first answer breaks — and then staying with the next one. Tell your students: don't try to win. There is nothing to win. That is the whole lesson.
