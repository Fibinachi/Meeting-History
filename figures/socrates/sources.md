## Authored devices

Dramatic devices the encounter uses that the history does not document. Plausible is not documented — said plainly here.

- **The library handoff** ("From here, my friend, you will do the questioning") — pure dramatic device. The historical Socrates knew nothing of any library and died unsure whether anyone would remember him.
- **The inverted interrogation** — Socrates questions the student rather than the student questioning him. A pedagogical extension of the elenchus, which is historically his method; but no ancient text shows him examining a modern student.
- **Compressed final-night cell scene** — the dialogues span roughly a month (trial, the month of imprisonment, the last day). The encounter folds trial material (*Apology*), the prison refusal (*Crito*), and the final day (*Phaedo*) into one last-day cell scene.
- **His unfamiliarity with atomism by name** — no text in the corpus shows Socrates encountering Democritus or the word "atomism." His responding to the physics question by asking what the student means is period-plausible, not documented.

## Historical caveats

Things a teacher may be asked about, with the honest answer. Written before the figure ships, even the ones that don't matter.

- **The Socratic problem.** We have no writing of Socrates. Everything is secondhand: mainly Plato (who knew him roughly eight years and was about twenty-eight at the trial), second Xenophon. Scholars have argued for 2,400 years about how much of "Socrates" is the man and how much is Plato's mouthpiece. The encounter leans on Plato; Xenophon is the named second witness.
- **"I know that I know nothing" is not verbatim Plato.** The slogan appears nowhere in Plato in that form; it is a later compression of *Apology* 21d ("what I do not know I do not think I know either"). Worse for the slogan: at *Apology* 29b-c Socrates claims positive moral knowledge, twice. The canonical answers use his actual words, not the slogan.
- **Plato at the trial, absent at the death.** Plato names himself as present at the trial (*Apology* 34a) but notes he was ill and absent on the final day (*Phaedo* 59b). The serene death scene is Phaedo's report, not Plato's eyewitness account.
- **Birth year c. 470 BC is scholarly convention** (some give 469). No source states it; the figure never offers it.
- **Exact execution date unknown.** Spring 399 BC, the day after the sacred ship returned from Delos (*Phaedo* 58a-b). The encounter's "the cup will come soon" is all the precision the history allows.
- **The jury was 501.** The vote was close enough that, by Socrates's own reckoning, thirty votes the other way would have acquitted him (*Apology* 36a). The 501 figure is the scholarly convention for the panel size.
- **The charges carried a political subtext.** Several men Socrates had talked with — Critias, Alcibiades — were enemies of the democracy restored in 403 BC, four years before the trial. This is the scholarly consensus view; Plato does not state it in the dialogues. The debrief states it as consensus, not as Socrates's own claim.
- **Aristophanes' *Clouds* (423 BC) is the third ancient witness** — and it shows a younger Socrates as a natural philosopher studying "insects to stars," precisely what *Apology* 19b-d denies. It is comedy, not used for voice, but it is evidence that his turn away from natural philosophy was real and noticed.
- **Democritus (c. 460–c. 370 BC) overlapped Socrates's life; Plato never mentions him.** We cannot establish whether Socrates ever heard of atomism. The figure's honest unfamiliarity with the name is our marked gap, not an assertion about history.
- **He was seventy at the trial** (*Apology* 17d), married to Xanthippe, with three sons — one nearly grown, two children (*Apology* 34d).
- **He served as a hoplite** at Potidaea, Delium, and Amphipolis (*Apology* 28e).
- **DEAD RULE satisfied:** Socrates died 399 BC in Athens, by hemlock, after conviction.
