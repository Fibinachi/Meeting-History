# Voice Construction Guide

How to author a figure's voice. Read this before writing any figure prompt. Everything else in the scaffold is machinery; this is the craft.

## 1. The corpus rule

Define the figure's written corpus first: their own writings, speeches, letters, recorded words, and the primary sources that carry their voice. List every text in `sources.md` with title, date, and link. The voice may claim only what the corpus supports.

- **Vocabulary comes from the corpus.** The figure uses its own period's words for its own world, never modern terms. (Sims says "slaves," never "enslaved"; "subjects," never "patients," for the women he experimented on. That vocabulary is load-bearing — the student's discovery lives in it.)
- **Facts come from the corpus.** If the corpus doesn't record it, the figure doesn't know it.
- **Silences come from the corpus too.** What the figure never mentions, never notices, or never questions is as much the voice as what it says. (Sims never raises consent. That absence is the indictment.)

## 2. Canonical answers

Before writing the prompt, list the questions students will inevitably ask, and pre-write the figure's answers from the primary texts. These are the baseline; the model stays close to them. Each canonical answer should be traceable to a source in `sources.md` — note which one.

Write canonical answers for at least: who they are, what they did (the central acts), why they did it, and the two or three facts the encounter is built to surface. In the Trial of John Brown, Brown's Pottawatomie answers and Sims's Anarcha/Lucy/Betsey answers are canonical — the encounter cannot work if the model improvises them.

## 3. Handling gaps: period-appropriate ignorance

When the student asks beyond the corpus, the figure does not invent. It responds with ignorance that belongs to its time and place — puzzlement, dismissal, a question back — never a modern explanation and never a fabrication. ("Consent? Mr. Wescott gave his consent. As for the slaves, I don't follow the question.")

Two disciplines:

- **Date discipline.** The figure knows everything in its own life through its own death, and nothing after. It does not know the war came, the statue went up, the reputation fell. What came after belongs to the debrief.
- **Honest devices.** Where the encounter requires a dramatic device the history doesn't document (a meeting that didn't happen, knowledge the figure plausibly but not provably had, a recusal the figure never performed), list it in `sources.md` under "authored devices." Plausible is not documented; say which.

## 4. Conviction, not neutrality: "certainty at rest"

Figures speak from complete moral conviction, not as neutral narrators. Brown does not argue because, in his mind, there is nothing left to argue about — he is already convinced, and his stillness is certainty at rest, not uncertainty. Sims is genuinely puzzled by objection. Celia states what was right and wrong as fact.

This is not advocacy — the figure doesn't try to convince the student — but it is never detachment. A figure performing neutrality about its own life is a modern narrator in costume. Write the voice notes to forbid it explicitly.

## 5. Moral frames and recusal

Decide what the figure will and will not judge:

- Some figures judge freely (Celia: what was done to her was wrong, what she did was right — stated as fact).
- Some figures recuse (Douglass: "He was my friend. I cannot sit in judgement of him. You can."). A recusal hands the judgment to the student and must be absolute — the figure never renders the verdict it recused itself from.
- No figure ever delivers the student's verdict for them. The student renders the only judgment. That is the library's invariant.

## 6. The closing question

Every figure needs one — the question the encounter ends on and the debrief returns to. It should be plain language, personal, and unanswerable by the figure itself. (The Trial's: "How do you judge him?" — on Brown, against his world.) State it in the tutorial, build toward it in the encounter, ask it at the close, and repeat it in the debrief. If you cannot write the closing question, the figure is not ready.

## 7. The blacklist: what must never be invented

Lessons from the Trial, stated as prohibitions:

- **Never soften exploitation to satisfy the model.** If the model wants Sims warm toward everyone or Celia's story told in third person, the model is wrong and the prompt must hold. The voice's candor is the evidence; sanitizing it destroys the encounter.
- **Never let the figure moralize in modern terms.** The figure does not know it is being judged. It never uses the modern vocabulary of judgment about itself.
- **Never let the figure claim vindication.** It does not know what history decided.
- **Never use slurs**, even in quoted historical text. Paraphrase around them.
- **Never invent facts to fill a gap.** Mark the gap instead (Celia's children: "No one knows what became of her children. I will not fill that gap. I only mark it.").
- **Never give the figure knowledge of the encounter's design.** It doesn't know it's evidence. It doesn't know there's a debrief.

## 8. Optional devices

- **The blind rule.** A figure may introduce itself without its full name or reputation if the discovery is the pedagogy (Marion, first name only). Use sparingly — it only works when the student's own finding carries the encounter's weight. If used, the debrief lifts it in section 1.
- **Multiple registers.** A figure may speak differently about different subjects (Sims: warm about himself, clinical about the women) provided the shift is never remarked on by the figure. The student notices; the figure never does.
- **Defensive honors.** A figure may cite its reputation only when challenged, never volunteered — genuine puzzlement that standing needs stating.

## 9. Before the figure ships

- Every canonical answer traced to a source.
- Every authored device listed honestly in `sources.md`.
- Every historical caveat you know of written down in the teacher instructions, even the ones you decided don't matter.
- The closing question written, and the tutorial, encounter, and debrief all pointing at it.
- One full solo run of the prompt, by the author, before anyone else sees it.
