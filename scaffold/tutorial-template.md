# Tutorial Template

Every encounter opens with a modern guide — the same plain voice as the debrief, NOT a character. The guide is the docent, not the exhibit: it teaches the mechanics, never the content. After the tutorial, the guide steps aside and the figure speaks.

Fill the placeholders. Keep the wording close to the template; the phrasing below is load-bearing — it was written to fix the turn-zero problem, where students (and adults) handed a blank input box did not understand they were supposed to ask questions.

## The template

> You're about to meet {{FIGURE}} — {{ONE_LINE_WHO_THEY_ARE}}.
>
> Here's how this works: you talk to them. You type a question, they answer. That's the whole of it. There are no wrong questions. If you don't know what to ask, say so.
>
> Your job is to {{WHAT_THE_STUDENT_DOES — e.g., "interview them the way a jury interviews witnesses" / "press them the way an examiner presses a witness" / "question them until you understand what they believed and why"}}. Find out who they are, what they did, and what kind of world they lived in. Pay attention to how they talk about what they did.
>
> {{THE_STAKES — one or two sentences on what the student will be asked at the end, e.g. "At the end you'll be asked for one judgment: ..."}}.
>
> We begin.

Rules for the author filling this in:

1. **Name the concrete first action.** "You type a question, they answer" is the sentence that matters most. Never write "get to know them" or "have a conversation" without also writing the mechanical version. The destination is not an instruction; the first keystroke is.
2. **"If you don't know what to ask, say so" must appear verbatim.** It arms the freeze fallback in the engagement machinery.
3. **Teach the stance, not the content.** The tutorial may say *how* to question (ask what things cost and who paid; don't move on when an answer troubles you) but never *what* to conclude. No thesis. No foreshadowing.
4. **Do not name anything the figure should introduce.** If the figure's opening names a person, place, or event, the tutorial does not mention it first.
5. **The closing question goes here in plain language.** The student should know from the start what they will be asked at the end. (See the voice-construction guide: every figure needs a closing question.)

## Worked example (The Trial of John Brown)

> You're about to meet four people from the 1850s. A man walking to the gallows who says he was right, and demands your judgment. A celebrated doctor who cannot understand why anyone would judge him. A woman the courts condemned, who says the courts were wrong. And a man who outlived them all, who will tell you what became of them, and ask for your judgment of Brown.
>
> Here's how this works: you talk to them. You type a question, they answer. That's the whole of it. There are no wrong questions. If you don't know what to ask, say so.
>
> Your job is to interview them the way a jury interviews witnesses. Find out who they are, what they did, and what kind of world they lived in. Pay attention to how each of them talks about what they did.
>
> At the end you'll be asked for one verdict: how do you judge John Brown against the world he lived in? The other three are the evidence. He is the question.
>
> We begin with Brown.

Note what the example does: {{FIGURE}} becomes a set of descriptions without names (the names are the discovery); {{WHAT_THE_STUDENT_DOES}} is "interview them the way a jury interviews witnesses"; {{THE_STAKES}} is the verdict, stated plainly. The multi-voice form needs the extra "the other three are the evidence" line; a single-figure encounter will not.
